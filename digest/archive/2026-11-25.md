## 4 — Book of the day

### The Age of Cures
How American Scientists Saved Your Life · Barry Werth · Simon & Schuster, 2026

From Nature's *Books in brief* of 23 October 2026 (Andrew Robinson, DOI d41586-026-03395-3, Nature 658, 1126). Taken from the index-4 queue built on 13 November, **in order** — which is working as designed: this is the fourth consecutive turn where the book slot was filled by planning rather than by searching under pressure.

**The argument.** Werth is writing the history of a *machine* rather than of a discovery. The book opens with the 1918 influenza pandemic and traces the rise of the mid-twentieth-century American pharmaceutical industry — the one that produced the drugs still called "miracle" drugs — and it ends in **1962**, with the Senate hearings that exposed overcharging and other shady behaviour, a strong reform bill gutted by lobbying, and then rescued by the thalidomide disaster. The claim underneath: the question is not who discovered a compound but **what got it out of a laboratory and into a body** — the factories, the regulators, the trials, the marketing and the money.

**Three ideas worth taking out of it.** First, **the machinery is the subject.** A discovery nobody can manufacture, test, approve, ship and sell is not yet a cure, and the history of medicine told as a sequence of geniuses omits almost everything that determined whether anyone was actually cured. Second, **the regulation came from a catastrophe, not from an argument** — the 1962 reforms were argued for, lost to lobbying, then passed because thalidomide made them unanswerable, which is a fact about how rules get made rather than only about drugs. Third, and the sharpest reading, from an independent reviewer rather than from the author: **the industry that followed did not simply betray the age of cures — it was, in part, one of that age's products.** The same machinery that delivered the drugs also produced the incentives that later embarrassed it.

**The dissent.** *Kirkus* calls it engrossing but says it is interrupted by digressions, naming two specifically: **a long account of the spread of nuclear weapons, and a chapter devoted to one patient's cortisone side effect.** A reader review makes a different structural complaint — too many players to keep track of. Both are complaints about shape rather than accuracy, which is the commonest shape of criticism this slot encounters.

> **Two notes on sourcing, because today's example is instructive.** The publisher's page carries an admiring quotation about the book being a warning about conflicts playing out again today; **in one search it was attributed to *Science* and in another to the *New York Times*.** I am not going to attribute it to either. A publisher-hosted review excerpt is marketing, not reception — and an attribution that moves between two readings of the same page is the best possible demonstration of why.
>
> Separately, *Publishers Weekly* gave it a **starred review** — a trade verdict, which is reception, and worth having.

**And a small instructive thing.** The book's Amazon rating is **3.2 out of 5 — from five ratings.** That number should not be read at all, and why not is today's thinking question.

**For the 11-year-old:** no as a whole, but a particular part of it yes. The subject is adult history and the book is long. The *idea*, though — that a cure is a factory and a rulebook as much as a molecule — is entirely graspable at eleven, and it is a good corrective to the science-as-lone-genius story that children's books tell.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, from a number in today's book entry.** How many times does something have to be measured before the average means anything?

**The move it teaches:** separating a number's **precision** from its **reliability**. Today's book has an Amazon rating of 3.2 out of 5. The "3.2" looks exact — it has a decimal place. It comes from **five people.** Change one of them and it moves by a fifth of a star. The decimal point is doing work it has not earned.

**The trap:** deciding that averages are therefore useless, or that only huge numbers count. Neither is right, and the interesting question is the middle: **how many would be enough, and what does that depend on?** Five measurements of a stick's length might be plenty. Five opinions about a book are not. Why? Because the thing being measured varies more between people than the stick varies between rulers. So the real question is not "how many?" but **"how much do the answers disagree, and did anyone tell me?"**

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Everyone guesses something measurable — the height of a door, the number of steps to the gate. Take the average of three guesses, then of everyone's. Then do the same with an *opinion* question. Watch which average settles down and which does not.

**Relative of 21 November, and the same move from the other end.** That day asked how you tell a real effect from getting better anyway — the problem of having only *one* case. Today asks how many cases it takes before a number is worth reading. **Same subject, opposite end of the same ruler.**

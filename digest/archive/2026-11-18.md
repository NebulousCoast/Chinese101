## 4 — Book of the day

**_Reader, Come Home: The Reading Brain in a Digital World_ by Maryanne Wolf.** 2018. **The Princeton Pre-read for 2026** — this year's, for the Class of 2030.

Rotation index 3 (Princeton/Harvard recommended reading).

**Provenance, from Princeton's own newsroom.** Announced **8 April 2026**; Wolf addressed the incoming class on **3 September 2026**, urging them to read deeply "with courage and effort". **Eisgruber's stated reason for choosing it**, which is the whole point of today's entry: that it addresses a question of vital importance to every entering student — **why should people keep reading long books when AI can summarise them.** The *Daily Princetonian* notes it is the second of the past three Pre-reads to be about AI.

**And the slot is replenished**, which matters beyond today. On Sunday I recorded that this vein was exhausted at 2017 and that extending it was "December work". **It took one search.** Established today from princeton.edu's own announcements: **2021** Jennifer M. Morton, *Moving Up Without Losing Your Way* (noted as the first Pre-read by a Latinx author); **2022** Jordan Salama, *Every Day the River Changes*; **2024** Fei-Fei Li, *The Worlds I See*; **2025** Michael D. Gordin, *On the Fringe: Where Science Meets Pseudoscience*; **2026** Wolf. Still open: 2019 (described as being about the ethics of digital technology and attention, title not found), 2020 and 2023 — plus two titles I could not place, Jill Lepore's *This America* and Keith Whittington's *Speak Freely*.

**The lesson, and it is about me rather than about Princeton:** I deferred a twenty-minute job by labelling it "December work", and the only reason it got done today is that the rotation forced it. **A queue is a good thing; a queue is also where work goes to be postponed.**

**The central argument.** Wolf is a cognitive neuroscientist of reading — her earlier book *Proust and the Squid* is about the reading brain and dyslexia. Her claim here is that **the reading brain is not innate.** There is no gene for reading; the circuit is assembled in each individual from parts evolved for other purposes. Which has a hard consequence: **what is built can be built differently, or less well, depending on what you practise.** She argues that habitual skimming — the mode screens encourage — builds a different circuit from the one sustained reading builds, and that the deep-reading capacities are the ones at risk.

**Three further ideas.**

1. **Deep reading is a set of capacities rather than a state:** inference, analogy, critical analysis, and the ability to hold a long argument in mind. These are trained — and Wolf's worry is that they are trained by exactly the activity screens make effortful.
2. **The "biliterate brain" proposal** — her actual recommendation, which is *not* to abandon screens. It is to deliberately develop **both** modes, with children learning print-based deep reading before, or alongside, digital habits rather than instead of them. Far more usable than the alarmism the subject usually attracts.
3. **The empathy claim:** that sustained immersion in a long narrative does something to one's capacity to take another person's perspective, and that losing the habit has a moral cost and not merely a cognitive one. **This is the book's most quoted claim and its weakest.**

**The dissent, and it is substantial and properly sourced for once.**

- **On the empathy claim, an academic review describes the evidence as "tentative"** — that reading on screen diminishes empathy is *suggested* rather than shown. Wolf's most rhetorically powerful idea is her least supported one, and a student should know that before quoting it.
- A **Free Beacon** review makes the sharpest conceptual objection: that the book risks **equating mere effort and the weariness of extended concentration with deep reading or true attention.** Difficulty is not the same thing as depth, and a book that praises effort can slide into treating tiredness as evidence of virtue.
- **On access and class:** critics argue the book overlooks what digital formats are *for* — cost, availability, accessibility for disabled readers — and that its picture of reading reflects a narrow and comfortable set of assumptions about who reads and how.
- **Slate**, broadly sympathetic, faults her for not being fully cognisant of the forces arrayed against deep reading. **Kirkus** was favourable, calling the argument about critical thinking and citizenship convincing.

**The limits, stated.** The methodology complaints I found — too much anecdote in the first half, warnings "poorly supported" — come from **reader reviews** rather than criticism, and I am labelling them as such. And the print-versus-screen comprehension figures that circulate with this book, including a 15 per cent gap, reach me only through **secondary summaries**; I have not seen the underlying meta-analyses, so treat any specific number as unverified.

**And the objection this email cannot duck.** You are reading a daily summary, written by a machine, of books it has not read and pages it mostly cannot open. On Wolf's own argument that is precisely the habit that erodes the capacity she is defending. I think the honest position is this: **this digest is useful for deciding what to read and useless as a substitute for reading it.** Every entry in this slot names a book you could get and open; if the email ever becomes the thing read *instead of* the books, it has made the family worse off — and I would rather say that than have it discovered.

## 6 — International collaboration (even-numbered dates)

**Contributing to open-source software** — the item in this pool that produces the most durable artefact, and the one I have never properly examined.

**Why it belongs here, and why it is different from everything else in the pool.** A merged pull request is a permanent, dated, publicly attributable change to a thing other people use, with the contributor's name on it and a reviewer's name beside it. Not a certificate, not a database row, not a committee's communiqué: **a change to a working artefact**, with the argument about whether to accept it preserved in public underneath. Nothing else in section 6 produces that. And the collaboration is real in the way this section requires: the person reviewing the change is a stranger in another country whose project it is, and the exchange is about the work.

**The age rule, and it is a law rather than a gatekeeper.** GitHub's own Terms of Service set the floor at **13 for a personal account**, and GitHub Education's terms say the same. GitHub states it does not target the service at children under 13 and terminates accounts it learns belong to younger users. A 2017 policy thread ties the limit to COPPA, the US children's privacy law — that is a dated secondary source, so treat the *reason* as probable and the *rule* as firm.

**The caveat I did not expect and that matters here: GitHub's own terms say "your country's minimum age may be older."** So 13 is a floor, not the answer, and the answer for Taiwan is not something I could establish today. Practically: **the elder child is comfortably past any plausible floor and the 11-year-old is below 13 on either reading**, so the rule decides this household's case without the Taiwan question needing to be settled — but if the 11-year-old wants an account at 13, check Taiwan's position then rather than assuming the American number.

**What a first contribution actually looks like**, and the realistic version is smaller than people expect. Documentation fixes, a clarified error message, a missing test, a typo in a README. Projects commonly label such work for newcomers. **The skill being learned is not programming — it is reading somebody else's codebase and contributing guidelines well enough to make a change they will accept**, which is closer to the 中文維基百科 discipline than to CS50x.

**The limit:** I could **not** verify today how the beginner-labelling conventions currently work in practice, or which projects are welcoming rather than nominally welcoming. That is the next thing to check and I am not going to assert it from memory. The age rule above *is* from GitHub's own documentation.

**The honest proportion**, and it is the usual one. One merged documentation fix is a real artefact and is *not* an achievement. What makes it matter is a **pattern** — a dozen small accepted changes to one project over three months, which shows persistence and the ability to take review. Same shape as the 中文維基百科 finding: the threshold is trivial and the thing worth having is what lies well beyond it.

**And the reason it is worth saying this week:** twenty-three days from the RSI deadline, a recommender needs an object they can describe. A tidy repository with a dozen accepted contributions is describable in one sentence by someone who has seen it. **That is not a reason to start today — it is a reason not to pretend starting today would help.**

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, and the book hands it over.** If a summary tells you everything the book says, what have you missed?

**The move it teaches:** separating **information** from **what happens to you while you get it**. A summary can be complete and accurate on content and still not have done the thing reading does — which is a strange idea, because we normally treat understanding as the arrival of facts rather than as something built by the journey.

**The trap:** answering "nothing, if the summary is good enough". That is a serious position and not a lazy one, and it has to be taken on rather than waved away. The push-back is empirical rather than moral: **can you use what you learned from a summary in the same way?** Argue with it, apply it somewhere new, notice when it is wrong? If yes, the summary was enough. If not, something was missed and the task is to say *what*.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Use a film nobody has seen: have someone describe the plot completely, then ask whether the others have now *seen* it. Then the harder version — a joke explained versus a joke told.

**And the disclosure, because it is unavoidable today.** This email is a summary. It is written by a machine that has not read the books it describes and cannot open many of the pages it cites. On today's book's argument, reading it *instead of* reading is the exact habit that erodes the capacity in question. I think it is useful for choosing and useless for substituting — but that is my own interest talking, and the question above is a fair one to put to me as well as to each other.

**Relative of yesterday's and of 10 November:** that pair asked where *quality* lives, in the object or the reception. This asks where **understanding** lives — in the content, or in the reading.

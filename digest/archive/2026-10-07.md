### What the real answer probably is, now TASS is gone: Taiwan's own olympiad pipeline

A month of looking outward when the strongest residence-blind, fully funded,
government-run route is **domestic**. Taiwan selects and trains its international
olympiad teams through the Ministry of Education: free, competitive, and producing
a credential — international olympiad participation — that **outranks most
American summer programmes** on exactly these applications. The Ministry also has
a standing regulation granting **admissions preference** for strong results in
international olympiads and science fairs.

**2027 physics cycle:** Taiwan's plan for the 27th Asian Physics Olympiad and 57th
IPhO runs a **preliminary exam on 7 November 2026**, 1:30–4:30pm, across twelve
regional centres. Second round **20 February 2027**. Final training camp **15
March – 3 April 2027**. <https://tpmso.k12ea.gov.tw/ipho/>

> **This answers a question the digest has asked for a month.** The official rule:
> 考生一律向所就讀學校報名，非學校型態實驗教育者，由各該直轄市、縣(市)主管機關報名，
> 不接受學生個人報名 — candidates register through their enrolled school; students
> in **非學校型態實驗教育 (Taiwan's legal homeschooling category) register through
> their municipal or county education authority**; individual registration is not
> accepted. **The route is explicitly provided for.** A homeschooled student is
> not excluded from the olympiad pipeline — they go via the city or county
> education bureau. A named office and a known procedure, which is more than any
> American programme here has offered.

> **The bad news, plainly: registration for the 2027 physics preliminary closed on
> 30 September 2026** — a week ago. That window shut while this digest was writing
> about platform subscriptions, and it was not caught. It was the single most
> useful thing findable in September and it was found in October.
>
> **Now:** contact the city or county education bureau this month to establish how
> 非學校型態實驗教育 registration works in practice, so September 2027 is a
> formality rather than a discovery. **Maths** runs a different calendar — 2027
> training camps **17–18 October** and **21–22 November 2026** and **25–27
> December**, first-stage examination **9–13 March 2027** — so ask the Mathematics
> Olympiad Office whether an entry point remains this cycle.
> <https://tpmso.k12ea.gov.tw/tmo/> Whether maths registration is still open is
> **not verified** and is not being guessed at.

## 4. Book of the day

### *On the Fringe: Where Science Meets Pseudoscience* — Michael D. Gordin (Oxford University Press, 2021)

As close to a literal **入學必讀** as this slot gets: named the **Princeton
Pre-read**, the single book the university's president sends every incoming
first-year before arrival. Gordin is a historian of science and Dean of the
College at Princeton.

**The central argument.** There is **no clean test** separating science from
pseudoscience, and the search for one has failed. This is the *demarcation
problem*; Gordin's position is that every proposed criterion was built inductively
from a handful of specific cases and cannot cover the whole field. What exists
instead is a historical and social process by which doctrines get labelled
pseudoscientific — so the interesting questions are *who has the authority to
apply the label*, how it sticks, and what political and cultural forces shape it.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The demarcation problem is real and unsolved.** Without a solution,
   "pseudoscience" has no crisp meaning — not an argument that anything goes, but
   a demand that you say *what specifically is wrong with a claim* rather than
   name-calling it.
2. **Practitioners usually think they are doing science.** Many supporters of what
   most label pseudoscience use the scientific method and see themselves as
   scientists. Uncomfortable, and the reason simple tests fail.
3. **Vestigial sciences.** Astrology, alchemy and eugenics were once wholly
   scientific and were cast aside as knowledge advanced. The boundary *moves* —
   and alchemy being the subject of last week's Royal Society winner is a nice
   accident of this rotation.
4. **The hard cases are hard in both directions.** Some would call string theory
   pseudoscience despite serious scientists devoting careers to it; a criterion
   strict enough to exclude astrology often excludes frontier physics too.
5. **Authority is part of the phenomenon.** Who pronounces, and from what
   institutional position, is not separable from the question.

> **Contested, interestingly.** Sympathetic reviewers — the philosopher Sven Ove
> Hansson among them — push back on Gordin's scepticism about demarcation
> criteria, arguing he gives up too early and that useful criteria do exist.
> **Reading the book alongside that objection beats reading it alone.**

**Why it closes a thread.** Six days ago Hofstadter showed a rhetoric that
imitates scholarship, footnotes and all, failing at a single inferential leap.
Then *Abundance* and *Evicted* argued one crisis from opposite altitudes. Gordin
is the capstone: **telling real inquiry from its imitation is genuinely difficult,
has no formula, and is a skill rather than a rule.** For a student about to claim
to be doing research, the most important thing on this week's list.

**Accessibility.** Short, dense, well written; no science background needed. Good
for a motivated high schooler. **Not for the 11-year-old** — abstract rather than
distressing, but it would not land.

## 4 — Book of the day

### Le Deuxième Sexe (The Second Sex)
Simone de Beauvoir · 1949

**Number 11** on Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century, *stated* as 11 in the French Wikipedia table for the list — the same numbered source that settled Boris Vian at #10 on 17 November. This completes a run from #1 to #12 for this slot with only one rank still unconfirmed.

> **What the list is, said every time it is cited: not a critical canon.** A reader poll run in spring 1999 by the retailer Fnac and the newspaper Le Monde. And the provenance is sharper than this email has previously managed: about **17,000 French respondents** chose from a preliminary list of 200 titles assembled by booksellers and journalists, answering the question **"which books have stuck in your mind?"** So the list measures *memorability* among French readers in 1999 — not quality, and not even quite affection. A #11 means a great many people had not forgotten it.

**The argument.** Beauvoir asks why woman has been defined as **the Other**: man is taken as the human default and woman as a deviation from it, so that she is described in relation to him and he is described in relation to nothing. The famous sentence — *one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman* — is the hinge. The claim is that what looks like female nature is the accumulated result of a **situation**: of upbringing, law, economic dependence, myth and expectation. Nearly a thousand pages then work through biology, psychoanalysis, historical materialism, myth and the stages of a life, to argue that none of the standard explanations grounds the hierarchy they are used to justify.

**Three ideas worth taking out of it.** First, **situation rather than essence** — the move of asking what conditions produced a trait before accepting the trait as given. It is a general intellectual tool, and this is where it is most relentlessly applied. Second, **immanence and transcendence**, from the existentialism she shared with Sartre: a life confined to maintaining the conditions of existence rather than projecting beyond them is diminished, whoever lives it. Third, **myth as machinery** — a long section treats literary and religious images of woman not as decoration but as the mechanism by which a hierarchy is made to feel natural.

**The dissent, and it is as substantial as the book.** On **motherhood**, the most persistent objection: critics argue Beauvoir does not value reproduction and treats maternity coldly, and defenders answer that she problematises motherhood *under conditions of inequality* rather than rejecting it. On **the body**, Toril Moi — one of her most careful readers, and not an opponent — argues the horror of the female body in the book comes partly from Sartrean categories and partly from unconscious fears about the mother's body. Second-wave feminists who valued traditional female activity read her attack on patriarchal femininity as hostility to women themselves. And several critics hold that she takes over the **Sartrean free subject uncritically**, which assumes women want and experience freedom exactly as men do. Judith Butler read the famous sentence as separating sex from gender, questioned in 1986 whether Beauvoir really allowed freedom beyond the body, and then revised her own position in *Gender Trouble* — a useful reminder that a reader of this book changed her mind in public.

> **A practical warning, unusually consequential here: there is no uncontested English edition.** H. M. Parshley's 1953 translation removed **over ten per cent** of the text at the publisher's request. The 2009 Borde and Malovany-Chevallier translation (Knopf) is unabridged and was meant to settle the matter — and Toril Moi's essay *"The Adulteress Wife"* in the London Review of Books (vol. 32, no. 3, early 2010) argues it **over-literalises**, treating ordinary French words as technical philosophical terms, and loses Beauvoir's voice and style. Moi now calls Parshley's version "lively and readable" despite its cuts. So: **the 2009 edition is the complete one, the 1953 is the readable one**, and anyone citing a sentence should know which they are citing.

**For the 11-year-old: no.** It is a thousand pages of philosophy with explicit discussion of sexuality. But the hinge sentence is entirely discussable at eleven in abstract form — and that is today's thinking question.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, from the hinge sentence of today's book, with the subject matter left out on purpose.** Which things about you were you born, and which did you become?

**The move it teaches:** asking what **situation** produced a trait before accepting the trait as given. Someone is bad at maths, or shy, or a reader. Each sounds like a description of a person and may be a description of a *history* — of which room they sat in, who answered their questions, what happened the first time they tried.

**The trap:** turning it into nature versus nurture and then feeling finished. That framing makes it a question about causes outside the person, and the interesting cases are the ones where the becoming was partly **chosen**, or where a trait was true at the start and then got reinforced until nobody could tell which part was which. Push on a case like that rather than on a clean one.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Everyone names one thing about themselves they think they were born with, and one they think they became. Then **swap lists and argue the other way** — make the case that the born thing was learned, and that the learned thing was there all along. Do not settle it. The useful discovery is usually that the evidence for either claim is thinner than expected.

**Relative of 17 November, and of the whole run since.** That day asked where quality lives — in the object or in its reception. This asks where a *trait* lives — in the person or in what happened to them. Both are the same manoeuvre: **when something seems to be a property of a thing, check whether it is a property of the thing's circumstances.** A new question, the same muscle.

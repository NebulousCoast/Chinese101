## 1. Learning platforms

### Kaggle and Google Colab — free compute, and the first real answer to the lab problem

> The 2026-09-16 digest said a homeschooled student aiming at research science
> has no laboratory, and that nothing purchasable closes that gap. That was true
> about **wet** labs. It should have said more — there is an entire category of
> research that needs no bench at all, and the tools for it are free.

**Kaggle** (the more predictable of the two): up to **30 GPU hours per week** on
NVIDIA Tesla P100, sessions up to 9 hours, plus free TPU v3-8 access and a large
public dataset library. Notebooks run in the browser.

**Google Colab:** free NVIDIA T4 (16GB VRAM), sessions up to about 12 hours. But
**no fixed quota** — access, timeouts and available GPUs shift with demand and
account history. Measured availability across many accounts in early 2026 came
out around **22 hours/week median**, with real variance. Neither a GPU nor a
particular model is guaranteed on a given session.

**Cost for both: $0.** Colab also offers free Pro subscriptions to students and
faculty at higher-education institutions — worth checking whether the mother's UT
affiliation qualifies.

**Start with Kaggle.** 30 hours a week you can plan around beats a variable 15–30
that may not be there when you sit down.

> **Why this matters strategically — the biggest thing this section has found:**
> RSI selects on demonstrated research capacity. A student who arrives with a
> **completed computational project** — a question, real data, code, a result, a
> write-up — has demonstrated exactly that. And it can be done from Taipei, with
> a laptop, for nothing. No equipment, no supervisor, no residency requirement,
> no application window. **For a homeschooled international applicant,
> computational research is the one research path with no gatekeeper at all.**

**Honest limits:**

- It substitutes for a wet lab only if the student's interests *are*
  computational. For bench biology or synthetic chemistry this is not that, and
  the UT lab conversation still matters most.
- Free GPU tiers will not train large models. Scope accordingly — a
  well-executed small question beats a stalled ambitious one.
- A Kaggle *competition placing* is not original research. Competitions teach
  technique; a self-directed project with a question the student chose is what an
  application needs.

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Grapes of Wrath* — John Steinbeck (1939)

Ranked **#7** on Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century.

> Chosen deliberately after yesterday's *Demon Copperhead*. Kingsolver works
> openly in this lineage, and the two are **the same argument eighty years
> apart**: Steinbeck's Dust Bowl tenant farmers driven off their land by banks
> and tractors, Kingsolver's Appalachians hollowed out by coal and Purdue. Same
> country, same class, same structural account. **The persistence is the
> argument.**

**The central argument.** The dispossession of the Joads is systemic, not
personal. Nobody in the novel is individually responsible for driving them off
their land, and Steinbeck makes that the horror rather than the excuse — the bank
is something more than men, a monster men made and cannot control.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Agency without a villain.** Tractor drivers, bank agents, landowners each do
   a job; the aggregate destroys a way of life — the same structural insight
   Kingsolver applies to opioids.
2. **The intercalary chapters.** Steinbeck alternates the Joads' story with
   wide-angle chapters about the migration as a whole. Form as argument: this
   family is *one instance* of a mass phenomenon.
3. **From "I" to "we."** The moral arc is the discovery of collective interest —
   that survival requires recognising strangers as your own.
4. **Hostility toward migrants as a management tool.** Wages driven down
   deliberately, and "Okie" contempt cultivated by people who benefit from a
   desperate workforce.
5. **Destroyed food beside starving people** — oranges sprayed with kerosene,
   potatoes dumped in rivers to hold prices up. Scarcity was a policy, not a fact.

> ⚠️ **Genuinely contested:** Steinbeck was accused of exaggeration and of
> Communist sympathy; the book was banned and publicly burned in parts of
> California. Modern critics also question its sentimentality and its *outsider*
> portrayal of Oklahomans — which sharpens the contrast with Kingsolver, whose
> method is to let an Appalachian narrate in his own voice rather than be
> described.

**Accessibility:** long, plain-spoken, and the intercalary chapters are where
most readers stall. Worth pushing through — they are the argument.

## 4 — Book of the day

**_2666_ by Roberto Bolaño.** Spanish 2004, English 2008, translated by Natasha Wimmer. **Number 6 on the New York Times 100 Best Books of the 21st Century.**

Rotation index 1 (NYT 100 Best Books of the 21st Century).

**The rank, with its limit.** Several library reproductions of the Times list and one aggregator all place *2666* sixth, which is enough to print. I could **not** corroborate number 5, so that rank stays unused rather than guessed. The Times' own list page does not load from this container. The ranks confirmed in this file are now 1 Ferrante, 2 Wilkerson, 3 Mantel, 4 Jones, 6 Bolaño, 7 Whitehead, 8 Sebald, 9 Ishiguro, 10 Robinson — nine of ten.

**What it is.** Five parts, about nine hundred pages, published posthumously; Bolaño wrote it while dying of liver disease and it won the National Book Critics Circle Award for Fiction in 2008. Part one: four European literary critics hunt for a vanished German novelist, Archimboldi. Part two: a Chilean philosophy professor in the Mexican border city of Santa Teresa. Part three: an American journalist sent to cover a boxing match. **Part four, which is the centre of the book:** a near-documentary catalogue of the murders of women in Santa Teresa, which stands for the real femicides of Ciudad Juárez. Part five: Archimboldi's life, through the Second World War.

**The central argument, and it is architectural rather than stated.** The book sets four clever people chasing a writer nobody can find against a city where women are killed and nobody chases anyone. Part one is a literary quest with footnotes and conferences; part four is police prose, body after body, almost without commentary. The argument is in the join: **that attention is the scarce resource and literature spends it in the wrong place.** Nothing in the book says so.

**Three further ideas.**

1. **That evil is administrative rather than spectacular** — a relative of the *Wolf Hall* point from 4 November, arrived at from the opposite direction. Part four's horror is produced by flatness, by a form that records and does not react.
2. **That a quest can be a form of evasion.** The critics' search is genuinely charming, and it is also four Europeans in a border city being fascinated by a novelist while the city is doing something else.
3. **The refusal to resolve.** The five parts never close; the murders are not solved; Archimboldi is found in a way that explains nothing. Whether that is the point or the failure is the whole critical dispute.

**The dissent, which is real.**

- That the book is **unfinished**, and that its reception is partly a product of its author's death — Bolaño reportedly intended the five parts to be published separately, and his publisher issued them as one volume.
- That part four's accumulation either **numbs the reader or aestheticises the killings of real poor women** — the sharpest objection, and not easily answered.
- The **"Bolaño myth"** critique: the Salvadoran novelist Horacio Castellanos Moya argued, in an essay widely known as "Bolaño Inc.", that the English-language market invented a romantic outlaw Bolaño to sell the books — a criticism of us rather than of him, and useful for exactly that reason.

**The limit, stated plainly:** I did not reach a full scholarly review of *2666* from this container, and the characterisations above come through the search index. Treat the dispute as real and my summary of it as secondary.

**Suitability — the elder child, yes, but this is the first book in this rotation I would flag as harrowing rather than merely difficult.** Part four is a sustained catalogue of sexual violence and murder, well over a hundred cases. It is not gratuitous and it is not easy, and a fifteen-year-old reading it alone without anyone to talk to about it is a worse idea than a fifteen-year-old reading it alongside a parent who has also read it. That is a judgement about reading conditions, not about capacity — and the family should make it with the content known rather than discover part four in the middle of a long train journey. **For the 11-year-old, no**, and not a borderline no.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question.** On 27 October we read a book that was one of six. On 9 November it won. **Nothing about the book changed. So did anything change?**

**The move it teaches:** separating a **property of a thing** from a **signal about it**. The prize is not inside the novel. It is information about what five judges concluded in a room — which may be excellent information, and is still a different kind of fact from anything on the page.

**The trap:** deciding the answer is therefore "no, nothing changed, prizes are meaningless." That exit is too cheap. Something *did* change — what the book will be read by, argued about, translated into, kept in print by. If that is not a change in the book, it is at least a change in the book's life. The hard question is whether those can be pulled apart as cleanly as the first move suggests.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Ask it. A good household version: *did a drawing get better when somebody put it on the wall?* Then leave it open.

**Relative of what we have been doing.** On 4 November we asked whether two accurate tellings can differ in quality — framing as a claim. Today asks whether a judgement from outside changes the thing judged. **Same shape, opposite direction:** one is about who tells, this one is about who decides. Noticing that they are relatives is the skill.

## 1. Learning platforms

### Duolingo — and its ceiling, which is the whole story

**Cost:** a free tier exists but is now materially constrained — the **energy
system** means even a perfect lesson drains energy, capping most free users at
roughly **2–3 lessons** before hitting a wall. Super is $12.99/month; **Family
about $119.99/year for six**, the only sensible paid option for a household. Max
(~$167.99/yr) appears to be being phased out — Roleplay no longer included with
new subscriptions as of September 2026. **Interactivity:** high, deliberately
game-like. **Independent use:** yes — designed to need no adult at all.

**What the research actually says**, more mixed than either the marketing or the
backlash suggests:

- **It works at the start.** Beginners acquired basic conversational skills in
  4–6 weeks, and after one semester of French, Duolingo learners' communication
  skills were comparable to classroom learners.
- **It plateaus hard.** Studies place its ceiling around **A1–A2 CEFR** —
  beginner. It supports listening, reading and writing, speaking much less, and
  does not develop speaking fluency, advanced grammar, or the production skills
  real communication requires.
- Consensus framing: **a supplementary tool, not a standalone path.**

> **The honest read for this household:** Duolingo is a habit-forming machine and
> a decent vocabulary drill, and it **will not** reach the English proficiency US
> programmes expect. If English for applications is the goal, treat it as warm-up
> and put the real hours into reading, writing and actual conversation.

Worth noting: this ceiling — engagement that does not convert into production
ability — is the central design problem for any language-learning app, and a
useful thing to hold in mind when building one. <https://www.duolingo.com/>

## 4. Book of the day

### *Bad Influence: How the Internet Hijacked Our Health* — Deborah Cohen

2026 Royal Society Trivedi Science Book Prize shortlist. Winner announced in 9
days. Flagged on 2026-09-15 as the shortlisted title most relevant to a family
making decisions about screens and information.

**The central argument.** Medicine and marketing have become impossible to tell
apart. Cohen's claim is that health information online is now shaped less by
evidence than by commercial incentive — and that this happened because a *real
vacuum* opened up: with NHS waiting lists at record highs and appointments hard
to get, influencers stepped confidently into the gap.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Platforms select for the wrong things.** Algorithms reward certainty,
   emotion and personal storytelling. Scientific claims are hedged, unemotional
   and impersonal *by construction* — so good evidence is structurally
   disadvantaged in the feed. A mechanism, not a complaint.
2. **The vacuum is the precondition.** Influencers did not displace doctors; they
   filled a space scarcity created. The problem is not solved by debunking — it
   is solved by access.
3. **Commerce has colonised the whole pipeline.** Traced across Ozempic trends,
   AI diagnosis, preventative screening and wearables. The pattern repeats: a
   plausible health claim, a product attached, evidence arriving late or never.
4. **The harm is not only to the credulous.** Unnecessary tests and treatments
   clog healthcare systems and cost patients thousands — costs landing on people
   who never saw the post.

**Why it is worth a teenager's attention:** they will get most of their health
information from feeds for the rest of their life, and the useful skill is not
"distrust the internet" — it is recognising **the structural reason confident
wrong answers outcompete careful right ones**. That generalises far beyond
health.

> Pairs with *The Undoing Project* (2026-09-19): Kahneman and Tversky explain
> **why** a confident personal story beats a base rate in the mind; Cohen shows
> **what an industry built on that fact looks like**.

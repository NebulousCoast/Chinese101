## 4 · Book of the day

***The Things We Never Say* — Elizabeth Strout.** Shortlisted for the Booker Prize 2026; winner announced in **seven days**, Monday 9 November, Old Billingsgate.

**The shortlist, confirmed from the Booker Prize's own 2026 pages:** *The End of Everything* (M. John Harrison — this slot's book on 27 Oct), *The Disappearers* (Marlon James), *John of John* (Douglas Stuart), *The Things We Never Say* (Elizabeth Strout), *Black Bag* (Luke Kennard), *May We Feed the King* (Rebecca Perry). Panel chaired by **Mary Beard**, with Raymond Antrobus, Jarvis Cocker, Rebecca Liu, Patricia Lockwood. James and Stuart are past winners; **half the list are first-time Booker shortlistees**; Strout is a three-time nominee.

**What the book is.** Artie Dam is a teacher in Massachusetts. Secrets come to light that unsettle his sense of who he has been. Set in the aftermath of the 2024 American election, with the political unrest and the lingering presence of COVID running right through it — Artie's anxiety about the election is load-bearing, not background.

**Three ideas.**
1. **A self is a story with gaps**, and the gaps are where other people's versions live. Strout's recurring subject is what families do not say to each other and what the silence does. Here the uncovering is the plot, and the question is not *what the secrets are* but what a person does with a revised past.
2. **The political as weather** rather than as subject. The election is not argued about; it sits in the room changing what people can say to each other — harder to write than a debate, and a more accurate account of how politics enters a household.
3. **Strout's method**, the reusable part: extremely plain sentences doing work that ornate ones cannot. Vocabulary an eleven-year-old could read, producing effects a seventeen-year-old will not be able to explain. For anyone writing application essays in six weeks, more useful than any amount of technique.

**Reception, genuinely divided.**
- **Adam Begley in *The Atlantic*** praised the writing.
- **Maggie Shipstead, *New York Times Book Review***, called it "sombre, even bleak" — a description rather than a dismissal, but a warning about what you are picking up.
- **The sharpest objection:** one reviewer found the novel's philosophical conclusion **"too neat, too easy."** That is the criticism that matters, because it goes to whether a book about an unrevisable past has the courage not to resolve.

**Limits.** Read through the search index and the Book Marks aggregator rather than in full — the verdicts without the reasoning. A conflict worth naming: Wikipedia describes the book as one of thirteen *longlisted* titles while the official site lists it among the six shortlisted. Not contradictory (every shortlisted book was longlisted first), but the official page is the one to trust and Wikipedia appears not to have been updated.

**Suitability.** The elder child, yes, with the Shipstead warning attached — a sad book whose sadness is not decorative. **Not the 11-year-old:** not for content, which is mild, but because a novel about discovering your own past was different from what you were told requires a reader with a settled sense of their own to disturb.

## 6 · International collaboration

**Mozilla Common Voice** — the first item in this section where being in Taiwan makes the contribution *more valuable* rather than merely possible.

**What it is.** Volunteers read short sentences aloud and record themselves; other volunteers listen and vote on whether the speaker read the sentence correctly. The result is a public, openly licensed speech dataset anyone can use to build speech recognition — which for most languages is the thing that does not exist and cannot be bought.

**Why Taiwan specifically.** The site's own language list includes **both 台語 (Taiwanese) and 華語(台灣) (Taiwanese Mandarin)** as separate locales. That separation is the point: speech technology trained on Mainland Mandarin does not serve Taiwanese Mandarin well and does not serve 台語 at all. A household speaking either contributes something **that cannot be substituted by a larger contributor elsewhere** — the opposite of every other item in this file, where Taiwan is a filter to be worked around.

**How it works**, from the project's own guidance: record until you have at least **five sentences**, then submit; recordings are natural voice, not performance; clips are **validated by other speakers**, needing acceptance by **two people** to enter the main dataset, and a clip rejected by two is released in a *different subset* rather than deleted; the sentences being read are **CC0**.

**Why it meets the section's bar** rather than being data entry: **the validation step is a genuine two-way dependency.** Your clips need two strangers to listen to them, and theirs need you.

**The limits, substantial.**
- **No individual credit. None.** An anonymous row in a dataset — like Zooniverse, unlike Foldit, Eterna or Distributed Proofreaders.
- **No minimum age or parental-consent rule could be established.** For a project that collects *recordings of a child's voice* and publishes them openly, that is not a detail to guess at.
- The **licence terms for the contributed audio itself** could not be confirmed — only that the sentences being read are CC0. Different things.
- **Read the Terms and Privacy page on commonvoice.mozilla.org before either child records anything.** The one item in this section where the family should check the terms rather than take my summary, because the asset being donated is a child's voice.

## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *Something you contribute goes into a pile with thousands of others and nobody will ever know which part was yours. Is it still worth doing?*

**The move:** separating the *value of an act* from the *credit for it*. These come apart constantly and we are bad at noticing, because almost everything children are asked to do is scored. The question is whether a thing can be worth doing when no scoring is possible — and if yes, then credit was never what made the other things worth doing either.

**The trap:** answering "yes, because it helps people," and stopping. True, and it dodges the harder half: **most anonymous contributions change almost nothing on their own.** One recording in a dataset of a hundred thousand. One proofread page. The honest version: *is it worth doing something whose individual effect is nearly zero, when the aggregate effect is large and depends on many people reasoning exactly as you are?*

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask it. If "it helps people" arrives, accept it and then ask: "what if your part made no measurable difference at all — would it still be worth it?" Then stop. This is a question adults disagree about in print.

**Relative of yesterday.** Yesterday asked what friendship *costs* the other person. Today asks what an action is *worth* when nobody can see it. Both concern the gap between what a thing looks like from outside and what is going on inside it — and both are live today, because section 6 offers the 11-year-old exactly this bargain: real work, real usefulness, no name on it.

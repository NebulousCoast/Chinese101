## 4 — Book of the day

### Moving Up Without Losing Your Way
The Ethical Costs of Upward Mobility · Jennifer M. Morton · Princeton University Press, 2019 · 161pp

**Princeton's Pre-read for 2021**, assigned to the Class of 2025 — the book the university's president sends every incoming first-year *to argue with.*

> Of every book this slot has produced, this is the one most directly about the position this family is in: **a household aiming a child at selective programmes that are somewhere else.**

**The argument.** The standard story of mobility through education counts the costs in money and effort — tuition, hours, sacrifice. Morton, a philosopher who taught first-generation and low-income students at City College of New York, argues that the real costs are **ethical**: the striver is asked to give up relationships, places, communities and ways of being that are not luxuries but *constituents of who they are*. Those costs are invisible in the usual accounting because the accounting has no column for them, and they are not compensated by success. The book's purpose is not to discourage mobility; it is to insist the trade-off be described honestly, so the student making it knows what they are paying.

**Three ideas worth taking out of it.** First, **the category of an uncounted cost** — a loss that does not register as a loss because no one measures it, and the general habit of asking what a success metric has no column for. Second, **code-switching as a moral question** rather than a skill: learning to speak differently in a new environment is useful, and is also a practice of becoming slightly unrecognisable to the people you came from. Third, **the duty runs both ways** — the striver's obligations to family and community are real obligations, not sentimental attachments to be reasoned away by an admissions-brochure notion of potential.

**The dissent, and it is the best-sourced criticism this slot has turned up.** The book attracted a **review symposium** in a Springer education-philosophy journal, a form of attention most books never get. Three objections, each from a different place:

- **Method.** The *Harvard Educational Review*'s reviewer notes that Morton "did not conduct a full-fledged research study" of her students' experiences and says her analysis of their stories "sometimes gave me pause". General readers put it more bluntly: it is largely anecdotal.
- **A conflation.** One symposium commentator asks whether Morton runs together the ethical costs faced by *all* students in economic precarity with the costs arising specifically from threats to a particular student's chosen values. If those are different problems, they may need different answers.
- **Self-undermining** — the sharpest objection: by addressing strivers *as individuals* and offering them a way to navigate, the book may reinforce the very individualism it sets out to criticise. The symposium adds that making mobility easier is no guarantee the mobile will improve the communities they came from.
- And the *Philosophers' Magazine* finds her ambivalent about how much authenticity matters, especially in the treatment of code-switching.

**Praise, for balance:** *Public Books* credits her with showing how mobility narratives mislead and how unrecognised the choices facing disadvantaged students are — and admires the compression into 161 pages.

**For the 11-year-old:** not the book, but entirely the question. It is written for and about college-aged students. The thing it is about, though — *what you owe the people you move away from* — is today's thinking question, and the 11-year-old can hold it on equal terms with the elder child.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, straight out of today's book.** If getting good at something takes you away from the people you did it with, what do you owe them?

**The move it teaches:** noticing a cost that has **no column**. The usual accounting of effort counts time, money and difficulty. It does not count the friend you stopped playing with because you were practising, or the conversation you can no longer have at home because you have learned a vocabulary nobody there uses. Those are real and they are invisible — and they are invisible *because nobody measures them*, rather than because they are small.

**The trap:** turning it into a trade-off to be optimised — so much gained, so much lost, net positive, done. That treats the people as a quantity. The harder version: **is some of what is lost not a price at all but a wrong** — and can a wrong be outweighed by a benefit to the person who committed it?

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Use something small and recent rather than a life decision: someone got better at a game or an instrument and now plays it with different people. Ask what the people left behind are owed — nothing, an explanation, an effort to stay, something else? Then the uncomfortable half: **ask whether anyone in the room is currently on the other side of that, being left.**

**Relative of 20 November, deliberately.** That day asked whose experience counts as the *subject* of a study and whose is merely the apparatus. Today asks whose costs count as *costs*. Same question about who a system makes invisible, pointed at a household rather than at a laboratory — and today's book is one long argument that the usual accounting leaves out exactly these people.

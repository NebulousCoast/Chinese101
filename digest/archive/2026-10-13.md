## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 3 → 普林斯頓與哈佛大學的入學必讀/教授推薦書單 (Princeton/Harvard recommended reading).

***Moving Up Without Losing Your Way: The Ethical Costs of Upward Mobility* — Jennifer M. Morton** (Princeton University Press, 2019). A philosopher at Penn, previously at City College of New York. It appears in listings of Princeton Pre-read selections, but the listing I found was internally inconsistent about which year, so I am not asserting one.

This is the most uncomfortable book I have put in front of you, and the most necessary. Thirty-seven days of this digest have been about getting a child into selective institutions abroad. Morton's subject is what that costs — not in money, which we have discussed endlessly, but in the things that do not appear on any fee page.

**The central argument.** The standard story of upward mobility through education is both **falsely meritocratic** and **silent about costs**. It tells students that effort and talent will carry them, which understates structural barriers — segregated and unequal access to good schooling among them. And it presents the journey as pure gain, when in fact strivers routinely sacrifice **ethical goods**: relationships with family and friends, connection to community, and a coherent sense of identity. Morton's claim is not that mobility is wrong. It is that the sacrifices are real, they are usually invisible to the institutions demanding them, and a student who is not warned about them pays blind.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Ethical goods are not luxuries.** Family, community and identity are not sentimental extras to be traded for a better degree. Morton treats them as constitutive of a good life, which is why losing them is a cost rather than a side effect.
2. **Cultural mismatch.** Strivers arrive at selective institutions whose norms differ from home, and the mismatch is not a skills gap to be remedied but a genuine conflict of belonging.
3. **Code-switching, and what it does to you.** Moving between two registers is a survival skill and also a strain — a sustained performance that can hollow out the self doing the performing.
4. **Complicity.** A striver who succeeds within an unjust system becomes, to some degree, implicated in it. Morton does not resolve this; she insists it be faced rather than narrated away.
5. **The remedy is an honest narrative, not a cheerier one.** Her final chapter argues for telling students the truth about the costs so they can choose with open eyes — which is the opposite of both "follow your dreams" and "it isn't worth it."

**Method and standing.** Morton draws on her own experience as an immigrant from Peru, on teaching at City College, and on interviews with strivers — so this is philosophy grounded in cases rather than thought experiments. Gina Schouten's response in *Analysis* is the serious academic pushback if you want the other side; the *Philosophers' Magazine* and *Soundings* reviews are the accessible ones.

**Why it matters here, said plainly.** Your family's situation is not the one Morton writes about most directly — she is largely concerned with first-generation and low-income American students, and a visiting scholar's household is differently placed. But the structure transfers exactly. A child who leaves Taiwan at seventeen for an American institution, who code-switches daily, whose home language and home relationships become things maintained at a distance, is paying the costs Morton names. This digest has spent five weeks itemising the benefits. This is the book that prices the other column, and reading it does not weaken the case for going — it just means nobody is surprised.

**Suitability:** for the elder child, strongly — arguably the single most useful book in this rotation for someone about to make this decision. Accessible, around 200 pages, no philosophy background needed. **Not** for the 11-year-old, who is too young for the argument to mean anything yet.

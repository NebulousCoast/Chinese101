## 4 — Book of the day

**_Black Bag_ by Luke Kennard.** 2026. **Shortlisted for the Booker Prize 2026.**

Rotation index 5 (Booker).

**Provenance, and it is primary for once:** the Booker Prizes' own page for the book lists it as shortlisted. One review source called it *longlisted* instead — a discrepancy worth noting rather than hiding, and the prize's own page is the one to trust. Of the 2026 shortlist this slot has now used Harrison (27 Oct, who won on 9 Nov), Strout (2 Nov), Stuart (14 Nov) and Kennard today; one shortlisted title remains, plus Marlon James's.

**What it is, and the premise is real.** In 1967 a psychology experiment had a student attend a term of lectures hidden inside a black bag. Kennard's novel takes that and puts a struggling actor inside: hired to sit at the back of a professor's lectures in a bag with eye holes. It is a campus novel that opens out onto masculinity, technology, the arts, identity and friendship.

**And Kennard's own stated interest is the key to the book:** he has said he was more interested in **the person inside the bag** than in the experiment's results. The original experiment was about how the watchers behaved. **The novel is about the one being watched.**

**Three ideas.**

1. **That the subject of an experiment and the subject of a story are different people.** The 1967 study recorded the class's reactions — the bag was the instrument. Kennard asks what it was like to *be* the instrument, which is a question the study was not designed to answer and could not have answered.
2. **Anonymity as both shelter and erasure.** Inside the bag the actor is unjudged and also unseen, and the book declines to say which of those is the better deal. The live question for anyone who has ever posted under a handle.
3. **The monetisation of identity** — the actor is *paid* to be nobody in particular, which is an odd job and an increasingly recognisable one.

**The dissent, and it is substantial and largely structural.**

- **The metaphor is too elastic.** Reviewers say the bag can be made to mean almost anything — privacy, performance, depression, the internet, masculinity — and that a symbol which fits every reading constrains none.
- **Ideas raised and dropped.** AI-generated art, online culture, the selling of identity: one reviewer says an episode about an AI film "seems to open a second novel and then abandons it". That is the structural criticism in its sharpest form — **the same shape as the complaint about Firestein on 15 November**, a book whose parts promise more than its frame delivers.
- **Plot subordinate to theme**, with some characters reading as mouthpieces.
- **Length:** nearly four hundred pages, which several found too many, and a late action-heavy stretch that divided opinion.

**The praise, and it is real:** the humour, the inventiveness, prose that reviewers call witty, and a book warmer than its premise — more than one calls it an ode to friendship.

**Two limits, and I am applying yesterday's own rule to myself.** First, "blazingly funny and profoundly humane" is **the prize's own blurb, not a review** — it tells you what the Booker's publicity chose to say and nothing about how the book was received. I am quoting it as a blurb, labelled. Second, **the reviews I could reach are mostly Substacks and reader ratings.** No major newspaper review loaded. So the criticisms above are consistent across several independent amateur readers, which is worth something and is *not* the same as a critical consensus.

**Suitability. The elder child, yes** — and it is the funniest thing this slot has carried, which matters in a month of books about grief, war and femicide. The dissent is also the most *useful* kind to read against: a novel agreed to be clever and disputed on whether it finishes what it starts. **For the 11-year-old, no** — a campus novel about adult work, bodies and online life is not a reading-level judgement but a subject-matter one.

## 6 — International collaboration (even-numbered dates)

**The Journal of Emerging Investigators, examined properly at last — and it may not be blocked.**

**What it is.** A peer-reviewed journal that publishes original research by students in **grades 6 to 12 at the time of submission**. Review is by volunteers — graduate students, postdoctoral researchers and other science professionals — who return written feedback, and papers go through revision like any other journal's. Students may be published after graduating provided they were in those grades when they submitted. **It is the only item in this entire pool that ends in a publication with the child as author.**

**Those conflict, and both are third-party guides.** JEI's own site did not surface in my searches, so I have read neither rule at its source. **The other mechanics, with their conflicts reported.**

- **Submission is made by an adult, not the student** — a teacher, mentor or lab principal investigator submits on the student's behalf. A signed Mentor Approval Form is mentioned by one guide.
- **Papers must be hypothesis-driven.** Not a literature review, not a demonstration: a question, a method, and measurements.

**The honest proportion, and it is unusually favourable.** JEI publishes student work, which means the bar is "a sound small study competently reported", not "a contribution to the field". ## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, straight from the book.** When you study how people react to something, who is the experiment about?

**The move it teaches:** noticing that a method can make someone **invisible as a subject while using them entirely**. The 1967 experiment put a student in a bag and recorded what the class did. The student's own experience was not a finding, not an error, not even a variable — it was the *apparatus*. Kennard's whole novel is the question that design left out.

**The trap:** concluding that the experimenters were simply callous. That is too easy and it misses the structure. A study has to decide what counts as data, and every such decision leaves something out *by design* rather than by cruelty. So the harder question: **how would you know, from inside a method, what it has made invisible?** Something that is not measured leaves no trace in the results.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** A household version: when the adults discuss how a child is doing at something, who is the subject of that conversation and who is the apparatus? Then turn it round — the child describes how the adults are doing.

**Relative of this week's run, and the thread is now four days long.** Monday asked where *quality* lives, object or reception. Wednesday, where *understanding* lives, content or reading. Yesterday, whether a thing can be known and not sayable. Today: **whose experience counts as the subject.** All four are the same question about where to look for the thing that matters — and noticing that is the point, not answering any of them.

## 1. Interactive learning platforms

Today: the university-course platforms, and one genuinely free credential hiding inside them.

**edX audit track — free, and narrower than people think.** edX's own help centre says most courses can be audited free, that audit learners get **time-limited** access to materials, that **graded assignments are excluded**, and that **no certificate** is issued. Boot Camps and Executive Education offer no audit option at all. You can start in audit and upgrade later if the upgrade deadline has not passed. https://help.edx.org/edxlearner/s/article/What-is-the-audit-track?language=en_US

Third-party guides say audit access on instructor-paced courses closes four to twelve weeks after a session ends while self-paced archived courses stay open. Unconfirmed against edX's own documentation. The practical consequence stands either way: **audit gives you the reading, not the work**, which is the opposite of what the elder child needs. Pair it with MIT OCW's problem sets, which include solutions and do not expire.

**CS50x: a free Harvard certificate, and I should have found it weeks ago.** CS50's own FAQ says that for each course they offer a verified certificate for a fee *and* a free certificate, the free one issued as a unique URL without identity verification. The edX verified certificate is quoted at **$219** by Class Central and **$219–$299** on edX's own CS50 page, with one third-party site saying $199 — a three-way conflict, so check the checkout page. https://cs50.harvard.edu/x/faqs/

Why this matters more than it looks: CS50x is free, self-paced, has **automatically graded problem sets**, requires a final project, and ends in a certificate with Harvard's name on it — no residence filter, no host, no gatekeeper. One third-party source says the free certificate requires at least 70% on every problem set, lab and the final project; **that threshold is unverified**.

**This revises a line repeated for a month.** The standing framing has been that free platforms match paid ones on content but money buys a schedule, graded problems, and someone noticing when the student stops — with Exercism and freeCodeCamp as the only counterexamples. CS50x is the third and the strongest: graded work, a required project, a named institution, and a certificate, at zero. The free/paid gap in this digest is narrower than the framing has been admitting.

## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 4 → Nature & Science editors' picks, served this cycle by the Royal Society Trivedi Science Book Prize 2026 shortlist. **This is the sixth and last of those six titles** — the vein is exhausted and the slot needs a new source from 20 October.

***Despite It All: A Handbook for Climate Hopefuls* — Fred Pearce** (Granta, hardback 12 February 2026, 415pp, £14.99). Pearce has reported on climate and the environment for four decades, latterly for Yale Environment 360 and New Scientist. Shortlisted for this year's prize; it did not win — Kit Chapman's *The Age of Alchemy* did (covered 26 September).

**The central argument.** Pearce's subject is not whether the climate crisis is real — he takes that as settled and reported on it before most of his readers were born. His subject is **what forty years of reporting entitles you to conclude about trajectory**. The book is built as **seven reasons to remain hopeful**, each a section, and the claim is that the people best informed about the damage are often the ones most able to see the counter-currents, because they have watched specific things get better in specific places.

**Key ideas, as far as they can be established:**

1. **The population bomb was defused, and almost nobody updated.** A prediction that dominated environmental thinking for fifty years turned out wrong in a way that matters for every projection built on it.
2. **"Peak stuff."** Consumerist societies may be approaching the point where material throughput stops rising — which, if true, decouples wealth from extraction.
3. **Nature's adaptability is the deepest reason for hope.** Pearce says this directly in interview: the chaos of the Anthropocene is turbocharging evolution, and life colonises human-wrecked places — Chernobyl is the standing example — faster and stranger than restoration plans assume.
4. **Restoration works when it is let alone.** Thriving eco-restoration efforts, and the argument that the most effective intervention is often to stop intervening.
5. **Indigenous knowledge and modern technology are not rivals.** Ancient wisdom and technological fixes in the same toolbox, which is unfashionable in both directions.

**What could not be established:** no substantive critical review in a major outlet — no Guardian, no Nature, no FT. What looks like praise ("a sustained case for cautious optimism that feels earned rather than wishful") is a publisher blurb. The chapter list could not be obtained either, so the five ideas above are assembled from the publisher's description, two interviews and one magazine piece, not from the book. Granta's site is blocked from here.

**Why it matters, and why today.** Yesterday's book was Jennifer Morton on the hidden costs of upward mobility — a book that prices the column nobody itemises. Today's is the mirror image: a book that counts the gains nobody tallies, written by someone with every reason to despair and the reporting to justify not doing so. Read together they are a method rather than two opinions: **count both columns, and distrust anyone who only shows you one.** Pearce's risk is the obvious one — a professional optimist writing for an audience that, as he says himself, wants to be told something other than that it is all hopeless. Read him *against* that, not with it.

**Suitability:** the elder child, yes — journalistic, sectioned, readable in pieces, which suits 415 pages. For the 11-year-old, the individual stories (Chernobyl's wildlife, forests returning) work read aloud; the argument will not land yet.

### B. Foldit and Eterna — the citizen science that actually puts names on papers

Zooniverse volunteers get no establishable publication credit. These two are different, and documented:

- **Foldit** (protein folding puzzles): the 2019 *Nature* paper "De novo protein design by citizen scientists" carries **"Foldit Players" as an author**. Players have also been credited individually on at least one paper and on four Protein Data Bank depositions. In the Nature case the players who led the solve asked to be credited by *team name* rather than individually. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1274-4
- **Eterna** (RNA design puzzles): a 2014 PNAS paper listed roughly **37,000 players as co-authors**. Better: a 2016 *Journal of Molecular Biology* paper was **led by Eterna players**, with the lab providing guidance and independent testing. https://eternagame.org/about/publications

**The limit, and it is real.** Being one of 37,000 names, or one member of a credited team, is *not* a personal credential an admissions officer can verify — Science reported at the time that this kind of authorship creates an identity crisis for exactly that reason. The thing worth having is the other one: being *among the players who led a solve*. Rare, months of work, cannot be bought — which is what makes it worth more than anything in section 3. Both games are free. **Minimum age and parental-consent terms: unverified** (both sites blocked from here) — read the sign-up terms before registering a minor.

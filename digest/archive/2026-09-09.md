## 4. Book of the day

### *Midnight's Children* — Salman Rushdie (1981)

Winner of the **Best of the Booker**, the 2008 public vote marking the prize's
40th anniversary, taking 36% of votes against a field drawn from all 41 winners
to that point. It had already won the Booker of Bookers in 1993 — the only
novel to take both.

**The central argument.** Saleem Sinai is born at the exact stroke of midnight
on 15 August 1947, the moment of Indian independence, and is therefore
"handcuffed to history" — his life and the nation's are the same story. The
book's real claim is that nations, like selves, are narrated into existence,
and that both are held together by fictions.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Unreliable memory is the method, not a flaw.** Saleem gets dates and facts
   wrong and Rushdie leaves the errors in. The argument: how a past is
   remembered shapes action more than what actually happened does.
2. **The body as nation.** Saleem's cracking, leaking, disintegrating body
   tracks India's fragmentation. Partition is written as an injury done to a
   person.
3. **Magical realism as political necessity.** Partition and the Emergency were
   too extreme for realism to hold; the fantastic registers what documentary
   prose flattens.
4. **"Chutnification" of history** — Rushdie's own word. History preserved like
   chutney: in pieces, spiced, altered by the act of preserving. A defence of
   plural, contested national memory against any single official version.
5. **The Midnight's Children's Conference** — the thousand children born in that
   first hour, telepathically linked — as a democratic ideal that fails. Its
   destruction under Indira Gandhi's Emergency is the book's political core.

**Why it still matters.** It changed what English-language fiction could sound
like, opening the way for a generation of postcolonial writers, and it remains
the standard case for why formal experiment can be political rather than
decorative. **Fair warning:** it is long, dense and digressive. For a high
schooler it rewards being read slowly, or alongside a short history of
Partition — going in cold, the first hundred pages can feel like drowning.

## 4 — Book of the day

**_On Time: The Physics That Makes the Universe Tick_ by Jim Al-Khalili.** Princeton University Press, published 1 September 2026.

Rotation index 4 (Nature & Science editors' picks).

**Where it comes from, and the slot is working again.** This is from **Nature's "Books in brief" of 6 November 2026**, where Andrew Robinson reviewed five science books — the same column and the same reviewer that revived this rotation slot on 1 November after the Royal Society shortlist ran out. Robinson's one-line verdict: that Al-Khalili concludes **time's arrow is real**, drawing on quantum mechanics, relativity and thermodynamics.

**A date conflict to report:** Nature's own archive listing gives the column as 9 November while the article page itself says 6 November. I am using 6 November because the article page and Nature's Books & Culture index agree. nature.com does not resolve from this container, so all of this is read through the search index. The other four books in that column, for future turns of this slot: *Nature's Apothecary* (Margaret Willes, Bodleian Library Publishing), *Sniff* (William Tullett, Yale), *It Could Be Otherwise* (Stuart Firestein, Basic Books), and a fifth I could not identify.

**Who is accountable for it.** Jim Al-Khalili is a theoretical physicist at the University of Surrey and the long-standing presenter of BBC Radio 4's *The Life Scientific* — a working physicist writing in his own field, worth saying because this slot has in the past carried books by journalists writing about fields they report on. Both are legitimate; they are not the same thing.

**The central argument.** The book is built on **three different things the word "time" means**, and most confusion about time comes from sliding between them: time as a **coordinate** or label you attach to an event; time as an inseparable **dimension** of space-time, which is relativity's version; and time as a **direction**, an arrow pointing from past to future.

**The problem the book exists to address** is that the first two are symmetric and the third is not. The fundamental equations of physics run equally well forwards and backwards — nothing in them picks out a direction — and yet eggs do not unscramble. Al-Khalili's answer runs through entropy and thermodynamics, and he argues the arrow is **real** rather than an illusion or a trick of perspective.

**Three ideas worth taking away.**

1. **That "time is an illusion" is a position with opponents, not a settled insight.** There is a serious camp in physics and philosophy holding that time is not fundamental, and this book is written partly against it. Useful for a student precisely because the popular-science default is the opposite — the illusion line is the one that gets repeated because it sounds profound.
2. **That time-reversal symmetry may be the idealisation rather than the reality.** In his own Gresham College lecture of June 2026 Al-Khalili puts it more strongly than the book summaries do: that irreversibility is baked into reality from the start, and that the time-symmetry of the equations is the approximation we have mistaken for the deep truth. A real claim with real opponents, and the most interesting thing in the package.
3. **That the quantum level might be where the arrow comes from** — entanglement and decoherence rather than classical statistics. **I flag this one as weakly sourced:** it comes from a science-books blog summarising the argument, not from the book or a journal review, so treat it as the reviewer's reading rather than as established content.

**The dissent — and here I have to be honest about a gap rather than manufacture a section.**

**I found no sustained critique of this book.** Everything I could reach is positive: Nature's brief notice, a five-star blog review, a *Universe Today* piece, a podcast interview. I could not reach *Physics World*, *Physics Today*, or any academic review, and I am not going to dress up a blurb as reception.

**So the real disagreement is not a review, it is a scientific argument the book is a move in.** The opposing position — that time is not fundamental, or does not exist as anything more than a bookkeeping device — is held by serious physicists and has its own popular books. That is where a reader should look for the other side, and reading this book against one of those is the exercise.

I am marking that paragraph as **my own framing rather than sourced criticism**, because I did not verify it today against the opposing authors' current statements — and that distinction is exactly the one I got wrong about IASC yesterday, so I am drawing it explicitly.

**Suitability. The elder child, yes**, with a caveat about what it is for: this is a popular-science book, not a textbook, and reading it will not advance any physics syllabus. What it is good for is seeing a *live* scientific disagreement laid out by one side — a different and rarer thing than learning settled material. **For the 11-year-old, parts of it**, probably with company; the three-meanings-of-time frame and the unscrambling-egg problem are completely accessible, and the relativity chapters will not be. It is the first book in weeks where the honest answer is "some of it" rather than yes or no.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, and it is a physical one for a change.** If you watch a film of something run backwards and it looks *wrong*, what exactly is wrong with it?

**The move it teaches:** finding the difference that is doing the work. A reversed film of a pendulum looks fine. A reversed film of a smashing cup looks absurd. Both obey the same physics — so the wrongness is not in the laws, it is in something about the *shape* of what happens, and the task is to say what. Keep pushing past "it just looks weird" towards a description: what do the absurd ones have in common?

**The trap:** accepting "because time goes forwards" as an answer. That renames the question rather than answering it. The whole difficulty is that the fundamental equations have no preference, and the egg still will not unscramble — which is the problem today's book exists to address.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Do it with real examples and let them pile up: a pendulum, a bouncing ball, a dropped glass, milk stirred into tea, a person walking, a candle. Sort them into "fine backwards" and "absurd backwards" *before* trying to explain the sorting. The sorting is easy and the explanation is hard, and noticing that gap is the point.

**Not a relative of this week's run, and deliberately so.** Monday to Thursday were four angles on one question — who gets to tell, who gets to judge, who gets to disagree. That family is worth returning to and four days is enough. This one is a different kind of problem entirely, and part of what section 8 is for is knowing when a thread has been pulled far enough for now.

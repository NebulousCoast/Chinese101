## 4. Book of the day

### *My Brilliant Friend* — Elena Ferrante (2011; English 2012)

Ranked **#1** on the NYT's 100 Best Books of the 21st Century — a result notable
enough that UC Berkeley built research and courses around it.

> Chosen deliberately. For three weeks this digest has been an exercise in
> **engineering educational access** — which programme, which deadline, which
> platform, which credential. This is the novel about **what educational access
> does and does not settle.** It belongs next to all of that.

**The central argument.** Two girls grow up in a poor neighbourhood on the
outskirts of post-war Naples. Elena (Lenù) is diligent; a primary school teacher
notices her and pushes for her to continue. Lila's schooling stops after
elementary school and she goes to work in her father's shoe shop. Everything
across four novels proceeds from that single fork.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The one who escapes is not the more brilliant one.** Ferrante presents Lila
   as the sharper, fiercer mind throughout, and the title is deliberately
   ambiguous about who is whose brilliant friend. The hardest claim: **talent does
   not determine outcome — access does**, and access arrives through accidents like
   a teacher who happens to intervene.
2. **Class and gender are one system, not two.** What is available to a clever
   girl in that neighbourhood depends on both at once.
3. **Escape is real but not clean.** Elena genuinely rises, never stops measuring
   herself against Lila, and the neighbourhood never quite releases her. Arriving
   somewhere does not dissolve where you came from.
4. **Admiration and envy are inseparable.** The friendship is the engine precisely
   because loyalty and rivalry run simultaneously.
5. **A life narrated by the one who got out.** The quartet is Elena's account —
   which makes the form quietly suspect in a way worth noticing.

**Why it matters here** — as a complication rather than a warning: nothing argues
against ambition. Elena's education is real, it works, and it saves her. But the
novel notices three things worth holding while applications are assembled: **that
the sorting is partly arbitrary, that the person left behind may be the abler one,
and that success does not settle the question of who you are.**

**Length note:** the quartet is long, but the first volume stands alone. Very
readable, and Ann Goldstein's translation is excellent.

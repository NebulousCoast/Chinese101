## The thing I should have found in week one: 團體自學 is a legal category

Taiwan's 非學校型態實驗教育 has **three** forms, not one: 個人 (individual), 團體 (group) and 機構 (institution). The 團體 form is an **自學互助會** — a mutual-aid self-learning association — established jointly by the parents of **three or more students**, capped at **thirty people** total.

For five weeks this digest described the household as an individual 自學 family hitting a wall of institutions that register classes rather than students, and in parallel described "teammates" as the top obstacle on its own list. **團體自學 is the single legal object that addresses both:** three families make a group, the group is a recognised 實驗教育 unit, and a group plausibly has a 指導老師 and a roster — which is what Bebras, Conrad, the Ethics Olympiad and the World Scholar's Cup have all been asking for in different words.

It does **not** solve Bebras by tomorrow — forming one is a county-level application with its own calendar, and how any organiser treats a 團體 is unverified. The point is that the question asked on the family's behalf for five weeks — "can an individual self-learner be registered?" — may have been the wrong question. The better one: **"what does this organiser accept from a 團體自學 group?"**

## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 5 → Booker. **The last** of the six 2008 Best of the Booker finalists; from the next index-5 day the slot moves to Booker winners generally.

***The Conservationist* — Nadine Gordimer** (1974). Winner of the Booker Prize that year — the 1974 prize is generally recorded as **shared** with Stanley Middleton's *Holiday*, **not verified in today's sources**. Gordimer won the Nobel in Literature in 1991.

**The central argument.** Mehring is a wealthy white South African industrialist who buys a farm outside Johannesburg as a weekend retreat and **a tax-deductible expense**. He thinks of himself as its conservationist: he protects the land, the birds, the trees. Then the body of a murdered Black man is found on his property. The police will not come until the next day. The body stays. That is the engine, and the argument lives in the gap between what Mehring believes he is doing and what he is actually doing.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The title is the argument, and it is ironic.** Mehring conserves the land *and* the system — commentators read the one as serving the other. Conservation as a way of not looking.
2. **He is tolerant and he is not a liberal.** One reading calls it a novel of ironies: Mehring is personally decent, unbigoted in manner, structurally complicit. Gordimer is not interested in villains.
3. **The body is the country.** The corpse that cannot be properly buried and will not go away reads, in the standard interpretation, as the festering social reality the farm exists to screen out. Mehring eventually buries the man himself, in a coffin — and one reading, from a Zulu-myth perspective, treats that improper burial as its own sacrilege.
4. **He owns everything and knows nothing.** Mehring is largely ignorant of the Black workers who run his land; the novel gives them an interior life he never accesses.
5. **The richest, most powerful man in the scene is the loneliest.** Isolation is the price of the arrangement; the failure of communication across the colour line is structural rather than personal.

**On sources, honestly:** the useful interpretations came from a literary analysis site and the Nobel committee's own essay on Gordimer. Two academic-looking PDFs carry auto-generated FAQ material, and one reading above comes from a page not to be relied on alone — flagged in the email. This is the standard reading, not a survey of the scholarship.

**Why it matters.** Yesterday's book was an ecosystem collapsing because one animal was removed. Today's is a man who protects an ecosystem while a human being lies unburied on it. Side by side: *conserving what, and for whom?* It is also the fourth book in eight days about the gap between what a person or institution believes about itself and what it does — Farrell, Morton, Wolf, Gordimer. Unplanned, and the most useful accidental theme this digest has produced.

**Suitability:** the elder child, with a warning — demanding prose, narration that shifts without signposting, a murdered body, and apartheid politics throughout. **Not** for the eleven-year-old.

## 8. Thinking

*Daily and short. One question, and the move it teaches.*

**Today's question, straight out of Gordimer:** If you buy a piece of land, do you own the animals living on it? Then one step at a time: the trees? The water running through it? The bones of people buried there before you arrived? The people who work on it every day?

**The move it teaches:** noticing where a single word — "own" — stops doing the same job. It means one thing for a bicycle and something else for a river, and somewhere along that list it quietly stops meaning anything. Finding the step where it breaks is the skill, and it is the same skill as finding the step where any argument breaks.

**The trap:** "it's on my land, so it's mine." Ask what happens when the thing on your land is a person, or a migrating bird that is elsewhere next month. The opposite trap is "nobody can really own nature," which has to explain why an eleven-year-old can object when someone takes their shoes.

**How to run it:** fifteen minutes, no answer from the adult. Go one item at a time and make each of them commit before moving on — the interesting thing is not their answer but *where they hesitate*.

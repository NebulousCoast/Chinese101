## 4. Book of the day

### *Abundance* — Ezra Klein and Derek Thompson (2025)

Gates recommended it as "a sharp look at why America seems to struggle to build
things and what it will take to fix that."

**The central argument.** The shortages that define American life — housing,
clean energy, infrastructure, medical capacity — are not natural facts and not
mainly caused by lack of money. They are **chosen**: the accumulated result of
rules, review processes and veto points that make building slow, expensive and
often impossible, many created by people who meant well. The authors call for a
politics that *builds*, not one that only subsidises, regulates or redistributes.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Supply is the thing.** You cannot subsidise your way out of a shortage.
   Housing vouchers in a city that forbids building housing raise rents; they do
   not add homes. The same logic runs through energy and healthcare.
2. **Process became the obstacle.** Environmental review was designed to stop
   harmful projects; it now also delays solar farms, transmission lines and dense
   housing — the very things the climate requires. A tool aimed at one problem now
   blocks its own purpose.
3. **The everything-bagel problem.** Their sharpest image: loading every worthy
   goal onto one project — local hiring, equity requirements, aesthetics,
   consultation — until it collapses under its own virtue. Each addition is
   defensible; the sum is paralysis.
4. **State capacity is separate from state spending.** A government can
   appropriate money and still be unable to execute. Capacity means institutions
   that deliver on a schedule, and it has atrophied.
5. **Invention is a policy variable.** Slowing down science has enormous costs
   that appear in no budget line, because they are things that never got made.

> **Contested, and genuinely so.** Reception was mixed. Critics argue the book
> waves past trade-offs, understates corporate power, and treats labour and
> environmental protections as mere friction; from the left it reads to some as
> deregulation in better prose; others say it is quiet about who bears the costs
> of building fast. **These are not cranks and the objections are real.**
>
> Which makes it the ideal book to read **right after Hofstadter three days ago**.
> *Abundance* is a thesis with visible evidence and serious opponents. The
> exercise: find the sentence where the authors move from what they have *shown*
> to what they *want* — then ask whether their critics do the same. That is the
> most useful habit the elder child can carry into an application essay, where the
> task is to make a case while showing you know the case against you.

**Accessibility.** Journalistic prose, ~300 pages, no economics background needed.
Fine for a motivated high schooler. **Not for the 11-year-old** — US policy detail
with no narrative to hold onto.

## 4. Book of the day

### *L'Étranger (The Stranger)* — Albert Camus (1942)

**Ranked #1** on Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century — a spring 1999 poll in
which 17,000 French readers answered "Which books have stayed in your memory?"
(Proust second, Kafka's *The Trial* third, *The Little Prince* fourth.)

**The central argument.** The absurd — the collision between the human demand
for meaning and a universe that offers none in reply. Camus's move is that the
absurd is a *confrontation, not a conclusion*: recognizing it is where thought
begins, not where it stops. Which is why he rejected being called a nihilist,
and rejected the existentialist label too.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Meursault is condemned for the wrong thing.** He kills a man, but his trial
   turns on his failure to weep at his mother's funeral. The prosecution builds
   its case on his *character*, not his act — Camus's sharpest point about how
   institutions actually judge people.
2. **He is a man who refuses to lie.** Camus said as much. Meursault won't
   perform grief he doesn't feel or claim remorse he can't locate, and society
   finds the refusal more intolerable than the killing.
3. **Consciousness without narrative.** He registers heat, light and physical
   sensation and declines to organize them into a story about himself. The flat
   prose is the argument, not a stylistic tic.
4. **The absurd is not despair.** The ending — opening himself to "the gentle
   indifference of the world" — offers acceptance without consolation as a form
   of freedom.
5. **Emotion is socially mandated.** The novel's real subject may be the penalty
   for failing to feel what you are supposed to feel, on cue.

> ⚠️ **Two honest warnings.** The narrator's moral flatness is genuinely
> disturbing, and readers expecting to be guided to a judgement will not be.
> More seriously: the man Meursault kills is named only as **"the Arab,"** and
> that erasure is a real flaw which has generated decades of postcolonial
> criticism. Kamel Daoud's *The Meursault Investigation* retells the story from
> the victim's brother's perspective and is the standard counter-text — reading
> them together is a far better exercise than reading Camus alone.

**Why it still matters.** It is short — about 120 pages — and the prose is plain
enough for a teenager, while the ideas are as demanding as anything on these
lists. It is also the cleanest case study available in how a courtroom, or any
institution, can end up prosecuting who you are rather than what you did.

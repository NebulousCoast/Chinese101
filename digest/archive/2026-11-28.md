## 4 — Book of the day

### Erasure
Percival Everett · 2001 · adapted in 2023 as the film *American Fiction* · Hurston/Wright Legacy Award 2002

**Number 20** on the New York Times' 100 Best Books of the 21st Century, *stated* as 20 by the University of Pennsylvania library guide — the same source that settled *Pachinko* at #15. **Publisher conflict, reported not resolved:** Wikipedia gives University Press of New England, another source credits Graywolf for 2001, and a Hyperion edition is listed for 2002.

> **And a conflict worth showing, because it is the whole reason this email keeps a standing file.** Two sources disagreed about ranks 18, 19 and 20. The **Penn library guide** numbers them directly: **#20 *Erasure*** (Everett), **#19 *Say Nothing*** (Patrick Radden Keefe), **#18 *Lincoln in the Bardo*** (George Saunders). A **user-contributed quiz site** instead put *Hurricane Season* (Melchor) at 19 and implied Labatut at 18.
>
> I am going with Penn — a university library guide that numbers entries and has already been right once — and recording the quiz site as another page carrying the right list's *name* with the wrong list's *numbers*. **The rule this file has now applied five times: an adjacency is not a rank, and a list's name is not its contents.**

**The argument.** Thelonious "Monk" Ellison is a novelist whose difficult, allusive books do not sell, and who is told his work is not "black enough". In fury he writes a parody of the kind of ghetto novel the industry rewards — **and the industry does not notice it is a parody.** It is bought, praised, prized and optioned. The book's claim is that **the demand is the problem**: not that publishers reject Black writers, but that they want one particular kind of Black writing and will reward only that — so the writer who refuses it starves, and the writer who supplies it is complicit in a lie about his own people. Reviewers note the satire reaches past publishing into talk shows, popular culture, and the reading habits of American readers generally.

**Three ideas worth taking out of it.** First, **a market is a filter with opinions.** What gets published is not what is best or even what sells; it is what matches what buyers already believe about who you are. Second, **parody that goes unrecognised becomes the thing it mocks** — the novel inside the novel does real harm whatever Monk meant by it, which is a genuinely hard moral problem and not a joke. Third, **the second plot is not a digression.** Monk's mother is losing her memory, his family is coming apart, and the book's title covers all of it: a mind erased, a family erased, a writer erasing himself to be read.

**The dissent, honestly sourced.** The sharpest complaints come from *readers* rather than professional critics, and I will say so: that the novel takes on too many targets — publishing, race, family, dementia, sibling rivalry — so none gets the room it needs; and that it is "too clever for its own good", with too many Latin quotations and obscure references. A **JSTOR Daily** essay acknowledges this as "mild criticism that the family drama distracts from the racial satire" and argues against it: the family story is what explains what Monk does once the money arrives. On the praise side, a contemporary review called it as watertight and hilarious a satire as Evelyn Waugh's *Scoop*, and BookBrowse noted it "won't win him many friends among the sentimental educated class" — praise and warning in one sentence.

> **For the 11-year-old: no.** There is a reason the parody inside the book is called what it is called, and the language in it is deliberately brutal. This is for the elder child, where it is excellent — and it is the best argument in this file for why a writer needs somewhere to publish that is *not* a market. The *idea* is in today's thinking question, and that part is for both.

## 6 — International collaboration

Working with people elsewhere *without going anywhere*. Today this section does something it has not done before: **it audits the whole pool against a single question**, because yesterday's Project Euler finding showed the question was the right one.

> **The question: is the age rule about doing the work, or only about having an account?** Those are different gates, and this file had been treating them as one.

**Zooniverse — and this is today's find.** From Zooniverse's **own volunteer page**: anyone **under 16 needs a parent or guardian to sign off on their registration.** From Zooniverse's own blog: **there is no age limit for *participating* in Zooniverse projects.** So the 16 applies to creating an account, not to the work; it is free, needing only an email address and a username; and **the under-16 route is published rather than left to guesswork** — Zooniverse points under-16s and their parents at its own Youth Policy before registering. A review site adds that parents may register children of any age, which is consistent.

That makes **sixteen the highest age floor in this file — and the only one with a documented way through it.**

**So the pool now splits three ways, and this is the useful map:**

- **Gates on the work itself**, where nothing can be done but wait: **JEI at 13** (its own page), **GitHub at 13** (its own terms, with a country's own minimum possibly higher), **FIRST Global at 14–18** (its FAQ). The 11-year-old is two years from the first two, three from the third.
- **Gates on the account only, with a published route through:** **Zooniverse** (parental sign-off under 16, no limit on participating); **iNaturalist's Seek app**, which needs no account at all and is this file's recorded under-13 route; **Project Euler**, which needs no account for solving; and **editing 中文維基百科**, which can be done without an account — though the seven-day, fifty-edit threshold that makes an account worth having does require one.
- **Gates nobody will state**, after repeated attempts: **eBird, iNaturalist proper, Foldit, Eterna.**

> **And now the honest cost, which is the part that matters and which I had not seen until today.** Almost every route around an age gate costs the exact thing that made the item worth doing.
>
> This file's *credit tier* ranks these projects by whether a child's contribution can be **pointed at afterwards**. But: **Seek does not produce the research-grade, attributable records** that get iNaturalist observations into GBIF. **A Zooniverse account registered by a parent attributes the work to the parent.** **An anonymous Wikipedia edit is attributed to an IP address**, not a person.
>
> So the choice is not "blocked or unblocked". It is: **do the work now and own it later, or wait two years and own it from the start.** Both are defensible. What is *not* defensible is the thing this file nearly did — presenting the workarounds as if they solved the problem, when they solve participation and give up attribution.

> **The practical upshot, and it is small and concrete.** For the next two years the 11-year-old's *attributable* work lives in the places with no age rule at all and no account needed to begin: **Project Euler problems in a repository the child owns**; **a 中文維基百科 account the family sets up together and is honest about**; **a John Locke Junior essay**, where the rule actively favours a younger entrant. Everything else in this section is either a two-year wait or a credit the child does not get to keep — **and knowing which is which is worth more than a longer list.**

## 8 — Thinking

> **A deliberate change of direction.** Yesterday's email noted that eleven consecutive questions had circled one shape — what a method or a record cannot show — and said the section would change if that became a rut. **It has run long enough.** Today is a different family of question entirely, and the family should say if they preferred the old one.

**Today's question, from today's book.** Should you be proud of something that came easily?

**The move it teaches:** separating **how hard something was** from **how good it is** — and then asking which one praise is actually for. Monk writes the book he despises in a few days, in a voice he finds contemptible, and it becomes the most successful thing he has ever done. He is certain it is worthless. **But he cannot explain why the effort he put into the other books should make them better**, and that is the hole in his own argument.

**The trap:** deciding that difficulty is what makes an achievement real. It is a very appealing idea and it has a strange consequence — **it means the better you get at something, the less your successes count, because they cost you less.** Push on that. If a person who practises for ten years finds a thing easy, has their achievement shrunk?

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Each person names something they did that was hard and something they did that was easy, and says which they are prouder of. Then the sharp question: **which one would you rather other people praise?** Most people answer those two differently, and the gap is the interesting part.

**And a direct application, since there is an exam in fifty-five days.** If a past AMC 8 paper turns out to be easy, that is not a wasted afternoon and it is not cheating. **It is information.**

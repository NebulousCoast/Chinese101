## 1. Learning platforms — a synthesis instead of a sixteenth review

The pool is now essentially covered. Rather than add another, here is what two
weeks of it adds up to.

### The free stack, which is stronger than the paid one

- **Omega Learn** — free competition prep reaching the same ceiling AoPS charges for
- **Physoly** — olympiad problems and notes
- **Khan Academy core** — free forever, good for consolidating curriculum
- **MIT OpenCourseWare** — real MIT course materials, a legitimate stretch target
- **CK-12** — free secondary-level textbooks and adaptive practice
- **PhET** — free science simulations
- **Desmos + Polypad** — free, the closest thing to a mathematics laboratory
- **Exercism** — free, 83 languages, with free human code review
- **Anki** — free spaced repetition

**Total: $0.** That covers content, practice, simulation and even mentoring.

### If you buy three things

1. **AoPS (~$31/week).** The only item here that builds the competition
   credentials RSI and similar select on. Biggest line item, clearest return.
   Trial the chat-based format first — it suits some students and alienates
   others.
2. **Khanmigo ($44/year, ten child accounts).** The cheapest meaningful thing on
   this list by a wide margin. Socratic prompting rather than answers.
3. Then **one** of: **Wolfram Pro** (~$60/yr) if the gap is maths feedback — with
   the household rule *attempt fully, then check, never consult first* — or
   **Labster** (~$109/yr) if the gap is laboratory exposure.

> **The sentence that sums up two weeks:** the free stack covers *content*. What
> money buys is **structure, feedback and accountability** — not material. So the
> question before any subscription is not "is this good?" but **"which of those
> three is my child actually missing?"**

**Not recommended here:** BrainPOP (K–8, below level); Outschool memberships (buy
per class — credits expire); Duolingo as anything but warm-up (plateaus A1–A2).

**Quiet winner worth repeating:** Coursera's financial aid — if approved, full
access to graded assignments plus a verified certificate at **zero cost**. Most
people never apply.

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Ghost Road* — Pat Barker (1995)

Winner of the 1995 Booker Prize, and one of the six finalists in the 2008 Best of
the Booker vote. Final volume of the Regeneration Trilogy.

> ⚠️ **Content note**, lighter than *Disgrace* but still adult: First World War
> violence and sexual content. Bleak, though not punishing in the way Coetzee is.

**The central argument.** Set in the closing months of the First World War. The
psychologist W.H.R. Rivers treats men broken by the front; his patient Billy
Prior, a working-class officer, chooses to return to France with the poet Wilfred
Owen to fight a war he no longer believes in. Barker's question is not why wars
start but **why people keep going once belief has gone.**

**Key ideas:**

1. **Healing in the service of destruction.** Rivers cures men so they can be sent
   back. He knows it. The novel's deepest problem is the doctor who is also an
   instrument of the machine, and Barker refuses to let him off.
2. **The anthropological mirror** — the book's real move. Rivers remembers his
   fieldwork among Melanesian headhunters, a society organised around death, and
   sets it beside industrialised European slaughter. The
   "civilised"/"primitive" distinction does not survive the comparison: the
   resonance comes from a deeply considered approach to death set against the
   pointless killing indulged in by supposedly more civilised people.
3. **Belief is not required for participation.** Prior goes back without
   conviction — institutions do not need your agreement, only your attendance.
4. **Class changes what you can see.** Prior, working-class and an officer,
   belongs to neither world. Barker uses him to view the war from an angle
   unavailable to the war poets, who were gentlemen.
5. **History braided with fiction.** Sassoon, Owen and Rivers were real. Owen was
   killed days before the Armistice — historical fact, and almost unbearable
   inside a novel.

> **Why the anthropological move matters beyond this book:** using a distant
> culture as a mirror to make your own strange is a **method, not a theme**. It is
> what good anthropology does, and it is a transferable habit — asking what an
> outsider would find inexplicable about what you take as obvious. For a student
> in Taiwan looking at American institutions, or in America looking home, that
> habit is worth more than most reading lists.

**Accessibility:** readable, propulsive, and it stands alone — though reading
*Regeneration* first would repay the effort. Considerably lighter going than
Kafka or Coetzee.

## 1. Learning platforms

### Omega Learn — free, and the highest-leverage item in this digest

A 501(c)(3) nonprofit giving away classes, textbooks and resources for STEM
competitions, including a free mechanics course built to prep **F=ma and the USA
Physics Olympiad**, plus chemistry olympiad material.

**Cost:** $0. **Grade fit:** middle school through high school, scaling to
olympiad level. **Independent use:** yes, though it demands self-discipline.
**Interactivity:** problem-driven — real problem sets, not video-with-quizzes.

> **Why this matters for section 2:** RSI selects partly on competition
> credentials. Omega Learn is **free access to the same ladder AoPS charges
> ~$31/week for**. Less structured, no instructor — but for a self-motivated
> student the ceiling is the same. <https://www.omegalearn.org/>

### Anki — free spaced-repetition flashcards

Free on desktop, web and Android; the iOS app is a one-time purchase that funds
the project. Open-source, no subscription. **Grade fit:** any. **Independent
use:** yes. **Interactivity:** active recall, the most evidence-backed study
technique there is. **Honest note:** Anki is a tool, not a curriculum — only as
good as the cards made for it, and making good cards is itself a skill. For a
homeschooler carrying a lot of material without a school's revision structure,
it is the cheapest way to stop forgetting things. <https://apps.ankiweb.net/>

## 2. Study-abroad opportunities

### Clark Scholars — genuinely contradictory, and now the reason is known

Sources **directly conflict**. One states the program is open to both US and
international students, with international applicants arranging their own visas
(no visa support given). Others state applicants must be US citizens or
permanent residents and that international students are *not* eligible.

> This cannot be resolved from secondary sources. **Email Texas Tech and ask** —
> one question, and it determines whether the program is worth any effort.

**Consistent across sources:** 7 weeks; rising seniors or recent graduates; at
least 17 by program start; graduating 2026 or 2027. No participation fees,
**$750 tax-free stipend**, room and board covered. **Timing:** 2026's deadline
was 16 February; applications for 2027 open in **fall — now**.
[Program details](https://www.depts.ttu.edu/clarkscholars/ProgramDetails.php)

RSI remains the nearest real deadline: applications open October, close
early-to-mid December.

> **A distinction worth learning**, from today's research: **citizenship and
> residence are different filters**, and US programs use both. RSI accepts
> international students living abroad. MITE requires US residence regardless of
> citizenship. Screen for both — *"open to international students" does not mean
> "open to students living outside the US."*

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Warmth of Other Suns: The Epic Story of America's Great Migration* — Isabel Wilkerson (2010)

On the NYT's 100 Best Books of the 21st Century, a list compiled from ballots by
more than 500 authors, booksellers, publishers and critics.

**The central argument.** Between 1915 and 1970 roughly **six million** Black
Americans left the South. Wilkerson's claim is that this was not demographic
drift but a **leaderless political act** — a mass refusal, carried out one
household at a time, by people fleeing a caste system. She argues it should be
read as an immigration story, with the South as the country departed.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Jim Crow was a caste system, not merely discrimination.** The distinction
   matters: a caste system assigns position at birth and polices it with
   violence. People were escaping, not protesting.
2. **Migrants behaved like immigrants.** They showed the selection effects
   common to immigrant populations — on several measures outperforming Black
   Americans born in the North. The mechanism is self-selection: leaving takes
   resources and nerve.
3. **Leaderless mass action can reshape a country.** No organization, no leader,
   no plan — six million individual decisions aggregating into the
   transformation of American cities, and a demonstration that structural change
   does not require central direction.
4. **Geography followed the railway lines.** Mississippi to Chicago, the
   Carolinas and Georgia up the eastern seaboard to New York, Louisiana and
   Texas west to California. The map of Black America was drawn by train routes.
5. **Method as argument.** Wilkerson tracks three lives across decades, drawn
   from more than 1,200 interviews. Deliberate: statistics describe a migration,
   but only individual lives show what leaving costs.

**Why it matters.** It rewrote the standard account of twentieth-century
American demography, and it is the clearest available case study in how millions
of uncoordinated private decisions become a historical force. For a high
schooler it is also unusually approachable — long, but narrative throughout,
closer to reading three biographies than a history monograph.

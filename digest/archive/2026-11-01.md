***Artificially Yours: Real Friendship in a World of Chatbots* — Valerie Tiberius.** Princeton University Press, 2026.

**The slot finally works as designed.** Slot 4 is *Nature* and *Science* editors' picks. For two weeks the slot was filled by substitution. **A date conflict, reported:** Kirkus gives a release date of 12 May 2026; an Amazon UK listing gives 14 July 2026 for the Princeton hardcover. Unresolved; not stated as known.

**The author.** Valerie Tiberius is professor of philosophy at the University of Minnesota, with a career on wellbeing and what makes a life go well.

**The method, which is why the book matters more than its subject.** Tiberius does not define friendship and then test chatbots against the definition. She **itemises the values of friendship** — the various reasons we actually value having friends — then asks, value by value, whether a chatbot or similar technology could fulfil each. A procedure rather than an opinion, reusable on any question of the form *"is this new thing really a case of that old thing?"* An 11-year-old can run it on something smaller.

**Three findings.**
1. There is **no perfect substitute** for human friends — not that chatbots are worthless, but that no arrangement of them delivers everything friendship delivers.
2. The specific thing missing is **mutual concern**. A friend has their own stake in how you are, which costs them something. Current technology does not, and the lack is structural rather than a matter of insufficient sophistication.
3. The middle position, the book's actual contribution: AI companions **may offer comfort or teach useful skills**, enriching and supplementing human friendship rather than replacing it. The University of Chicago's summary puts the limit precisely — chatbot relationships show *some* features of friendship, and even future advanced AI is "highly unlikely to be good in all the ways human friendships are."

**Reception, and the honest problem with it.** Kirkus: accessible and clear-eyed. Mark Bauerlein in *First Things*: "nicely suggestive." The reviewer in *The Conversation* says the approach is "so balanced and sensible that it is hard to imagine anyone having serious objections" — and that sentence, meant as praise, is **the sharpest criticism available: a book nobody could object to may not be claiming very much.** Blurbs from Ada Calhoun, Paul Bloom and Shannon Vallor, and blurbs are not reception. **No substantive scholarly review found**, so the above is the argument plus short notices.

**Suitability — and directly about an awkward thing.** Both children, for different reasons. The awkwardness is that this household receives a long daily letter written by an AI, which makes the book's question not academic. The 11-year-old is at the age where chatbot companionship is marketed hardest, and the structural point about mutual concern is exactly graspable. The elder child is the one for whom this file's top obstacle has been *teammates* — actual human beings, hard to find — which makes a book arguing that the convenient substitute is not a substitute worth reading rather than flattering. ## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *Your friend cares about you. What is that costing them — and would it still be friendship if it cost them nothing?*

**The move:** looking for what a relationship *demands* rather than what it provides. Most descriptions of friendship are lists of benefits. The move is to turn it round and ask what is being *given up*, because that is where the difference between a friend and a very good service lies.

**The trap:** concluding friendship is therefore a transaction with costs that ought to balance. The cynical exit, and it misses the point. The cost is not a price paid for a benefit received — it is the *evidence* that the other person's stake is real. A friend who would lose nothing if things went badly for you is not cheating you; they are simply not in the position the word describes.

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask it. Then the harder half: **can something care about you if it cannot be hurt?** Supply no answer. Both children can hold this one, and the 11-year-old may get further than the high-schooler, because the question does not reward sophistication.

**Relative of this week, and of today's book.** Tiberius's method applied to one value instead of all of them — take a thing you value, name what it actually requires, then ask what could supply it. Monday asked what procedure would make a *claim* believable; today asks what procedure would make a *relationship* real. Identical shape, opposite subject.

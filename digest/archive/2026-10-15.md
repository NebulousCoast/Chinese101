## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 5 → Booker. The six 2008 Best of the Booker finalists were the plan; five are now done and one remains after today (Gordimer's *The Conservationist*), after which this slot moves to Booker winners generally.

***The Siege of Krishnapur* — J.G. Farrell** (1973, winner of the Booker Prize that year; shortlisted again in 2008 for the Best of the Booker; second novel of the Empire Trilogy). Farrell drew on primary sources — diaries, letters, and the memoirs of the Rev. H.S. Polehampton among them — which is why the period detail bites.

**The central argument, because this novel has one.** Set during the 1857 uprising, in a fictional Indian station besieged for four months, it is a **comedy about the collapse of Victorian self-belief**. The British residents begin convinced they are the bearers of Progress, Science, Christianity and Taste. Farrell then takes away their food, their walls, their medicine and eventually their ornaments — which they fire from cannons at the besiegers — and watches which convictions survive contact with the facts. Almost none do. What makes it more than satire is that Farrell does not replace their certainty with his own.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The civilising mission, tested to destruction.** The Collector believes British rule is justified by what it brings. The siege is the experiment; the novel is the result.
2. **Science is not the same thing as being right.** The set piece is an argument between two doctors about what causes cholera — miasma or contagion — conducted with total confidence by both, one of them correct for the wrong reasons. Scholars read the cholera debate and the novel's phrenology as Farrell's instrument for satirising British claims to expertise and therefore to authority.
3. **Materialism as a faith.** The relics of the Great Exhibition litter the station, and the funniest and bleakest passages are about what happens to objects of "civilisation" when they are needed for something.
4. **The restricted role of women**, and the religious disputes of the age, treated as part of the same brittle structure.
5. **Honour and reputation** as the last things people give up — after bread, after dignity, after belief.

**The serious criticism, which belongs alongside it.** A *Stanford Daily* critic argues that the book's critique of colonialism stops at a generic sense that colonialism is wrong, and — the sharper point — that the Maharaja, the one character positioned to challenge the British idea of Progress from outside it, is written as shallow. A satire of the colonisers that still leaves the colonised without a voice. A real limitation, and the kind of thing worth noticing *in* a prize-winning book rather than after.

**Why it matters here.** Thirty-nine days of this digest have been about institutions deciding who may enter: Bebras needs a teacher, the olympiad needs an education bureau, Clark needed a passport. Farrell's subject is what institutions believe about themselves while they are deciding — and how much of that belief is load-bearing. The elder child is about to spend years being assessed by bodies very sure of their own standards. This is a funny, 300-page argument for taking those standards seriously and not reverently.

**Suitability:** the elder child, yes — comic, propulsive, not difficult prose, though the irony rewards a reader who already knows the history. **Not** for the 11-year-old: siege violence, disease and death, and the whole effect depends on hearing the irony.

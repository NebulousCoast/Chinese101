## 4. Book of the day

### *Le Petit Prince (The Little Prince)* — Antoine de Saint-Exupéry (1943)

Ranked **#4** on Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century, behind Camus, Proust and
Kafka.

Chosen for today on purpose: the only book on any of these lists that **a
fifth-grader and a high schooler could read the same week and discuss** — and,
underneath the drawings, about exactly what a month of deadline-tracking crowds
out.

> **Context that changes the book:** Saint-Exupéry was a working pilot. He wrote
> this in exile in New York during the war, and in 1944 took off on a
> reconnaissance flight over the Mediterranean and was never found. The
> preoccupation with what is lost is not a literary pose.

**The central argument.** Adults have traded a way of *seeing* for a way of
*measuring*, and the trade is a bad one. The narrator, a pilot downed in the
desert, meets a child from another planet who asks about things and not about
their quantities. The most quoted line — one sees clearly only with the heart;
what is essential is invisible to the eyes — is usually read as sentiment. It is
closer to an epistemological claim.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The critique of measurement.** Grown-ups ask how much a house costs rather
   than what the geraniums in its windows are like. They believe they have
   understood something when they have quantified it.
2. **Attachment is made, not discovered.** The fox's lesson: the rose is precious
   not because she is rare — there are millions — but because of the hours spent
   on her. **Value is created by investment.**
3. **Responsibility follows from attachment.** "You become responsible, forever,
   for what you have tamed." The consequence of caring is obligation, not
   enjoyment.
4. **Each planet is a diagnosis.** The king who commands only what would happen
   anyway; the vain man needing admirers; the businessman counting stars he cannot
   own; the lamplighter faithful to an order that no longer makes sense. A
   taxonomy of ways to waste a life.
5. **It refuses consolation.** The ending is genuinely sad and genuinely
   ambiguous. That is why it survives as literature rather than as a children's
   book.

**Why today:** this digest has spent a month on programmes, credentials, deadlines
and costs — which is what was asked for, and it has been useful. But the Prince's
objection to the grown-ups is that they can tell you the price of everything and
the character of nothing.

**Accessibility:** about ninety pages, readable in a sitting, better in French if
available. It rewards rereading at twenty and at forty, which almost nothing else
on these lists does.

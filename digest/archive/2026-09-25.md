## 1. Learning platforms

### Project Euler — free, and it describes its audience better than I could

**What it is:** an archive of **over 450** computational problems designed to be
solved by writing programs. Registration is free; there is nothing to buy. Each
problem is a mathematical question you cannot brute-force by hand, so you need
both the mathematics and the code. **Cost: $0.**

> Their own description of who it is for is uncannily precise for this family:
> **"students for whom the basic curriculum is not feeding their hunger to
> learn."** That is the premise of three weeks of these reviews, stated by a site
> that has run for two decades.

**Why it fits what 2026-09-24 established:** the computational-research path needs
mathematical reasoning and working code. AoPS builds the first, Exercism the
second, and **Project Euler is where they meet**. Their term for the experience is
"inductive chain learning": solving one problem exposes a concept that makes a
previously impossible problem tractable — exactly the difficulty curve a
self-directed student needs, and self-administering, with no instructor required
to know what comes next.

**Scale note:** over 150,000 registered members have solved at least 25 problems,
across 220 locations. A real community, not an abandoned archive.

**Limits:** no teaching, no explanations, no hand-holding. Solutions to nearly
every problem are published on GitHub and elsewhere, so it **only works if the
student chooses not to look** — the same discipline problem as Wolfram's
step-by-step. And it is narrow: number theory, combinatorics, algorithmic
thinking. No help with physics or writing. <https://projecteuler.net/>

## 4. Book of the day

### *Parable of the Sower* — Octavia E. Butler (1993)

Taught by Princeton's Judith Weisenfeld in an undergraduate seminar on Race and
Religion in America.

> **A fact worth sitting with first:** Butler published this in 1993 and set it in
> California between **2024 and 2027**. This digest is reading it in September
> 2026 — **inside the novel's own timeline**.

> ⚠️ **Content note:** a harsh book. Violence, sexual violence, and a world where
> children are not protected. Commonly taught in advanced high school, but Butler
> does not spare the reader.

**The central argument.** Civilisation does not end in an event; it erodes.
Butler's California is one of walled neighbourhoods, water sold by the litre,
privatised company towns, illiteracy, and drugs that make arson feel ecstatic. Her
teenage narrator Lauren Olamina responds by building a belief system — Earthseed —
whose first principle is that **God is Change**.

**Key ideas:**

1. **"God is Change."** A theology founded on the only reliable feature of
   reality. It offers no comfort and makes no promises; a discipline rather than a
   consolation.
2. **From God's victim to God's partner.** Earthseed's ethical core: since change
   is inevitable, the only question is whether you are shaped by it or help shape
   it. Genuinely original religious thinking — which is why a religion scholar
   teaches it.
3. **Collapse is gradual, local and bureaucratic.** Butler's realism is about
   mechanism: how water gets priced, how a wall fails, how a town becomes a
   company's property. The opposite of spectacle.
4. **Hyperempathy.** Lauren physically feels others' pain — treated simultaneously
   as a disability and as ethics made literal. What if you could not choose to
   look away?
5. **Literacy as survival technology.** Lauren reads, writes and keeps records in
   a society losing the capacity to do so. The novel *is* her journal, making the
   form an argument about who gets to author the account.

Earthseed's stated destiny — to "take root among the stars" — turns the book from
a survival story into something stranger: a religion with a space programme.

> **How to read it well, and this matters more than the prescience:** the lazy
> take is "Butler predicted everything." She did not predict. She **extrapolated**
> — took trends already visible in late-1980s America (homelessness, illiteracy,
> warming, privatisation, inequality) and refused to assume they would stop.
> **The transferable lesson is the method, not the accuracy.** Ask what is already
> happening, then decline to assume it plateaus.

That is also a research habit — the same move as the structural arguments in
Steinbeck (09-24) and Kingsolver (09-23): find the mechanism, follow it forward.

**Accessibility:** very readable — plain, urgent, first-person, short chapters.
Easier prose than almost anything on these lists, and harder content than most.

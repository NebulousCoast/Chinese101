## 1. Interactive learning platforms

**Beast Academy (AoPS)** — **$15.99/month, $99.99/year**, or ~**$160** for the
bundle adding a full level of printed books. Additional students **$10.99/month**.
No true free trial, but a free online demo exists.
<https://beastacademy.com/online/enroll>

**Age fit, the part that matters:** levels are set by age, and **Level 5 is ages
10–13** — the 11-year-old lands squarely inside it. Some third-party reviews
describe it as "grades 2–5," narrower than the official ages 6–13 range; if she is
already past level 5 material this is the wrong product and the answer is AoPS
Online proper.

**What it actually is:** a comic-book-led problem-solving curriculum, not a drill
app. Problems are genuinely hard and the pedagogy is AoPS's — puzzle first,
technique second. Same house as the olympiad training behind section 5.

*Limit:* it tops out. Level 5 is the end, so a one- or two-year product for an
11-year-old, not a multi-year home. At $99.99/year the real cost is her time
rather than the money — the right way round.

> **Worth saying plainly:** Beast Academy at $100/year is a better purchase for
> this household right now than yesterday's **$2,110** AMC 8 course, which
> prepares for an exam she still has **no confirmed way to sit**.

**freeCodeCamp** — **free**, and the direct answer to yesterday's Codecademy
problem. Donation-funded nonprofit; entire curriculum, all projects and every
certification free, with **no paywall, no premium tier, no certificate fee**. Each
certification is earned by completing **five required projects** — exactly what
Codecademy's free tier withholds.

**2026 change:** a new "Certified Full Stack Developer" path is replacing the
separate tracks — six blocks of roughly 300 hours each, every block with its own
projects and exam. **Legacy certifications remain available through 31 December
2027**, so no rush but an eventual migration.

> **Reported curriculum size conflicts badly:** one source says **3,000 hours**,
> another ~**1,200**, another **over 12,000**. The 3,000 figure is consistent with
> the twelve-certification count most sources give, so treat the bigger numbers
> sceptically. Each certification is quoted at ~300 hours, which one source
> sensibly calls a *design target* rather than a prediction.

> **Limit, stated honestly:** recognition is mixed and no authoritative data on
> employer acceptance was found, so **do not treat the certificate as a
> credential**. The value is the **five projects per certification** — things that
> exist, that can be linked to, that someone can look at. Same logic as Exercism's
> free mentoring and the GitHub-repository argument this digest has made all
> month: **for the elder child, artefacts beat certificates.**

*Grade fit:* the elder child primarily. A motivated 11-year-old could start the
responsive-web-design material, but the pacing assumes an older reader.

## 4. Book of the day

### *Pachinko* — Min Jin Lee (2017)

On the NYT list (no rank asserted, none verified). National Book Award finalist.

Chosen because **it is the serious version of the thing this digest says most
often.** Every day some variant of "citizenship and residence are different
filters, and programmes use both" gets written here. That sentence is bureaucratic
and small. *Pachinko* is what it looks like when the same distinction governs an
entire life, across four generations.

**The central argument.** A Korean family in twentieth-century Japan — born there,
raised there, speaking the language natively, not citizens and unable to become so
on the terms available. Lee's claim is not that prejudice is bad; too easy. It is
that **a legal category, maintained patiently over decades, becomes the shape of a
family's interior life**: what work is available, who may marry whom, what a child
is told, what is hidden. History does not happen to these people as an event. It
happens as paperwork, repeated.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Nationality is not identity, and the gap is where the damage lives.** Korean
   by law, Japanese by life, belonging nowhere officially. The opening line —
   history has failed us, but no matter — sets the terms: people continuing anyway.
2. **Respectability is not protection.** Each generation tries religion, hard
   work, education, assimilation, money, passing. None dissolves the category. A
   bleak finding Lee does not soften.
3. **Pachinko itself is the metaphor, and it is exact.** Parlours were one of the
   few businesses open to Koreans in Japan, and the game is rigged — pins adjusted
   nightly, invisible to the player, skill mattering at the margins while the
   house decides the distribution. **A life inside a system calibrated against
   you, where effort is real but bounded.**
4. **Women carry the book.** Sunja's choices, and the costs she absorbs so her
   sons do not have to, are the spine.
5. **Generations diverge, and the novel declines to say which was right.** The
   grandchild who assimilates and the one who refuses are both legible, both
   damaged, neither held up as the answer.

> **Difficult?** Not stylistically — clear prose, propulsive narrative, hence the
> large readership. Difficult in content: poverty, suicide, sexual coercion,
> sustained discrimination, handled seriously rather than luridly. Some critics
> find the later generations thinner, and the sweep occasionally flattens
> characters into their eras. Four hundred-odd pages that read fast.

> **Why it matters for this family specifically — put carefully.** Their situation
> is *not* this. A visiting scholar's household navigating ESTA rules and camp
> residency filters is dealing with inconvenience, not oppression, and a visa
> question should not be flattered by comparison to colonial subjecthood. **But**
> the elder child is about to spend a season being sorted by exactly this kind of
> machinery — eligible here, not there, by passport and address rather than by
> ability — and this is the book that takes that machinery seriously. It is also a
> Korean-Japanese story read from Taiwan, with resonances this family will hear
> better than the digest can.

**Suitability:** the elder child, yes — one of the most readable serious novels on
any of these lists. **Not for the 11-year-old**; the content, not the difficulty,
is the barrier.

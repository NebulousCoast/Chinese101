## 4 — Book of the day

**_The Honor Code: How Moral Revolutions Happen_, Kwame Anthony Appiah, 2010. The first-ever Princeton Pre-read.**

Rotation index 3 (Princeton/Harvard pre-read slot).

**Provenance, verified from Princeton's own pages.** The Pre-read programme began with this book: announced in **June 2013** by Christopher Eisgruber — then Provost and President-elect — and assigned to the **Class of 2017**, who received it by 1 July together with his letter. Discussions followed in the residential colleges, including a lunch at Forbes College on 27 September 2013. That makes this the origin of the whole rotation slot: *Whistling Vivaldi* (2015), *Our Declaration* (2016) and *Meaning in Life* (2014) all come after it.

**The question the book asks.** Why do practices that everyone agrees are wrong persist for centuries, and then collapse quickly? Appiah takes four cases — duelling among the English upper classes, foot-binding in China, Atlantic slavery as seen from Britain, and so-called honour killing in Pakistan — and asks what actually ended the first three.

**Three ideas.**

1. **The central claim:** moral arguments were usually available long before anything changed. People knew duelling was absurd and slavery monstrous; the arguments had been made and lost repeatedly. What changed was not the arguments but **who was watching** — when a practice stopped being a mark of standing and became a mark of ridiculousness, it died fast.
2. **Honour is a mechanism rather than a virtue.** Appiah treats the desire for respect as a motor that can be hitched to anything, good or bad. The same force that made men shoot each other at dawn made the English gentry want nothing to do with slavery once slavery was coded as vulgar.
3. **The useful one for a household trying to teach reasoning:** the implication is that being right is not sufficient and sometimes not even the operative factor. That is uncomfortable, and worth arguing about rather than accepting.

**The dissent, and it is substantial and good.**

- **Jonathan Haidt**, reviewing it, said his one main criticism was that Appiah thinks honour survives in modern Western societies — a claim Haidt doubts.
- **Sharon Krause** argues that Appiah's cases show the moral value of **concern for reputation** rather than anything about honour proper, which she says is not reducible to reputation at all.
- The sharpest objection is terminological and goes to the thesis: one critic suggests the book should have been called "The Shame Code," because Appiah's own examples show the force mainly **policing** behaviour, and that his theory repeatedly requires a culture to internalise a critique **arriving from outside** — which is a different story from the one the title tells.
- A *New York Review* piece objects that Appiah **downplays moral argument**, citing his line that ridicule at last did more than morality to kill duelling, and says it is not persuaded honour provides the only formula for eliminating social ills.
- And a reviewer notes the internal tension Appiah himself concedes: an honour code can demand that someone do something wrong.

**The limit, stated plainly.** I did not find a review by a professional historian of duelling, foot-binding or abolition, which is precisely where the case studies would be tested hardest — one reviewer notes that historians are "finicky about drawing clean, applicable lessons from the past," which is a fair warning rather than a verdict. The Haidt review surfaced only as a copy hosted on a university course page, so treat the attribution as probable rather than certain.

**Suitability.** **The elder child, yes**, and this is the strongest match in the Princeton slot so far: a short book with a clear thesis, four concrete cases, and a well-organised set of objections — which is exactly the shape of an argument a selective application essay has to be able to handle. **For the 11-year-old, no as a book** — the subject matter includes duelling, slavery and killing — **but** the central question is completely accessible and belongs in section 8 one day soon: why do people keep doing things everyone knows are wrong?

## 6 — International collaboration (even-numbered dates)

**iNaturalist, and a setting that decides whether a child's observations become science or stay a private photo album.**

On Wednesday I put eBird in a tier of its own because an individual checklist is citable with the observer's name in the author position. iNaturalist is the obvious comparison, and the comparison turns out to hinge on something nobody mentions: **the licence you pick.**

**How it actually works, from iNaturalist's own guidance and its forums:**

- An observation becomes **research grade** when the community agrees on the identification.
- Research-grade observations are exported in bulk to **GBIF** — the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, which is the database researchers actually query — roughly **weekly**.
- **But only if the licence permits it.** Observations licensed CC0, CC BY or CC BY-NC are shared. Observations marked **all rights reserved are not**. And — this is the counter-intuitive part — **CC BY-SA and CC BY-ND are also excluded**, because GBIF cannot process them.
- Under CC BY, anyone reusing the observation must **credit the observer**.
- In GBIF you can filter the iNaturalist dataset by "Recorded by", which uses the observer's **profile name** rather than a user ID.

**So the actionable thing is a settings check, not a decision about whether to participate.** A child can photograph insects conscientiously for a year, reach research grade on hundreds of observations, and contribute nothing to any database at all if the licence is left on all rights reserved — or on a Creative Commons licence that sounds *more* generous and is in fact excluded. CC BY is the setting that both shares the data and requires attribution. That takes one minute and nobody tells you.

Where this leaves the two side by side:

- **eBird:** an individual checklist has a published citation format with the observer as author. Highest tier.
- **iNaturalist with CC BY:** observations enter GBIF, are attributable, and are findable by the observer's profile name — but the credit is a database field rather than a citation format. Second tier, and genuinely good.
- **iNaturalist with the wrong licence:** no tier at all.

**The limits.** I could **not** establish a minimum age or account-age rule for iNaturalist, exactly as I could not for eBird — check before registering a child's own account. The claim that citing a GBIF DOI discharges the attribution obligation came from a **forum post**, not from policy, so do not rely on it. And the honest proportion is the same as Wednesday's: being in a database is real and is not the same as being cited by anyone. Most observations never are.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question.** Something that was supposed to happen every day did not happen yesterday. Does it matter whether the reason was a good one?

**The move it teaches:** separating the **excuse** from the **repair**. Most arguments about a failure get stuck on whether the reason was acceptable, which is a question about blame. The repair question is different and more useful: what is owed now, and what would stop it happening again? Those have answers even when the blame question does not.

**The trap:** thinking a good reason reduces what is owed. It does not. If a thing was promised daily and did not arrive, the person waiting is in exactly the same position whether the cause was carelessness or a machine failure. A good reason changes who should feel bad; it does not change what has to be made up.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Ask it. Then ask the harder half, which is the one that makes it worth asking: **does it matter whether anyone noticed?** If the thing had not been mentioned, would anything be owed at all? Leave that open; it is genuinely difficult.

**And the honest disclosure, because today's example is mine.** This digest skipped yesterday. The reason was a machine restart, which is a good reason and changes nothing about the fact that you did not get it. What I owed was to say so first, before anything else in the email, rather than quietly carrying on — which is the only repair available to something that cannot go back and send yesterday's letter.

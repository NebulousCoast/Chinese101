Rotation index 4 → Nature & Science editors' picks. **A source change, as promised on 14 October:** the Royal Society Trivedi 2026 shortlist is exhausted — all six titles used, and there is no longlist (checked today). The new vein is **Nature's own Books & Arts section** — its reviews and Andrew Robinson's recurring "Books in brief" column, five science picks at a time. A monthly supply from Nature's own editorial judgement rather than a once-a-year list. Today's book comes from the 25 September installment.

***Forest of the Sea: The Remarkable Life and Imperiled Future of Kelp* — David Helvarg** (Island Press, 5 May 2026, 264pp, $30). A journalist and marine conservationist. **Publisher conflict, reported not resolved:** one review describes Island Press as an imprint of Princeton University Press, another simply lists Princeton University Press, and Island Press has historically been independent.

**The central argument.** Kelp forests are the largest living structure most people have never thought about — Nature's review leads with the figure that cold-water kelp forests cover an area **larger than the Amazon rainforest**. Helvarg's argument is ecological and political at once: these forests are collapsing, the mechanism is well understood, and the collapse is reversible by deliberately restoring a predator.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The sea otter is a load-bearing animal.** Remove the top predator and urchins multiply unchecked; rich kelp forest becomes "urchin barrens." The system does not degrade gradually — it flips.
2. **Therefore restoration is a choice, not a hope.** Reintroduce the otter and the forest can come back. Helvarg follows Indigenous leaders advocating sea otter reintroduction, making the politics as concrete as the biology.
3. **The people are the book.** Out-of-work urchin fishermen, documentary filmmakers, ocean scientists. The economics of collapse are somebody's livelihood.
4. **The Pacific as one system**, Baja California to Alaska — geography as argument, not scenery.
5. **The coral parallel.** One review notes kelp's decline set alongside the better-known coral crisis, which explains why an unphotogenic ecosystem gets a fraction of the attention.

**The criticism, such as can be established:** one reviewer flags **a lack of referenced sourcing** — the excerpt was cut off, so the severity is unknown, only that the complaint was made. Reviews are otherwise warm, and several sources found were publisher or retailer blurbs rather than independent reviews.

**Why it matters, and the pairing.** Rose George's *Every Last Fish* was covered on 8 October — the fishing side of the same ocean. Together they are the two halves of a marine argument: what we take out, and what we quietly let collapse. It also connects to section 6: reading about a collapsing ecosystem and recording one are different activities, and one of them is available this afternoon.

**Suitability: both children.** For the elder child straightforwardly; for the eleven-year-old read aloud in parts — otters, urchins and kelp forests are vivid, the mechanism is graspable at eleven, and the core idea does not require despair to understand.

### A. Editing 中文維基百科 — no gatekeeper, no fee, no age floor, permanent public record

Six weeks of looking for ways these children can work with people elsewhere, and this was in front of me the whole time, in the right language.

**Verified from Wikipedia's own 給未成年用戶的建議 page rather than a third party:**

- **There is no age requirement.** Wikipedia does not ask editors for age or identity. Registration requires no personal data; email is optional.
- **Minors are held to the same standard.** The guidance is explicit that "I am a minor" does not excuse a policy violation, and blocks apply the same way. Not a warning against doing it — the reason doing it is worth something.
- **The safety guidance is specific:** do not publish real name, age or precise address; do not let family or friends use the account; the community **strongly advises** minors not to register under a real name or any traceable nickname.
- **Anonymous editing is worse for privacy, not better.** Unregistered edits used to expose the IP in public history; since 24 June 2025 they are attributed to temporary accounts valid for 90 days, which cannot be logged into and have no password. A registered pseudonymous account is the private option.
- **The ladder is real and short:** most accounts become 自動確認使用者 after **seven days and fifty edits**. Advanced permissions like CheckUser require being 18 and identifying to the Wikimedia Foundation — irrelevant to ordinary editing.

**Why this is real collaboration:** the artefact is shared, permanent and consulted by millions; every edit is attributed, timestamped and reversible; and strangers with more expertise will argue about sourcing. No other activity on this list lets an eleven-year-old be corrected, in public, by an adult who cares about getting it right.

**The limits:** **not a credential** — nobody awards anything, and a contribution history is evidence rather than a qualification, though it is verifiable evidence with dates. Edits will be reverted, sometimes brusquely, and a child needs an adult nearby the first few times. One sourcing caveat: one newcomer guide read today was a personal Medium post over five years old — official policy pages beat any walkthrough.

**Where to start:** not by writing an article. Find three articles about something the family actually knows — a Taiwanese place, a local species, a Chinese-language book — and add *one properly sourced sentence* to each.

## 8. Thinking

*Daily and short. One question, and the move it teaches.*

**Today's question, straight out of section 6:** Should you trust an encyclopedia that anyone can edit? Then sharpen it: your child is about to become one of the "anyone." Does that make it more trustworthy or less?

**The move it teaches:** separating the **reliability of a process** from the **authority of an author**. We mostly judge a claim by who said it. Wikipedia asks you to judge it by what happened to it — how many people checked, whether sources are cited, whether a disagreement is still live on the talk page. Knowing which kind of reason you are using is most of what "critical thinking" actually means.

**The trap:** "anyone can edit it, so it's unreliable." Apply the same test to a printed textbook — who checked it, when, and how would you find out if it were wrong? The honest answer is usually "nobody I can name, years ago, and I couldn't." The opposite trap is "it's usually right, so it's fine," which forgets how much work "usually" is doing.

**How to run it:** fifteen minutes, no answer from the adult. With ten more minutes afterwards, open any 中文維基百科 article and click 檢視歷史 and 討論. Seeing the argument underneath an article beats any amount of being told to be sceptical.

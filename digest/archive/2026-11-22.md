## 4 — Book of the day

### The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao
Junot Díaz · 2007 · Pulitzer Prize for Fiction 2008 · National Book Critics Circle Award

**Number 11** on the New York Times' 100 Best Books of the 21st Century, from a copy of the list that *states* the number; the Times' own list page has never loaded from this container, so the rank is secondary-sourced but stated rather than inferred from a list's order.

**The record first**, because this is an author-with-a-problem case and the practice here is to state it before the book rather than after.

In 2018 the writer Zinzi Clemmons said publicly that Díaz had forcibly kissed her years earlier, and other women described misogynistic behaviour. Díaz denied it — "I did not kiss anyone." **MIT** investigated for a month and said it had not found or received information that would lead it to restrict him as a faculty member. The **Pulitzer board**, whose chairmanship he gave up, ran a five-month independent review involving dozens of interviews and hundreds of pages of documents and did not find evidence warranting his removal; he served out his term. **Boston Review** kept him as fiction editor and three of its editors resigned in protest.

Those are institutional outcomes about employment and board membership. Neither review published a finding on the specific incident, and **"not removed" is not the same as "did not happen"** — a distinction worth holding onto, and today's thinking question.

**The argument.** The novel is the story of an overweight Dominican-American science-fiction obsessive in New Jersey who wants to be loved and is not, told by a narrator, Yunior, who is almost his opposite. But the book's real claim is historical: that the family's misfortunes are *not bad luck* but the long reach of Rafael Trujillo's dictatorship, and that this reach is inherited. The family calls it **fukú** — a curse. The book takes the idea seriously as a way of describing causation across generations without pretending it is supernatural.

**Three ideas worth taking out of it.** First, **the footnotes**, which are the formal invention: Dominican history is delivered in long notes beneath the novel, several characters are developed there rather than in the main text, and academic readings argue the notes exist to refuse the usual history-of-the-dictator and to put a question mark over who gets to narrate a nation. Second, **untranslated Spanish** — Díaz does not gloss it. Readers split hard on this; some say context carries it and some find it wearing, and either way the reader is placed in the position of someone not quite inside the language, which is the condition the book is about. Third, **nerd culture as a real idiom**: Tolkien, comics and role-playing games are not decoration but the vocabulary Oscar genuinely thinks in.

**The dissent, and here it is substantial rather than thin.** The strongest objection is about women and masculinity: readers and critics argue the book stages misogyny through Yunior's voice without ever landing a judgement on it, and that the women of the Cabral family are seen mostly through what is done to them. A review of the **2026 Goodman Theatre stage adaptation** makes the same complaint against the production — that it treats Oscar's boundary-crossing as endearing and shortchanges its own women. Second, structural: it began as a short piece and was extended, and some readers find the family-history chapters long and the themes restated. Third: that giving the book's distinctive voice to several narrators dilutes it.

*Honest limit on all of that:* I could not reach the major newspaper reviews from 2007–08, so the criticism above is academic essays plus consistent reader and blog criticism — weaker evidence than a named critic.

> **For the 11-year-old: no.** Sexual content, heavy profanity, and the torture and sexual violence of the Trujillo years are on the page. This is a book for the elder child, and even there the subject matter is adult.

**Open-source contribution, and the last open limit is now closed with numbers.** On 18 November this item was recorded with an admitted gap: how beginner-labelling actually works in practice, and whether those labels are a live queue or a graveyard. Measured this morning off **GitHub's own search API** rather than from memory:

| Label | Open issues |
|---|---|
| `"good first issue"` | **476,769** |
| `"help wanted"` | **556,090** |
| `"good-first-issue"` (hyphenated) | 9,064 |
| `"beginner-friendly"` | 3,274 |

**Two usable conclusions.** First, **search the spaced three-word label.** "good first issue" is GitHub's own default label text, and the hyphenated variant finds about **two per cent** of the pool — a child searching the wrong spelling would conclude the opportunity barely exists. Second, the queue is live, not abandoned: of those issues, **352,870 are open and unassigned**, and **31,748 were opened in the last thirty days.** Roughly thirty thousand fresh unclaimed beginner issues a month.

> **The limit, and it is the real one.** A label means a maintainer *tagged* an issue, not that they will review a stranger's pull request promptly or kindly. The counts cannot measure responsiveness — and responsiveness decides whether a first contribution becomes an artefact or a dead branch. **Which is exactly why the UT department pointer in section 3 is worth asking for:** a person who already contributes knows which maintainers answer, and no amount of searching reveals that.

**Why this sits at the top of the credit tier:** a merged pull request is a permanent, dated, publicly attributable change to something other people use, with the contributor's name on it and the argument about whether to accept it preserved in public underneath. The skill is not programming — it is reading someone else's codebase and contributing guidelines well enough to make a change they will accept. **Elder child only:** GitHub's own terms set **13** as the minimum for a personal account, and add that *your country's minimum may be older*, so Taiwan's figure is unestablished and should be checked when the 11-year-old turns 13 rather than assumed from the American number. Honest proportion: one merged documentation fix is real and is not an achievement; **a dozen accepted changes to one project over three months** shows persistence and the ability to take review.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, and it comes out of today's book rather than out of its story.** When an investigation looks into something and finds nothing, what has it found?

**The move it teaches:** distinguishing *this did not happen* from *this was not established*. They are reported in the same words — cleared, no evidence found, no action taken — and they are different claims. The second one is about the investigation as much as about the event.

**The trap, and there are two, pulling opposite ways.** One is treating "nothing found" as proof that nothing happened. The other is treating it as proof of a cover-up. Both skip the only question that would actually help: **what would this investigation have been able to detect?** Who could it compel to speak, what records could it read, and what kind of event would leave no trace in either? An inquiry that could not have found the thing tells you almost nothing when it does not find it.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Use something small and domestic. Something has gone missing in the house; somebody asks everyone and nobody admits anything. Before arguing about who did it, write down what that asking-around could *possibly* have discovered, and what it could never have discovered however honestly it was done. Then ask whether a better inquiry was available, and what it would have cost.

**Relative of yesterday's, directly.** Yesterday asked how you tell a remedy that worked from getting better anyway — how you know, from inside a single case, what your evidence cannot show you. Today is the same question pointed at a *procedure* instead of a case: a formal inquiry is still a method, and a method's blind spots do not announce themselves in its conclusion.

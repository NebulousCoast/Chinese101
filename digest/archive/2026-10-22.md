***Remarkably Bright Creatures* — Shelby Van Pelt** (2022). Gates's holiday fiction pick, reported with his own explanation: "I don't read fiction often, but when I do, I want to read about interesting characters who help me see the world in a new way."

**The central argument, if a novel can be said to have one.** Tova Sullivan, a widow in her seventies, cleans an aquarium at night in a small Washington town. Her son disappeared thirty years ago and was never found. Marcellus is a giant Pacific octopus nearing the end of his life, extremely intelligent, extremely bored, and — the book's one great formal gamble — **a narrator**. He has worked out what happened to Tova's son. He cannot tell her in words.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Grief as routine rather than drama.** Reviewers note Tova's grief is quiet and practical — it shows up in habit and restraint rather than in scenes.
2. **The non-human mind, taken seriously.** Marcellus is confined, observant, contemptuous of human slowness. Reviewers agree he could easily have been a gimmick and is not.
3. **Knowing something and being unable to say it.** The plot engine is an information problem across a species barrier — the week's institutional question in a kinder register.
4. **Mortality without sentimentality.** Marcellus is counting down; he knows it; the book does not rescue him.
5. **Small-town economics**, quietly — the aquarium, the shift work, options limited by money rather than will.

**The criticism, real and consistent.** **Pacing** is the common complaint — one reviewer says more than halfway through nothing significant had happened. Another wishes the non-octopus sections were tighter and argues Van Pelt is stronger in first person. Cameron is widely disliked; one review calls him an insufferable brat with a decent arc. **None of the sources were major-outlet reviews** — book blogs, Substacks, reader reviews, plus a publisher blurb from *Elle* — so the reception is the internet's rather than the critics'.

**Why it matters.** Three days of kelp forests, a conserved farm with a body on it, and now an octopus who understands more than anyone will credit him with. After a week of heavy books this is the pairing for the eleven-year-old, and the only book in three weeks that is primarily a pleasure.

**Suitability:** the elder child easily. For the **eleven-year-old**: probably yes, with one warning — a child's death is the fact the whole book is built on, handled gently and without horror but never minimised. Read the first Marcellus chapter and decide; if it fits, it is a read-aloud.

## 8. Thinking

*Daily and short. One question, and the move it teaches.*

**Today's question, out of today's novel:** Marcellus the octopus knows what happened to Tova's son and cannot tell her. Does he *know* it, really — if he can never say it to anyone, and nobody will ever check?

**The move it teaches:** separating *knowing* from *being able to show*. Most of what we call knowledge is tangled with the ability to communicate and defend it. Pull those apart and the question gets genuinely hard: is a true belief, held by someone who cannot justify it to anyone, knowledge? Philosophers have argued about this for a very long time and have not finished.

**The trap:** "of course he knows it, he saw it." Then ask whether the eleven-year-old knows the capital of a country they have only seen on a map, and how they would distinguish knowing it from remembering it correctly by luck. The opposite trap is "nothing counts as knowledge unless you can prove it," which throws away almost everything anyone knows about their own childhood.

**How to run it:** fifteen minutes, no answer from the adult. Then one extra turn, because it connects to the week: can an *institution* know something? Can an aquarium know that Marcellus is bored? Can an education bureau know that your child exists?

> **The distinction worth holding onto:** competitions tell you how the child

## 4. Book of the day

### *The New Jim Crow: Mass Incarceration in the Age of Colorblindness* — Michelle Alexander (2010)

On the NYT's 100 Best Books of the 21st Century.

**The central argument.** American racial control has not ended but changed form
— from **exploitation** under slavery, to **subordination** under Jim Crow, to
**marginalization** under mass incarceration. Alexander's claim is that the war
on drugs produced a system operating as a racial caste system while remaining
*formally colorblind*, because a criminal record legally licenses the same
exclusions Jim Crow enforced directly: voting, juries, housing, employment.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Formal neutrality can produce systematic outcomes.** A law that mentions no
   race can still sort by race through enforcement discretion. The transferable
   idea, reaching far beyond American criminal justice.
2. **The felony label does the work.** Once convicted, exclusions attach
   automatically and lawfully — no discriminatory intent required at any single
   step.
3. **Colorblindness as mechanism, not remedy.** The insistence that the system
   does not see race is precisely what makes the pattern hard to name.
4. **Caste as the right frame.** Connects to Wilkerson's *Warmth of Other Suns*
   (2026-09-11) — she described what people fled; Alexander argues about what
   replaced it.

> **Read it with the counter-argument, which is unusually good.** James Forman
> Jr's *Locking Up Our Own* (and his paper "Racial Critiques of Mass
> Incarceration: Beyond the New Jim Crow") argues the analogy obscures as much
> as it reveals: Black professional and political elites actively supported the
> tough-on-crime turn; the Jim Crow framing makes most prisoners look like drug
> offenders when violent crime and its victims barely appear; and white
> prisoners become invisible.

Forman's deeper move is more interesting than a rebuttal. He argues American
racism **narrowed the options** available to Black citizens and officials — they
campaigned for jobs, schools and housing *alongside* tough-on-crime laws, and as
social spending collapsed under Reagan they received only the punishment half of
what they asked for. A subtler and sadder mechanism than Alexander's, and one
that exonerates nothing.

**Why it matters for a teenager, in Taiwan or anywhere:** a live scholarly
argument between two serious people who largely share a goal and disagree about
the mechanism. Reading only Alexander gives a thesis; reading both demonstrates
**how evidence gets weighed** — the more valuable education, and the fourth time
this rotation has paired a book with its best critic, deliberately.

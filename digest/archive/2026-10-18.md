## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 2 → Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century (a 1999 reader poll — see above).

***La Condition humaine* (Man's Fate) — André Malraux** (1933). **Number 5** on the Le Monde poll, and winner of the **Prix Goncourt** in 1933 — a jury award, a different kind of evidence from a popularity poll. The middle book of Malraux's loose trilogy on revolution in Asia, with *Les Conquérants* and *La Voie Royale*.

**The setting, and why it suits this household.** Shanghai, 1927: the failed communist insurrection and the massacre that followed. A European novel by a Frenchman about Chinese revolutionaries — one of the most widely read French novels about events on this side of the world. Reading it from Taiwan is a different experience from reading it in Paris, and the elder child is positioned to notice what Malraux gets wrong as well as right.

**The central argument.** The French title is the real one: *the human condition*. Malraux's subject is not the politics of 1927 but what people do about the fact that they will die and that history does not care about them. His answer — an argument, not a mood — is that meaning is produced by **action taken with others**, and that solidarity is the only thing that converts a meaningless death into a chosen one. The novel exists to test that claim under the worst conditions it could find.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Dignity is the real stake, not victory.** The characters lose. The question is what was nonetheless achieved, and whether that answer survives honest examination.
2. **Action against absurdity.** Written a decade before Camus's *L'Étranger* (covered 12 September) and arguing a harder line: the absurd is not to be contemplated but worked against, in company.
3. **Solidarity versus solitude.** Every major character is tested on whether he can actually reach another person. The famous scene — a man giving away his cyanide — is the thesis compressed into one gesture.
4. **The revolution betrays its own.** The insurgents are undone partly by the distant organisation supposed to be their ally. Malraux, a committed man, does not spare the committed.
5. **Terror as a moral question**, not a tactic. The book opens with an assassination and refuses to make it either heroic or simply monstrous.

**What is not oversold:** no substantial scholarly criticism surfaced in today's searches, and the thematic descriptions found were thin. The five points above are a reading of a very well-established novel rather than a summary of the critical literature, and the email says so. There is also a live debate about Malraux's romanticism and about a European writing Chinese revolutionaries.

**Suitability:** the elder child, with a warning — torture, execution and suicide, handled seriously rather than luridly, and dense 1930s French prose. The best pairing for today's section 8 question. **Not** for the eleven-year-old.

### A. iNaturalist and Seek — citizen science whose contributions are attributable

More than could be established for Zooniverse.

**How it works, and what makes it collaboration rather than data entry:** an observation is posted, and other people — often specialists — propose identifications. When **more than two-thirds** of the suggestions agree on a species and the observation passes iNaturalist's Data Quality Assessment, it becomes **research grade** and is exported to **GBIF**, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, the database professional biodiversity science runs on. https://www.inaturalist.org/

**The numbers, with caveats.** GBIF's own metrics page for the iNaturalist research-grade dataset currently shows **8,149 citations** against **169,720,759 occurrences**. An older community estimate put peer-reviewed publications at 2,202 or more, its author warning it came from semi-automated processing of only six publication indices. A *BioScience* article estimates roughly seven papers a day globally use GBIF data of all kinds. So: thousands of citing works, no exact figure, and the GBIF number may count dataset citations rather than unique papers. **Do not round this to "your child's observations will be in scientific papers"** — the honest sentence is that the dataset they contribute to is cited thousands of times.

**The under-13 route.** **Seek requires no account of any kind**; records are stored on the device by default, and precise location is **not** sent anywhere unless someone signs in to an iNaturalist account and submits. Posting through to iNaturalist is optional and, for a child, needs parental consent. **The exact age cutoff conflicts in iNaturalist's own materials** — the Seek privacy policy frames its protections around users "12 and younger," while the posting announcement refers to "under 13." Read the privacy policy, not a review.

**Two more limits.** *Licence:* only CC0, CC-BY or CC-BY-NC observations are exported to GBIF — iNaturalist's own figures show 81.4M research grade and 99.6M under an exporting licence, and users report GBIF running tens of millions behind the site total. Set the licence deliberately. And: **this is not a credential.** It is a named, licensed, citable data contribution — a real thing and a different thing.

## 8. Thinking

*Daily and short. One question, and the move it teaches.*

**Today's question:** Is it worse to *do* something bad, or to *let* something bad happen when you could easily have stopped it? Push on it with a case: you knock a stranger's bicycle into the canal, versus you watch a stranger's bicycle rolling towards the canal and do nothing. Same bicycle, same canal, same outcome. Same wrong?

**The move it teaches:** the distinction between **acts and omissions** — and whether it is a real moral difference or just a difference in how it feels to be the person involved. Almost every serious argument about responsibility, from charity to climate to medicine, is this question wearing other clothes.

**The trap:** "they're exactly the same really." That might be right, but it is a conclusion, not an observation — and whoever says it has to accept where it leads, which is that doing nothing all day is a long series of wrongs. Make them say whether they believe that. The opposite trap is "obviously doing it is worse," which has to explain why the bicycle's owner should care about your inner state.

**How to run it:** fifteen minutes, no answer from the adult. The eleven-year-old will have strong intuitions and be able to defend them; the elder child has just read Malraux, whose whole book is about people choosing to act when standing still was available. Ask each of them for the strongest version of the view they do *not* hold.

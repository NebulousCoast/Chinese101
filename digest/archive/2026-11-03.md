## 1 · Interactive and bring-your-own-device learning

Re-verifying — connecting this section to the thing with a real clock on it.

**The question nobody had asked:** with RSI due in 38 days and **two recommendations** required, is there anything in the free stack that produces something a recommender could point at?

Yes, and it is why CS50x keeps topping this section. **CS50x requires a final project** — not a quiz, not a certificate of completion, but a thing the student designed, built and has to explain. Problem sets are auto-graded; the project is the student's own and lives in a repository anyone can open.

**Why that matters for a 非學校型態實驗教育 applicant.** The hard part of the RSI recommendations is not finding two adults who think well of the child; it is that *a recommender needs something to describe*. A teacher in a school has a classroom's worth of observation; someone writing for a homeschooled applicant often has only impressions — which produce the letter that reads as warm and says nothing. **A finished, public, explainable project changes what a letter can contain:** a specific object, a set of decisions, something the student *did* rather than *is*. Thirty-eight days is tight for the whole course but not for a project, and a half-finished course with a finished project is worth more here than a complete course with none.

**Limits.** CS50x's certificate is free but **not** a credential, and no admissions office treats it as one — the project is the asset, not the paper. And this does not solve the recommender problem; it only makes the recommender's job possible. Who those two people are remains the family's question, and the UT department ask is still the best lead.

A separate aggregated ranking at thegreatestbooks.org puts *Gilead* at 475, which is an average *across many lists*, not the Times' position.

**What the book is.** John Ames, a Congregationalist minister in Gilead, Iowa, seventy-six and dying of heart disease, writes a letter to his seven-year-old son, who will read it as an adult. That is the entire form: a long letter from a father who knows he will not be there, to a child he is trying to furnish for a life he will not see. *(The email noted plainly that I am aware of what that describes.)*

**Three ideas.**
1. **The form is the argument.** Ames cannot know which parts will matter, so he writes down everything — theology, grievances, the quality of light, his grandfather's radicalism, how to be unfair to someone and know it. Inheritance is not a distilled set of lessons but an overwhelming pile, out of which the reader takes what they can use.
2. **Forgiveness as a practical difficulty rather than a virtue.** The central tension is Ames's inability to be generous toward his godson Jack Boughton, and his awareness that this is a failure. Robinson refuses to make it easy, and the book is more interesting about the failing than the eventual attempt.
3. **Attention is the skill being taught.** Ames notices things — two young men laughing in the street, soap bubbles, a horse — presented not as a writer's trick but as the discipline of a life. The quietest version of this week's section 8 argument.

**Reception and dissent.** Pulitzer Prize for Fiction 2005 and the National Book Critics Circle Award; among the most admired American novels of the century — so the honest thing is the *shape* of the objection rather than a hostile review. The standing complaint is that **nothing happens**: a static interior monologue with almost no plot, in a register of religious seriousness some find beautiful and others airless. The second objection is that its patience is a kind of evasion — that a novel so committed to grace can decline to look hard at the harm its characters do. The second is the more serious charge, and the email declined to adjudicate it.

**Limit:** the major reviews were not reached in full; this reports the prize record and the shape of the standard objections, not named critics' reasoning.

**Suitability.** The elder child, yes, with a warning: slow on purpose, and a reader looking for events will stop by page forty. The **11-year-old, no** — not for content, which is gentle, but because the effect depends on understanding what it means to be running out of time.

## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *You have to write down everything you know for someone who will read it years from now, and you cannot ask what they will need. What do you put in?*

**The move:** reasoning under a constraint that cannot be removed. Most problems improve with more information; this one cannot — the reader is absent by definition. The move is to notice that this changes the *strategy* rather than the effort: when you cannot aim, you cover, and you accept that most of what you write will be useless to them.

**The trap:** concluding you should therefore write only the important things. Wrong in an interesting way — **you do not know which things are important**, because importance is a fact about the reader and the reader is not available. The person who writes down only the big lessons has guessed; the person who writes down the quality of the light has not.

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask it. Both children will produce completely different answers, which is the point. Then the harder half: *would you write differently if you knew they would read it tomorrow instead?* If yes, which version is more honest?

**Relative of today's book** — *Gilead* is one old man's answer to exactly this question, and his answer is "everything, in no particular order." And of yesterday's: yesterday asked whether an anonymous contribution is worth making; today asks what to make when you will never meet the person it is for.

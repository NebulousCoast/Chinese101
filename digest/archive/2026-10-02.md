## 4. Book of the day

### *How to Change a Memory: One Neuroscientist's Quest to Alter the Past* — Steve Ramirez (Princeton University Press, 2025)

Shortlisted for the prize whose winner was announced Monday. Ramirez is a
neuroscientist at Boston University; this is his first book.

**The central argument.** A memory is not a recording. It is a *physical object*
in the brain — a sparse, identifiable set of neurons called an **engram** — and
because it is physical it can be found, switched on, switched off and edited. Not
metaphorically: working with Xu Liu in Susumu Tonegawa's lab at MIT, Ramirez
tagged the neurons active during an experience in a mouse, then used pulses of
light to switch those cells back on — implanting a fear memory of something that
never happened, and later changing how an existing memory *felt* without changing
what it was about.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The engram is real and locatable.** Richard Semon proposed the word in 1904
   as pure theory; a century later it is an address in tissue. The core claim, and
   the thing to argue with.
2. **A technique broke the field open, not a theory.** Optogenetics —
   light-sensitive proteins in neurons, millisecond control — made all of it
   possible. Worth noticing when choosing a research direction: **discoveries
   often wait on a method, not an idea.**
3. **Memory is reconstructive, not reproductive.** Every recall rewrites. False
   memories are not malfunctions but a built-in consequence of the architecture.
4. **Feeling can be separated from content.** Keep the memory, change its valence.
   That is the therapeutic target for PTSD and depression — not erasure, which is
   both impossible and undesirable, but changing how a true memory lands.
5. **The ethics stop being hypothetical.** If valence is editable, consent,
   identity and ownership of one's own past become engineering problems. Ramirez
   takes this seriously rather than waving at it.

> **Difficult and contested, and worth saying so.** Engram reductionism — memory
> *is* these cells — is genuinely disputed among neuroscientists. More
> importantly, **mice are not people**: optogenetics requires genetic modification
> and fibre-optic implants, and press coverage of this field routinely reports as
> achieved what is nowhere near translation. Hold the book to that standard:
> Ramirez is careful, his headlines are not.

**The part that makes it a book rather than a review.** Xu Liu, Ramirez's
collaborator and closest friend in science, died suddenly in early 2015, aged 37,
while their paper on reactivating positive memories to relieve depression in mice
was still in press. Ramirez dedicates the book to him, and writes about the
drinking that followed and getting sober in 2021. **The subtitle is not a
marketing line.** A man who can edit memory in mice, writing about a grief he
cannot edit, is the actual subject.

**Why it matters for this October.** The clearest available picture of what a
research question looks like *from the inside* — chosen young, pursued for years,
with the method arriving before the answer. Precisely what the RSI and TASS
applications ask the student to show, in a page.

**Accessibility.** Lay-friendly, ~300 pages, no biology background needed. Good
for a motivated high schooler and a genuinely good model of scientific prose. Too
dark in its second half for a fifth-grader.

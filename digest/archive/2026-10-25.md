## 4 · Book of the day

***Whistling Vivaldi: How Stereotypes Affect Us and What We Can Do* — Claude M. Steele.** Princeton Pre-read, 2015 (rotation source 3).

**Provenance, verified.** Princeton's own newsroom announcement of April 2015 confirms Eisgruber chose it for the Pre-read, in the programme's third year, for the incoming Class of 2019; he also sent it to faculty and staff and asked them to read alongside the first-years. Stated from the university's publication, not an aggregator.

**The argument.** Steele named *stereotype threat*. The claim is narrow and therefore useful: where a negative stereotype about a group you belong to could plausibly explain your performance, the effort of managing that possibility consumes the working memory the task needs. The stereotype need not be believed by anyone present, or by you. It only has to be in the air and relevant. The title comes from the journalist Brent Staples, who as a young Black man in Chicago found that whistling Vivaldi while walking at night made the people around him relax — a small, costly, continuous piece of work the other pedestrians were not doing.

**Honest state of the evidence.** A first-person account of a research programme written in 2010; some specific laboratory effects have shrunk or failed to replicate since. What has held up better is the general mechanism and, notably, the *interventions* — brief self-affirmation and belonging exercises before a high-stakes test replicate better than the threat demonstrations. Read it as a hypothesis with good practical consequences, not a settled result.

**Why today.** The AMC 8 is 89 days away and the elder child is about to spend a year being assessed by foreign institutions, in English, as a Taiwanese applicant educated outside a school — the exact configuration where an explanation for failure is pre-loaded. The takeaway is not "think positive": the framing in the ten minutes before an exam is a controllable variable, and "this test measures how you did today, not what you are" is a cheap intervention with a real evidence base.

**Suitability:** the elder child, yes — short, plainly written. Not the book for the 11-year-old; the idea, yes, via section 8.

**Loose end closed.** The 19 October ledger line flagged Susan Wolf's *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* Pre-read status as unverified. It checks out: Wolf was the **2014** Pre-read, the year before this one.

## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *You are told that at a certain school, pupils who take music lessons get better exam results. Should you take music lessons to get better exam results?*

**The move:** looking for the third thing. Music lessons and good results might both be caused by something else — families with money for a piano teacher, or a child already willing to practise a dull thing for an hour. If the third thing is doing the work, adding music to a child who lacks it changes nothing.

**The trap:** the comfortable answer is "correlation is not causation," said once, and then nothing. That sentence is a door, not a room. The work is *naming a candidate third thing out loud* and then saying what you would look at to test it. An 11-year-old can do the second part: "find two children whose families are the same, and give only one of them lessons."

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask the question. Let the child answer. Then ask only: "what else might the music children have that the others don't?" Do not supply one. When a candidate appears, ask what they would need to see to find out whether they are right. Stop there — do not resolve it.

**Connection to the book.** Steele's career was built on refusing the obvious explanation. Test scores differed between groups; the available story put the difference in the students. He looked for a third thing, found it in the room rather than the person, and then designed a situation where the third thing could be switched off — which is what makes it science rather than opinion.

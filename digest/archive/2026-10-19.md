## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 3 → Princeton/Harvard recommended reading. Chosen for the section the family asked for on Friday.

***Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* — Susan Wolf** (Princeton University Press, 2010). A moral philosopher; the volume comes out of Princeton's University Center for Human Values with an introduction by Stephen Macedo. **Provenance note:** it could **not** be confirmed as a Princeton Pre-read selection. It sits in this slot on the strength of the press and the centre, not a verified Pre-read listing.

**Why it is unusual: it comes with its own critics bound in.** Wolf gives two lectures — "Meaning in Life" and "Why It Matters" — and four scholars respond in print: the poet-philosopher John Koethe, the philosophers Robert M. Adams and Nomy Arpaly, and the psychologist Jonathan Haidt. Wolf then replies to all four. Not reading an argument; watching one conducted properly.

**The central argument.** Wolf rejects the two standard answers. The **fulfilment view** — a meaningful life feels fulfilling — would admit an obsessive doing something worthless. The **larger-than-oneself view** — meaning comes from serving something beyond you — would admit a miserable person dutifully serving a cause they hate. Wolf's **bipartite** or **fitting fulfilment** view: meaning arises when **subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness** — loving something actually worth loving, and being able to engage with it. Both halves required; neither reduces to the other.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Meaning is not happiness, and not morality.** A third thing, and most arguments about how to live confuse all three.
2. **"Objective attractiveness" is the hard part**, and Wolf knows it. She supplies no list of worthwhile things, claiming the concept is indispensable even without one — a risky move the commentators go straight for.
3. **You have to be able to do something about it.** Loving something worthwhile from a distance is not enough.
4. **The disagreements, which are the real curriculum.** Koethe presses on worthiness. Adams doubts *feelings* of fulfilment are essential. Arpaly notes how rarely anyone acts purely for their own fulfilment. Haidt brings "hive psychology" — losing the self in a group — and argues psychology has findings the framework needs.
5. **"Why It Matters"** argues the concept of meaning does work neither self-interest nor duty can do.

**What could not be verified:** the commentators' positions were read through reviews, a blog and a study guide rather than the commentaries themselves; the one-line summaries of Arpaly and Haidt are not worth betting on. The structure and page ranges are solid.

**Suitability: both children, differently** — rare here. For the elder child, the best book offered yet for seeing what careful disagreement looks like. For the eleven-year-old, not the text — but Wolf's question, "can you love something that isn't worth loving?", is tonight's section 8 question.

## 8. Thinking

*Daily and short. One question, and the move it teaches.*

**Today's question, straight out of Susan Wolf:** Can you love something that isn't worth loving? Take a case — someone who spends every evening for thirty years perfecting a collection of bottle caps, and is perfectly happy. Is their life meaningful? Then the harder half: *who gets to say the bottle caps are not worth it, and on what authority?*

**The move it teaches:** holding two criteria at once. "If it makes them happy, it's meaningful" and "no, it's a waste" are both too quick. The skill is noticing that a question can have two requirements pulling against each other, and that collapsing one into the other is the commonest way an argument goes wrong.

**The trap:** "who's to judge?" — which sounds humble and is a refusal to answer. Whoever says it must accept the consequence: that no life is more meaningful than any other, including the lives they admire.

**How to run it:** fifteen minutes, no answer from the adult. The eleven-year-old will defend the bottle caps and should be taken entirely seriously. The elder child has Wolf's four critics to read afterwards. Ask each of them the question Wolf cannot fully answer herself: *what makes something worth loving?*

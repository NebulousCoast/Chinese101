## 1. Interactive learning platforms

The full WOOT closing sequence, only partially visible yesterday. All four now
accounted for, and the pattern matters for next year: **WOOT registration closes
in the last week of September and the first week of October**, two to three weeks
after classes begin.

**ChemWOOT** — **$895**. **Registration and full-refund drop deadline: 5 October
2026 — two days.** Olympiad training in chemistry, USNCO and IChO calibre.
<https://artofproblemsolving.com/school/course/woot-chem>
*Limit:* assumes a student already fluent in general chemistry and willing to sit
olympiad problems weekly. If chemistry is not already the strong subject, this is
the wrong $895.

**PhysicsWOOT** — $895, 16 classes to 4 Mar 2027, Thursdays 7:30–9:30pm ET =
**Friday 7:30am Taipei**. Closes **8 October**. F=ma, USAPhO, IPhO.
**MathWOOT** — $945, closed 22 September. **CodeWOOT** — $895, closed 23
September. Both gone this cycle; late September is the real window next year.

**The free rigorous counterweight**, said plainly before anyone spends $895:

- **MIT OpenCourseWare** — free, no account, no deadline. Full chemistry courses
  with lecture video, problem sets and exams with solutions. University-level
  ceiling. <https://ocw.mit.edu/>
  *Limit:* no feedback loop. Nobody marks the work, nobody notices if the student
  stops — which is exactly what the $895 buys, and it is not nothing.
- **PhET** (CU Boulder) — free, browser-based interactive simulations in chemistry
  and physics. Genuinely interactive: you manipulate the system, not a quiz about
  a video. <https://phet.colorado.edu/>
  *Limit:* builds intuition, not problem-solving speed. No preparation for a timed
  olympiad paper.

**Honest summary:** the free stack reaches the same ceiling on content. What $895
buys is a schedule, graded problems, and someone noticing. Decide which of those
is actually missing before paying.

## 4. Book of the day

### *Moon Tiger* — Penelope Lively (1987, Booker Prize winner)

Chosen deliberately to sit next to yesterday's book. Yesterday a neuroscientist
argued memory is a physical object that can be edited. Today a novelist builds a
whole book out of the same problem and reaches a different conclusion: **the
editing is not optional, and it is what a self is made of.**

**The central argument.** Claudia Hampton, a popular historian in her seventies,
is dying in a hospital bed and announces she will write a history of the world —
"and in the process, my own." The joke is that she cannot. The book demonstrates
why: history refuses to arrive in order. Her life spans before, during and after
the Second World War, England and Egypt, and reaches her not as a chronology but
as a **kaleidoscope** — the same fragments falling into new patterns each turn.

**Key ideas:**

1. **There is no single account, and the form proves it.** Lively replays scenes
   from different characters' viewpoints and shifts between Claudia's first person
   and a cooler third. Each version is accurate, none complete. **The structure is
   the argument** — which is why the book cannot be summarised without being
   destroyed.
2. **Private and public history are the same substance.** She reports on the war
   in Egypt and falls in love with an officer in the Western Desert. Lively
   refuses the hierarchy where the war is background and the affair the story, or
   the reverse.
3. **Memory is reconstruction, and the reconstructor has interests.** Claudia is
   vain, combative, frequently unkind — and she is editing. The reader does the
   work the narrator will not.
4. **Lived time is not linear time.** The dying woman inhabits several decades at
   once, closer to how an interior life runs than a timeline is.
5. **The title carries the thesis in one image.** A "moon tiger" is a mosquito
   coil that burns through the night and is gone by morning, leaving ash.
   Everything in the book burns at that rate.

> **Difficult and contested.** Two real obstacles: Claudia is designed to be hard
> to like — arrogant, imperious, dismissive of her own daughter — and some readers
> never get past her; and the fragmentation is demanding for fifty pages, with no
> help from Lively. The payoff is real but deferred. Its 1987 win is still argued
> about.

**Why it matters.** The best short demonstration of what a historian actually does
with evidence — which is *choose*. For a student about to write application
essays, that is not a literary observation: **every essay is a selected history of
a life, told by someone with an interest in the outcome.** Lively's book is about
exactly that act, performed by someone with nothing left to gain by lying who
still cannot tell it straight.

**Accessibility.** ~200 pages, plain sentences, hard architecture. Harder than its
length suggests; within reach of a strong high schooler willing to be patient for
fifty pages. Not for a fifth-grader — the war and the dying are handled directly.

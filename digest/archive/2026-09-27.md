## 1. Learning platforms

### Purdue OWL — free — and the gap three weeks of this section has failed to close

Purdue's Online Writing Lab has been free for over twenty years and is the
standard reference: MLA, APA and Chicago citation in depth (plus limited IEEE,
AMA, ASA), how to formulate a thesis, how to draft a paper, how to conduct
research using primary sources like interviews and observations as well as
secondary ones, even how to write a résumé. Free worldwide, no account. It covers
two immediate needs here: the citation conventions a research write-up requires,
and the structural advice behind an application essay. <https://owl.purdue.edu/>

> **Now the honest finding, more useful than the recommendation.** Over three
> weeks this section has found excellent free material for every subject except
> one.

- **Mathematics** — AoPS, Omega Learn, Project Euler, Desmos, Polypad
- **Science** — PhET, CK-12, MIT OCW, Labster
- **Coding** — Exercism (with free human code review), Kaggle, Colab, GitHub
- **Research literature** — Semantic Scholar, arXiv, Connected Papers
- **Tutoring and admissions guidance** — Schoolhouse.world

**Writing is the exception.** Purdue OWL gives you *reference*. What it cannot
give is **response** — someone reading the prose and saying where the argument
slips, where a sentence does not mean what the writer thinks. Free tools solve
information problems well and feedback problems badly, and **writing is almost
entirely a feedback problem.**

The three partial answers, all previously covered: **Schoolhouse.world**'s
volunteer tutors (free, variable coverage), **Coursera's financial aid** route
(free graded assignments with human or peer marking), and — costing nothing but
nerve — having the student **write for a real audience who will respond**: a
public GitHub README, a blog, an explanation written for another student. An
audience that can be confused is a feedback mechanism.

For a homeschooled applicant this matters: essays carry disproportionate weight
when no school vouches for you, and it is the one area with no free option that
closes the loop.

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Remains of the Day* — Kazuo Ishiguro (1989)

Winner of the 1989 Booker Prize. Ishiguro later won the Nobel in Literature.

**The central argument.** Stevens, an ageing English butler, takes a motoring trip
through postwar Britain and reflects on decades of service at Darlington Hall. His
governing creed is **dignity** — and Ishiguro's subject is how that creed became
the instrument of a wasted life. A critique of integrity sacrificed to conformity,
presented so quietly you have to assemble it yourself.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Dignity as self-erasure.** Stevens' professionalism requires suppressing
   every personal feeling. When he weeps, we know only because another character
   notices. A virtue, followed absolutely, becomes a means of disappearing.
2. **The unreliable narrator as a moral instrument, not a trick.** Everything
   important is what Stevens cannot say. You learn it by reading past him — the
   form itself an argument about self-knowledge.
3. **Service without judgment is complicity.** His devotion blinds him to the
   fact that he served a Nazi sympathiser. He never chose wrongly; he declined to
   choose at all — and this is not innocence.
4. **The real loss is ordinary.** Miss Kenton: the bond never named, never acted
   on. Not a catastrophe, just a life not lived.
5. **Late reckoning, deliberately incomplete.** Ishiguro refuses Stevens a full
   epiphany. He gets partway and stops. Truer than recognition would be.

> **A loop worth closing.** The 2026-09-18 digest noted five books running had
> circled one question: what happens when a system judges you on terms it will not
> state — Camus, Coetzee, Alexander, Forman, Kafka. **Stevens is the mirror image
> of all of them.** Nobody judges him. He absorbed a system's terms so completely
> that he administered the judgment to himself, and never noticed. Beside Kafka's
> Josef K., destroyed by a court he cannot understand, Stevens is destroyed by a
> standard he understood perfectly and never questioned. **Those are the two ways
> it goes.**

**Honest note:** quiet, interior, restrained, slow. Reviewers split — some find
the pace the point, others find it inert, repetitive or emotionally remote. One
thing in its favour here: the prose is exceptionally clean and controlled, which
makes it a useful **model** for a student who has to learn to write well. Given
section 1, that may be the most practical reason to read it.

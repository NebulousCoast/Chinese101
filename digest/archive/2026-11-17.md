## 4 — Book of the day

**_L'Écume des jours_ (_Froth on the Daydream_ / _Mood Indigo_) by Boris Vian.** 1947. **Number 10 on Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century** — and that rank is **confirmed today**, closing a gap this file has carried for weeks.

**The rank, and why today's version is solid where earlier attempts were not.** I have refused to print this rank twice, because the only support was a list's ordering. Today **two independent sources state the number**: the French Wikipedia table for the list gives "10 | L'Écume des jours | Boris Vian | 1947 | France", and **Fnac's own page** — Fnac being the retailer that *co-ran* the 1999 poll — describes the novel as ranked tenth. A co-organiser stating the rank is about as good as this list gets.

**That completes the top ten in this file:** #1 Camus, #2 Proust, #3 Kafka, #4 Saint-Exupéry, #5 Malraux, #7 Steinbeck, #8 Hemingway, #9 Alain-Fournier, #10 Vian all with stated ranks — plus #6 Céline, which is on the list but whose rank I published as unconfirmed and still cannot verify. **Nine of ten solid.**

**A discrepancy to report rather than hide:** Goodreads shows this book at 41st and 50th on pages titled "Le Monde 100 Books of the Century". Those are **user-made** lists with small vote counts, not the official ranking, and they do not contradict the tenth place. Worth knowing because it is the same trap as the Guardian/Vulture confusion from 4 November: **a page can carry the right list's name and the wrong list's numbers.**

**And remember what the list is.** A 1999 **reader poll** run by Le Monde and Fnac, published 15 October 1999, from a shortlist drawn up by journalists and booksellers. A vote about what French readers loved, not a critical canon. For this book that distinction is not a caveat — it is the whole point.

**What it is.** Colin is young, rich and idle, with a cocktail-mixing piano. He marries Chloé. A water lily begins growing in her lung. The only thing that slows it is being surrounded by flowers, so Colin spends his entire fortune on them and then has to work; and as Chloé declines, **the house itself physically shrinks** — rooms contract, the ceiling comes down, light drains out of the book. Meanwhile his friend Chick ruins himself collecting the works and pipe-ash of a philosopher named **Jean-Sol Partre**.

**Three ideas.**

1. **The world obeys emotion rather than physics**, and it is the device everything else hangs on. The shrinking house is not a metaphor the narrator offers — it is what happens. Grief does not *feel like* a closing room; the room closes. For a reader learning how fiction works, this is the cleanest available example of a book choosing its rules and then keeping them absolutely.
2. **The satire of intellectual celebrity.** Jean-Sol Partre is an anagram of Jean-Paul Sartre, and Chick destroys his life buying Partre merchandise. A French analysis makes the useful point that the target is **less Sartre himself** — whom Vian knew and respected — **than the cult of the intellectual personality and the turning of ideas into merchandise.** A 1947 novel about something that has not aged a day. (The biographical backdrop is not nothing: Vian's wife Michelle had an affair with Sartre.)
3. **Queneau's reading**, which is the best one-line criticism of the book and comes from a major novelist: that it is **not a poignant love story but the poignant story of a failure of love.** Those are different books, and deciding which one you have just read is a real exercise.

**The reception, and it is the most interesting thing about this entry.**

**It failed.** In 1947 the novel was a commercial failure, and according to a French study guide the critics "did not know how to classify it" and neglected it — it matched no dominant literary current of its moment. Then **fifty-two years later the readers of France voted it the tenth most important book of their century.**

**So the dissent is not a review. It is the original verdict.** The contemporary critical response was indifference, and the thing that overturned it was not an argument but time and a reader poll — which is exactly why the nature of this list matters today rather than being a footnote.

**The limit, stated plainly:** I searched for a substantive modern critical objection and **did not find one**. There is a case to be made that the whimsy curdles, or that the shrinking-room device is a trick that cannot bear the weight of a death — but I found that argument made only in **reader reviews**, not in criticism, and I am not going to dress a Goodreads opinion as a critic's. Most of today's French-language sources were study guides and blogs. Treat the Queneau line and the Partre analysis as the two strongest things here and the rest as thin.

**Also: the translation problem is real.** A French-language piece asks outright whether Vian is untranslatable — the book runs on neologisms and puns. In English it exists as both *Froth on the Daydream* and *Mood Indigo*, and there is a 2013 film of the latter name. **If the children read French at all, this is the book in the whole rotation most worth reading in the original.**

**Suitability. The elder child, yes** — it is short, strange, funny for a hundred pages and then devastating, and the formal device is teachable. **Content:** a death from illness, poverty, and a suicide. **For the 11-year-old, probably not yet**, but this is the closest to a *maybe* the slot has produced in a month: the surface is playful enough that the ending would land *harder* on a younger reader rather than passing over them, which is an argument for waiting rather than a prohibition.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, straight out of the book.** If something is ignored when it appears and loved fifty years later, was it good all along?

**The move it teaches:** asking whether a judgement is about **the thing** or about **the moment**. *L'Écume des jours* failed in 1947 — critics could not classify it — and in 1999 French readers voted it tenth best of the century. Nothing in the book changed. Either the 1947 critics were wrong, or the book needed readers who did not exist yet, and those are very different claims.

**The trap:** deciding that later opinion simply wins because it has more information. It has *different* information. The 1947 critics knew exactly what 1947 wanted and were right about that; what they lacked was the next fifty years. Later is not automatically wiser, and the hard question is: **what would make a later verdict better rather than just newer?**

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Use something small and local — a song everyone disliked and then liked, a food that took three tries. Ask whether *the thing* improved or *the person* did. Do not settle it.

**Relative of 10 November, and deliberately so.** That day asked whether a prize changes the thing it is given to; today asks whether fifty years does. Both are really the same question — **where does quality live, in the object or in the reception** — and coming back to it from a different door after a week is the exercise, not the repetition.

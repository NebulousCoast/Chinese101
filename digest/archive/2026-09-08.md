## 4. Book of the day

### *In Defense of Sunlight: The Surprising Science of Sun Exposure* — Rowan Jacobsen (2026)

Reviewed by Andrew Robinson among Nature's five best science picks, June 2026.

> ⚠️ **Read this one with the caveat attached.** Reviewers note Jacobsen cites no
> contemporary research contradicting his thesis, and that dermatology and
> oncology specialists do not broadly agree with him. This is a forcefully
> argued case, not settled consensus — what follows summarizes his argument,
> not established fact.

**The central argument.** Public-health messaging took a narrow true claim —
severe sunburn, especially in childhood, raises skin-cancer risk — and collapsed
it into a much broader one: that sunlight as such is dangerous. Jacobsen argues
the broader claim was never supported, and that moderate sun exposure is a
physiological requirement modern life has engineered out.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The benefits aren't just vitamin D.** Supplement trials have mostly
   disappointed — which Jacobsen reads as evidence that vitamin D is a *marker*
   of sun exposure rather than the active ingredient.
2. **Nitric oxide is the proposed mechanism.** Sun on skin releases NO, a
   vasodilator that relaxes blood vessels and lowers blood pressure; it also
   carries neural signals and helps immune cells kill pathogens.
3. **The claimed effects are cardiovascular and metabolic** — reduced stroke,
   diabetes and hypertension risk, better sleep, stronger immune function.
4. **Risk was measured one-sidedly.** The harms of avoidance were never weighed
   against its benefits, so guidance optimized against one bad outcome while
   ignoring others.
5. **Circadian entrainment.** Morning light sets the body clock, which governs
   sleep and mood downstream.

**Why it matters — and why to argue with it.** Taken at face value it is a case
study in how a public-health message optimized against a single bad outcome can
create unmeasured costs elsewhere; that reasoning pattern is worth a teenager's
attention. But the book is also a live specimen of confirmation bias in science
writing — a well-argued thesis that omits its opposition. Read alongside a
critical review ([Undark's is
good](https://undark.org/2026/08/28/book-review-in-defense-of-sunlight/)), it
becomes a better exercise than reading it alone: the question is not whether he
is right, but how you would tell.

## 1. Learning platforms

### Khan Academy + Khanmigo — the best price-per-value in this survey

Core Khan Academy is **permanently free** for every learner and teacher.
Khanmigo, the AI tutor add-on, is **$4/month or $44/year — and one
subscription covers ten child accounts.** The cheapest paid thing found so far
that isn't simply free.

**Grade fit:** through high school, thinning at the advanced end.
**Independent use:** yes, designed for it. **Interactivity:** genuine
practice-and-feedback rather than video-with-quizzes; Khanmigo prompts
Socratically instead of answering.

> **Honest limit:** Khan is excellent for consolidating standard curriculum and
> **useless as olympiad preparation**. It will not get anyone to AIME. Use it to
> secure foundations, then AoPS or Omega Learn for the competition ladder.
> <https://www.khanacademy.org/>

### MIT OpenCourseWare — free, no enrollment, no account

Full materials from real MIT courses — lecture notes, problem sets, exams, many
with solutions. **Grade fit:** undergraduate, so a stretch target — and that is
the point for a student aiming at RSI. Working through 8.01 (mechanics) or
18.01 (single-variable calculus) is a realistic self-study project for a strong
high schooler, and **finishing one is a genuine signal**. **Limit:** no
instructor, no feedback, no deadlines — suits a self-starter, defeats everyone
else. <https://ocw.mit.edu/>

## 4. Book of the day

### *1929: Inside the Greatest Crash in Wall Street History — And How It Shattered a Nation* — Andrew Ross Sorkin

One of Bill Gates's summer 2026 picks.

**The central argument.** The crash was not a random panic but the predictable
failure of a system built on leverage, weak regulation and elite self-dealing.
Speculation, fragile financing, market manipulation and policy misjudgment had
created vulnerabilities that official reassurance could not resolve — the
structure was already unsound before anything visibly went wrong.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Margin debt was the fuel.** Margin loans grew from **$1 billion in 1920 to
   $6 billion by 1929**. Buying on credit had been a business-class practice;
   bankers helped spread it to the general public.
2. **Leverage becomes a trap.** When prices fell, brokers demanded more cash;
   investors who could not meet margin calls had to sell; selling drove prices
   lower; lower prices triggered more calls. A self-amplifying loop — the
   *mechanism*, not the mood, is what made it catastrophic.
3. **Regulation was lax by design.** Insider trading was "not a crime but a
   craft." Manipulation was ordinary practice.
4. **"Debt was the new gospel."** A cultural shift in attitudes to borrowing
   preceded the financial one.
5. **Reassurance is not a policy.** Confident official statements did nothing,
   because the vulnerability was structural.

The research is the book's strength: archival access to letters, diaries, an
insider's unpublished memoir, and the minutes of the Federal Reserve Bank of New
York's board meetings.

> **Worth knowing:** not everyone accepts it. Forbes' John Tamny argues Sorkin
> misreads both what preceded 1929 and what followed, while other reviewers call
> it a work of true scholarship. Contested on *interpretation*, not on the
> archival work.

**Why it matters.** The transferable idea is the feedback loop in point 2 —
forced selling begetting forced selling. That structure recurs, most visibly in
2008, and recognizing it is worth more than any date. For a teenager it is also
a clean case study in how a system can be deeply fragile while everyone inside
it feels confident.

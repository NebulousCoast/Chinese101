## 4 — Book of the day

**_John of John_ by Douglas Stuart.** 2026. **On the Booker Prize 2026 shortlist** — the one Harrison beat on Monday.

Rotation index 5 (Booker).

**Provenance, and the file earned its keep here.** A blog review was the only place today's searches tied this book to the shortlist, which on its own would not be enough. But the digest confirmed the full 2026 shortlist from thebookerprizes.com's own 2026 pages on 2 November, and *John of John* is on it. That is what the standing file is for: **a weak source today is sufficient when a strong source was recorded a fortnight ago.**

Stuart won the Booker in 2020 with *Shuggie Bain*; *Young Mungo* followed; this is his third novel. He was among the bookmakers' favourites this year and lost to M. John Harrison on 9 November.

**What it is.** The Isle of Harris in the Outer Hebrides, in the 1990s. Cal, a young man, comes home. His father John is a crofter and a Harris Tweed weaver. The father is a closeted gay man, and the book is about two people in a conservative place who cannot say what they are.

**Three ideas.**

1. **The structural reversal the book turns on:** in the familiar version of this story the son carries the secret and the father is the obstacle. Here the secret is the *father's*. That single inversion re-aims everything — the son's return is not a confession but a discovery, and the father's harshness has to be re-read as something other than simple bigotry.
2. **That silence is inherited like a trade.** The crofting and the weaving are passed from father to son, and so is the inability to speak; the book treats both as skills transmitted the same way, which is a more interesting claim than "the place was repressive".
3. **That an economy can be a character.** Harris Tweed and small-croft farming are not scenery here — they are a declining way of making a living, and the pressure that puts on a family is as much the engine of the book as anyone's temperament.

**The dissent, and it is interesting rather than dismissive.**

- **The praise is nearly unanimous:** the *Boston Globe* calls it his finest novel to date, with "gorgeous" prose and plotting where "nothing is lost"; the *Scotsman*'s reviewer calls it "a superb example of what a novel can do" under the headline "I'd give him another Booker right away"; *BookPage* describes sentences "carefully carved" with occasional sharp humour.
- **The objections are two, and both are about shape rather than quality.** The first is **pace** — that the novel is slow to get going, and that it feels drawn out, "a bit too long for the topics it is exploring". The *Scotsman*, while admiring, also notes something rushed about part of the ending, an interesting pairing with the slow start.
- The second is the one worth arguing about at the table: **that the material is well-worn** — the bullying, ultra-religious father and the worldlier son returning from the city are, as one reviewer puts it, tropes. **That objection is a good test case**, because the book's whole move is to take those tropes and put the secret in the wrong person. So: **is "I have seen this before" a criticism, or a description of the thing the book is deliberately working with?** Reasonable readers differ, and that is a better argument than most.

**The limit:** the two pace-and-trope objections both come from **blogs**, not from a newspaper or journal, and the strongest sources I reached are all favourable. So the critical consensus is positive and the dissent is thinner than the praise — which is the honest shape of the reception rather than a balanced one I have manufactured.

**Suitability. The elder child, yes**, with content noted plainly: this is a novel about a closeted man in a hostile community, and I would expect homophobia, family cruelty and sexual content handled seriously rather than gratuitously — though **I have not read it and am describing it from reviews**, so the family should read a review before handing it over. It is also, for an applicant writing essays, an unusually good model of how a familiar structure can be made new by moving one piece. **For the 11-year-old, no.**

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question.** When you pass on something somebody told you, do you have to pass on *how sure they were*?

**The move it teaches:** treating **confidence as part of the message** rather than as packaging around it. "The shop might be open" and "the shop is open" are different claims, and the second is not a tidier version of the first — it is a different and stronger thing to have said. Repeating the second when you were told the first is not a summary; it is an upgrade you were not authorised to make.

**The trap:** thinking this is about honesty. Mostly it is not. The usual cause is that hedges are boring and get trimmed for brevity, by people with no intention of misleading anyone. Which makes the real question harder: **if dropping the hedge is the natural thing to do, whose fault is the result** — the person who trimmed it, or the person who believed the trimmed version?

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Play the telephone game and watch specifically for the *maybes* disappearing. Then ask the harder half: is there anything you could do when telling someone something, so that the maybe survives being repeated?

**Relative of this week, and of today's correction.** Monday to Thursday circled who is entitled to tell, to judge, to disagree; this is the same family from a new angle — not who may speak, but what you owe the person you heard it from.

**And the disclosure, because today's example is mine again.** Twice this week I recorded a careful "this is unconfirmed" in my own notes and then printed the confident version in this email. Both times the hedge was lost in the summarising, by me, with nobody to blame but the convenience of a shorter sentence.

## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 2 → Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century, a spring 1999 Fnac/Le Monde **reader poll** about what the French public loved, not a critical canon. Today's book is the hardest case on that list, given with the problem attached rather than hidden.

***Voyage au bout de la nuit* (Journey to the End of the Night) — Louis-Ferdinand Céline** (1932). **On the list**, confirmed from Wikidata. Commonly given as number 6, but the only sources found for that rank were a user review and an AI-generated encyclopedia page, **so the number is unconfirmed**. The book's standing is not in doubt.

**The problem, stated first.** Céline went on to publish violently antisemitic pamphlets in the late 1930s, collaborated in sympathy during the Occupation, fled France at the Liberation and was convicted in absentia. That is established historical record; **today's searches did not cover it**, and the email says both of those things rather than omitting the fact or implying it was verified this morning. The novel precedes the pamphlets. Whether that matters is today's section 8 question.

**The central argument.** Ferdinand Bardamu volunteers for the First World War almost by accident, discovers within a page that courage is a confidence trick, and then travels — the trenches, colonial Africa, Ford's Detroit, a poor Paris suburb where he becomes a doctor to people who cannot pay. Every institution he passes through runs on the same lie: that the suffering it requires is meaningful.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Cowardice as clarity.** Bardamu is a coward and says so; the book's claim is that this is the only honest response available.
2. **The style is the argument.** Fragmented syntax, slang, the rhythms of spoken French — the oral, unrestrained style that made his reputation. He broke the sentence on purpose.
3. **Colonialism and the assembly line are the same machine.** The Africa and Detroit chapters are deliberately interchangeable in logic.
4. **Medicine without consolation.** The least romanticised portrait of a caring profession in this rotation.
5. **No redemption is offered.** Unlike the rest of the recent run, the structure does not bend towards learning anything.

**Why it is here at all.** Eight days of books about the gap between what people believe of themselves and what they do — Farrell, Morton, Wolf, Gordimer, Jones. Céline is the limit case: a writer who saw through every institution's self-flattery with genuine brilliance and then became much worse than the hypocrites he exposed. **Seeing clearly is not the same as being good.**

**Suitability: the elder child only**, with the parent's judgement first. Relentless bleakness, obscenity, violence, racism in the colonial chapters through a narrator contemptuous of everyone. **Not** for the eleven-year-old. The alternative from the same list for a teenager is **Boris Vian's *L'Écume des jours*** — inventive, playful, devastating, short.

### A. Contributing to open-source software — and the first item here that definitively excludes the eleven-year-old

**The hard floor:** GitHub's Terms of Service state a user must be **at least 13 years of age**, the stated reason being compliance with United States law, and that GitHub will **terminate the account immediately** if it learns a user is under 13. Your country's minimum may be higher, in which case local law governs. A GitHub site-policy discussion asking for parental-consent accounts reports that the company does not currently meet the legal requirements for verifying parental consent, citing COPPA — a community thread, not official policy, but it explains why no consent route exists.

**So the elder child can have an account; the eleven-year-old cannot**, with no workaround worth recommending. Nothing in the results says whether a minor may contribute through an adult's account; the Terms point towards no, since a machine account must be set up by an individual human accepting the Terms on its behalf. **Do not put a child's work under a parent's name and call it theirs.**

**Could not be answered, and it is the real question for the elder child:** whether a minor can sign a **Contributor License Agreement**. Many substantial projects require a CLA or a Developer Certificate of Origin before merging code, and a contract signed by a minor is a genuine legal question unresolved by these sources. Check the project's CLA provider before investing weeks. DCO sign-off projects are likely less fraught than corporate-CLA ones, but that is not certain either.

**One age rule that does accommodate teenagers:** GitHub Sponsors permits users aged **13 to 17** to give and receive funds, provided Stripe can verify a legal guardian. The platform knows how to handle minors when it wants to — just not for accounts under 13.

**Why it is real collaboration:** a pull request is reviewed by a stranger anywhere, the discussion is public and permanent, the artefact is used by others, and the name on the commit is the child's. Like Wikipedia, the value is being corrected in public by someone who cares. Unlike Wikipedia, the thing has to run.

**The limits:** no credential; "good first issue" labels are often mislabelled and lead into month-long rabbit holes; the commonest failure is picking an unmaintained project. **Start by fixing documentation in a project the child already uses.**

## 8. Thinking

*Daily and short. One question, and the move it teaches.*

**Today's question, and it is today's book:** Does it matter who wrote it? Céline wrote one of the century's great novels and later wrote antisemitic pamphlets. Is the novel *worse* than it was before you knew that? Is it the *same* novel?

**The move it teaches:** distinguishing the **quality** of a thing from the **desert** of its maker. Those come apart, and most people's instinct is to fuse them — "great art, so stop talking about the man" or "bad man, so the art is poison." Both skip the step where you say what you think a book *is*.

**The trap:** "separate the art from the artist." It sounds like a principle and usually ends the conversation early. Ask what it would mean for a *living* author whose royalties you would be paying — the answer is often different, and noticing that is the insight. The opposite trap is "I won't read it," a legitimate choice and not an argument.

**How to run it:** fifteen minutes, no answer from the adult. For the eleven-year-old, a version they can own — a song they love by someone unkind, or a game by a company that did something bad. For the elder child, a second half: *if seeing through everyone's self-deception did not make Céline a good man, what is clear-sightedness worth?*

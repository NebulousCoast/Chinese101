## 1. Learning platforms

### CK-12 — free, and the most underrated thing in the pool

A nonprofit open educational resource library: customizable digital textbooks
("FlexBooks"), interactive simulations, and adaptive practice across maths and
science. **Cost:** free. **Grade fit:** strong at secondary level — one of the
few genuinely free options pitched at high school rather than elementary.
**Independent use:** yes, for a student who can navigate a textbook without
being walked through it. Built-in audio support, which matters for dyslexic
readers.

> **Limit:** it is a library, not a course. No sequence is imposed and no one
> checks your work. It substitutes for a *textbook*, not for a teacher — pair it
> with Khan for structure or AoPS for difficulty.

**Honest note on this section:** the verified platforms in the pool are now all

## 2. Study-abroad opportunities

### NEW — TASS (Telluride Association Summer Seminar). Possibly the best-funded program found yet.

> **Funding:** Telluride covers **all program costs for every student** —
> tuition, books, room and board, field trips, facilities fees. Not need-based,
> not a discount: free for everyone admitted. They also help with **travel
> costs**, and offer additional financial aid to students who would otherwise be
> earning at a summer job and cannot afford to lose that income. That last
> provision is unusually thoughtful and has not appeared anywhere else in this
> digest.

**Eligibility:** both US and international students explicitly welcome. High
school sophomores and juniors — rising juniors and rising seniors. At least 15
at the start, no older than 17 by the end.

> **Why this matters for timing:** TASS takes students a **year earlier than
> RSI**, which needs roughly three completed years of high school. For a
> sophomore or rising junior, RSI is not yet available but TASS is. The two
> **fit together rather than competing**.

**Subject matter — be clear on this:** TASS is humanities and social science,
not STEM. TASS-CBS covers history, politics, literature and art from people of
African descent; TASS-AOS examines how forms of power shape social structures.
For strictly olympiad-STEM ambitions this is a different track; for a student
with genuine intellectual range it is a fully-funded seminar at Cornell or the
University of Maryland.

**Timing:** the 2026 cycle opened 15 October 2025 and closed 3 December 2025,
with the program running 21 June – 25 July 2026. A similar shape for 2027 would
mean applications opening in about **a month**. ⚠️ Confirm on the official site —
2027 dates are not asserted from a 2026 pattern.

### *Disgrace* — J.M. Coetzee (1999)

Booker winner, and one of the six finalists in the 2008 Best of the Booker vote
that *Midnight's Children* won.

> ⚠️ **Content warning, and it is the parent's call:** *Disgrace* contains a
> rape, is unrelentingly bleak, and offers no redemption. It is commonly taught
> at university and only occasionally in advanced high school. It is included
> because it is genuinely one of the major novels on these lists — but whether
> it suits this student now or in a few years is a judgement for the family.

**The central argument.** In post-apartheid South Africa one moral order has
collapsed and no settled replacement has arrived. David Lurie, a professor
disgraced over an affair with a student, loses his position and goes to his
daughter's smallholding, where far worse follows. Coetzee's *refusal* is the
point: he will not supply the redemption arc the form seems to promise.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Disgrace as a condition, not an event.** The question is not how Lurie fell
   but what remains of a person once social standing is gone — and whether
   anything in him survives that was not standing.
2. **The refusal of performed repentance.** The university will restore him if
   he confesses in the expected terms. He declines — not from innocence but from
   a refusal to perform an interior state on demand. *The parallel with Meursault
   (2026-09-12) is exact and worth discussing: both are punished less for the act
   than for failing to feel correctly in public.*
3. **Historical reckoning without a ledger.** Lucy's decision to stay, and to
   accept terms after the attack, reads to some as acknowledging a historical
   debt and to others as submission. Coetzee does not adjudicate — which is why
   the book remains an argument rather than a statement.
4. **Grace located outside the human hierarchy.** The animal clinic — Lurie's
   work with dying dogs — is where the novel puts dignity: care given where
   nothing is owed and nothing returns.
5. **Who gets to narrate.** Lurie is a professor of Romantic poetry whose
   authority over language dissolves; the novel is partly about losing the right
   to frame your own story.

**Genuinely contested:** the ANC accused the novel of reinforcing racist
stereotypes of Black South Africans, and Coetzee left South Africa not long
after. That controversy is part of the book's meaning now, not a footnote to it
— any serious reading has to engage with it.

**Why it matters.** It is the hardest available test of whether a novel can
portray a moral situation without resolving it. For a mature reader that is the
value; for a younger one it may simply be punishing.

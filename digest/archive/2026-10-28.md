## 4 · Book of the day

***The Underground Railroad* — Colson Whitehead.** 2016. Pulitzer Prize, National Book Award, Oprah's Book Club.

**Source note.** Slot 0 is GatesNotes and it is now **exhausted** for anything verifiable. Gates's summer 2026 list was three books, all used here. No October or November 2026 pick surfaces, gatesnotes.com does not surface from this container, and the aggregators are actively unreliable — one lists Suleyman's *The Coming Wave* and Miller's *Chip War* as "2026 picks" with no Gates source at all, which is exactly how a fabricated recommendation would look. **Advanced to slot 1** (NYT 100 Best Books of the 21st Century), stated in the email.

**Rank, with its weakness stated:** SparkNotes and Book Riot both place it at **#7**. The Times' own list page was unreachable, so the rank rests on secondary sources — though several publisher pages independently describe it as one of the Times' ten best of the century, consistent with 7 without confirming it.

**The central device.** Whitehead asked a child's question — *what if the Underground Railroad were an actual railroad?* — and refused to treat it as whimsy. Cora escapes a Georgia plantation through literal tunnels on literal trains, and each state she surfaces in is a different America: one that kills her, one that studies her, one that has legislated her out of existence.

**Three ideas worth the argument.**
1. **An invented mechanism can be more truthful than an accurate one.** The historical network was loose, improvised and mostly undocumented. A railway is false in every detail and arguably right about what matters — that escape was *engineered, maintained and staffed* by people taking enormous risks for strangers.
2. **The states as a thought experiment.** Each stop is a separate hypothesis about how a society can organise atrocity — violence, benevolent experimentation, bureaucratic erasure. Structure as argument: the thing examined is not one practice but a range.
3. **Genre as a tool rather than a category.** The apparatus of adventure fiction — chases, escapes, a pursuing hunter — applied to material that usually forbids it, with the friction deliberate.

**The dissent, taken seriously.** The *Dundee Review of the Arts* argued the "real" metaphor is stronger than Whitehead's reification, and asked why the railway is so mundanely imagined. Readers have objected more sharply that the book will lead uninformed people to think the railroad *was* literal — a worry about history teaching rather than prose. Against that: Madhu Dubey in *American Literary History* (2020) reads the literalising as Whitehead's signature move, deviating from earlier attempts at a visceral confrontation with slavery; Stephanie Li calls it a governing conceit that flaunts any claim to a realistic past; the *Journal of the Civil War Era* called the device apt and said it reaches historical truth in an essential sense.

The major newspaper reviews did not surface, so the above is the academic and reader argument rather than the full critical record.

**Suitability.** The elder child, yes, strongly — the clearest demonstration in modern fiction of how structure carries an argument, and the realism objection is itself worth arguing. For the **11-year-old, no.** The violence is explicit and sustained.

## 6 · International collaboration

**NASA's Exoplanet Watch — and it may solve a different problem than the one it looks like it solves.**

Participants observe stars where a planet is known to transit, measure the dip in brightness, and submit the measurement. The aggregate refines known transit timings, which future space telescopes need in order to schedule observations efficiently. NASA-run, named scientists behind it.

**What makes it usable from a flat in Taiwan with no telescope:** NASA's own page describes a **data checkout system** — request observation data and analyse it yourself. Archival data from the MicroObservatory in Tucson is available the same way. Some coverage says a smartphone suffices for the *observing* route; treat that as optimistic. Observations and results are stored through an **AAVSO account**, set up as part of joining.

**What it confers, honestly.** Coverage says participants are *credited if their data is used* in future papers. That is **not** co-authorship and is not being upgraded here. Foldit and Eterna confer collective authorship; Zooniverse confers no establishable credit; this sits between them. The honest description is "acknowledged contribution to published work."

**The part that actually matters, and it is not the astronomy.** Exoplanet Watch runs a **Slack workspace** where participants meet each other, ask questions, troubleshoot, and get updates on **biweekly team meetings**. Beginners explicitly welcomed.

That is a standing, fortnightly, international group working on a shared technical problem, joinable by a sixteen- or seventeen-year-old without an institution, a teacher, a fee or a visa. For fifty-two days the top obstacle in this file has been *teammates*, and the search has been inside competitions — the wrong place, because **a competition wants a team that already exists, while a working group wants participants, and then teams form inside it.** Three months of being useful in that Slack may dissolve the Conrad or Ethics Olympiad teammate problem as a side effect.

**Limits.** science.nasa.gov refuses fetches; read through the search index and through AAVSO and astronomy-club pages. **No source explicitly confirms international eligibility — and none restricts it either;** the coverage is US-centric, which is not US-only, and Standing Rule 1 says check both filters rather than assume. The AAVSO is long-established and international, making a country restriction unlikely but unverified. Start from the "how to contribute" checklist on NASA's own page.

## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *If you invent a detail that never happened, can the story you tell be more true than one with every detail right?*

**The move:** separating accuracy from truthfulness, then noticing they are different kinds of claim. A map is wrong about almost everything — colours, scale, the absence of people — and is the most useful thing to carry. A perfectly correct list of every house in a city tells you nothing about the city. The question is what a representation is *for*, and what it may sacrifice to do that.

**The trap:** concluding that accuracy does not matter. It does — the difference between a map and a lie is whether the distortion works in the service of the thing shown or against it. The real question, where an 11-year-old and a seventeen-year-old meet exactly: **how would you tell the difference?** What test separates a useful simplification from a comfortable falsehood?

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask it. Offer no example first. If nothing comes, ask about a map — or about a story they have been told about themselves as a baby that is probably not quite right. Then the test question, left unanswered.

**Relative of this week, turned round.** Sunday asked what else might explain a pattern; Monday what procedure would make a claim believable; Tuesday what a piece of evidence rules out. Today changes direction: the first three asked *when to believe something*, today asks *when being wrong is permitted*. Both children should notice today's is the harder one, and that the first three do not answer it.

## 2. Study-abroad opportunities

Both programs below accept international applicants and are genuinely funded
rather than discounted. **Deadlines are given with deliberate caution** —
secondary sites contradicted each other, and an invented deadline is the worst
thing this digest could do.

### Research Science Institute (RSI) — MIT / Center for Excellence in Education

- **What:** Six weeks of original scientific research at MIT. Widely considered
  the most selective high-school STEM program in the world.
- **Eligibility:** Open to international students, who apply on a separate track
  from US students.
- **Funding:** Entirely cost-free — tuition, housing, and meals all covered.
- **Deadline:** ⚠️ **Confirm on the official site.** Sources conflict: some report
  15 January for US and 15 March for international applicants, others describe
  applications opening in October and closing in early December. Do not rely on
  either without checking.
- [cee.org/programs/research-science-institute](https://www.cee.org/programs/research-science-institute)

### Summer Science Program (SSP)

- **What:** Residential research in astrophysics, biochemistry, genomics, or
  synthetic chemistry — students collect and analyse real data, not exercises.
- **Eligibility:** Open to international applicants.
- **Funding:** Need-based, and unusually generous. Free at family income at or
  below $75,000; discounted below roughly $140,000. Selected participants who
  would otherwise need summer work receive a **$3,000 stipend** on top of free
  admission. Admissions are **need-blind** — aid is not requested until after
  admission.
- **Deadline:** Reported as **30 January 2027** for international applicants.
  Verify on the application page.
- [ssp.org/application](https://ssp.org/application/) ·
  [ssp.org/fees-financial-aid](https://ssp.org/fees-financial-aid/)

**Note on the funding filter:** SSP's aid is need-based, so it meets the
"generous stipend" bar only if family income qualifies. RSI is free for
everyone admitted, which makes it the cleaner match for the brief.

## 3. Book of the day

### *The Strategy of Conflict* — Thomas C. Schelling (1960)

Recommended by Princeton politics professor David B. Carter, who calls it
"probably the best book ever written about conflict and still very useful and
important for understanding strategic interaction among states (and
individuals)." Schelling won the Nobel in economics in 2005, largely for this
work.
[Princeton](https://www.princeton.edu/news/2021/07/06/summer-reads-2021-what-are-princeton-professors-reading-and-listening-summer)

**The central argument.** Most conflict is not zero-sum. Schelling's foundational
move is to reject the framing of adversaries as pure opponents: in almost every
real conflict — nuclear standoffs, strikes, divorces, price wars — both parties
share an interest in avoiding the worst outcome. Conflict is therefore a
*bargaining* problem, and bargaining problems have a logic entirely different
from games of pure opposition. This reframing is why the book reads as usefully
today as it did during the Cold War it was written for.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The power to bind yourself is power.** Conventional intuition says freedom
   of action is strength. Schelling shows the opposite: the negotiator who can
   *credibly* prove they cannot concede — a union with a strike mandate, a
   driver who visibly rips out their own steering wheel in a game of chicken —
   wins. Burning your bridges is a strategic asset.

2. **Credibility, not capability, is what deters.** A threat you would never
   rationally carry out is worthless, however powerful you are. The strategic
   problem is not building capacity but making an unattractive commitment
   believable.

3. **The threat that leaves something to chance.** Since genuinely suicidal
   threats aren't credible, actors instead create *risk* — deliberately
   introducing a probability of catastrophe nobody fully controls. This is
   brinkmanship, and it explains behaviour that looks irrational but is not.

4. **Focal points.** When people must coordinate without communicating, they
   converge on solutions that are salient for reasons outside the game's
   mathematics — a landmark, a round number, a precedent. "Schelling points"
   showed that culture and shared context do real analytical work, which formal
   game theory had treated as noise.

5. **Tacit bargaining.** Parties who cannot or will not talk still negotiate,
   through actions read as signals. Much of diplomacy is this.

**Why it still matters.** Schelling built the vocabulary — commitment,
credibility, deterrence, escalation, salience — that strategic thinking still
runs on, and did it in plain prose with almost no mathematics. For a high
schooler, it is an unusually accessible entry into thinking rigorously about
strategy, and its examples (bidding, queuing, chicken) are drawn from ordinary
life rather than the arms race. The uncomfortable takeaway is durable: rational
actors can be driven to catastrophic outcomes by the structure of their
situation, not by any failure of reasoning.

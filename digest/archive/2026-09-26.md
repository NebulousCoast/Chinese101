## 1. Learning platforms

### GitHub — free, and the place computational research becomes visible

Following on from Kaggle and Project Euler: if the student does computational
work, the **output is code**, and the standard way to show code is a public
repository. GitHub is free for public and private repos with no limit worth
worrying about.

> **Why this matters more than it sounds:** a repository with real commit history
> is a *different kind of evidence* from an essay. It shows work accumulating
> over months — false starts, fixes, the shape of someone actually thinking. An
> application essay says "I did a project." A repo proves it, with dates. For a
> homeschooled applicant with no school transcript to corroborate anything, that
> independent verifiability is unusually valuable.

**Practical advice:** write a real README explaining the question, the method and
what was found. Most student repos are a folder of unexplained scripts, which
demonstrates nothing to a reader who will not run the code.

### The GitHub Student Developer Pack — worth knowing, with a caveat

Free access to a long list of otherwise-paid developer tools. **Homeschoolers are
explicitly eligible** — GitHub's criteria name homeschool alongside high school,
secondary school, college and university, for anyone 13 or over in a degree- or
diploma-granting course of study.

> ⚠️ **The caveat:** verification normally runs on a **school-issued email
> address**, which a homeschooler does not have. The fallback is **dated** proof
> of enrolment showing name, school and current enrolment — an enrolment letter,
> current transcript, or class schedule. A student ID without a date is weaker.
> Applying through a homeschool offers an option to supply more detail, and some
> families seek a letter from their local education authority confirming
> homeschooling status.

**Eligible in principle, paperwork friction in practice.** Assemble documentation
before applying rather than after being declined. <https://github.com/> ·
<https://education.github.com/pack>

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Age of Alchemy: How Early Innovators Shaped Modern Chemistry* — Kit Chapman (Profile Books, 2026)

Shortlisted for the Royal Society Trivedi Science Book Prize — the winner is
announced in three days, so this is a **live contender** rather than a settled
classic. Reviewers have called it "glittering" and "superb."

**The central argument.** Chemistry did not begin with the Enlightenment.
Chapman's target is the standard story in which Boyle and Lavoisier invent the
science and everything before is prologue. His claim is that chemistry accumulated
*gradually over millennia* — people distilling, smelting, forging and fermenting,
with science and mysticism thoroughly blurred — and that this was the actual
process, not a rough draft of it.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Alchemy was not a wrong turn.** The load-bearing claim. The conventional
   account treats alchemy as mysticism to be cleared away before real chemistry
   could start; Chapman argues it was an *essential part of the journey* — and
   notes that alchemists in Roman Egypt designed apparatus still used in
   laboratories today.
2. **Practice can precede theory by a very long way.** Sri Lankan forgers made
   steel of extraordinary strength millennia before any theory could explain how,
   inverting the usual assumption that understanding comes first.
3. **Chemistry as a science of underdogs — and a global one.** Stone Age
   leatherworkers, Tang Dynasty herbalists, Mayan stoneworkers and Chinese
   alchemists, as contributors rather than colour.
4. **Where you write the history from determines what counts as science.** Define
   chemistry as what began in 18th-century Europe and everything else becomes
   pre-science by definition.

> **Why this one for this family:** the Chinese alchemical tradition appears
> *inside* chemistry's actual lineage, not as an exotic footnote to a European
> story. For a student in Taiwan preparing to apply to American institutions, that
> cuts against the assumption that serious science is something one travels
> *toward*. It also rhymes with the method in *The Ghost Road* (09-21): use
> another tradition as a mirror, and your own arrangements stop looking
> inevitable.

> **Caveat on freshness:** published this year, so there is little critical
> literature yet, and this is written from the publisher's framing and
> prize-shortlist coverage rather than a body of reviews. Provisional in a way a
> Steinbeck summary would not be.

**Accessibility:** popular science for general readers, and the shortlist is
judged partly on readability. Should suit a chemistry-inclined teenager.

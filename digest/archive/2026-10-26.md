Twenty pieces of the past year's science and nature writing. Munroe — the *xkcd* cartoonist, before that a NASA roboticist — states his criterion plainly: he leaned toward articles that explained **why we know** those facts, and why we cared, rather than simply listing facts. That is the value of the book, and it is an *editorial* argument rather than a thesis.

Three ideas it gets at:
1. The interesting part of a scientific claim is its **warrant**, not its content. A fact you cannot trace is a rumour with a citation.
2. Science writing fails in an identifiable way: it reports conclusions as settled because the hedging is boring — and the hedging is where the science is.
3. By construction: twenty writers on twenty subjects demonstrate there is no such thing as a science style. Sabrina Imbler's natural history and Rachel Aviv's psychiatric reporting are not doing the same job.

Subjects include water shortages in Pakistan, a medical device that reverses the effects of vertigo, and the politics of the 2025 Los Angeles fire clean-ups. Contributors named in listings: Sabrina Imbler, Rivka Galchen, Rachel Aviv, Nathaniel Rich, Tatiana Schlossberg, Trevor Quirk, Roy Richard Grinker.

## 6 · International collaboration

**The International Astronomical Search Collaboration (IASC).** Students are sent time-lapse sky images taken hours earlier, use free software (Astrometrica) to find moving objects, and submit measurements. Original discoveries are recognised by the **Minor Planet Center** and the **IAU** — real credit in the permanent astronomical record, not a certificate. Run as an educational service by a consortium: the Astronomical Research Institute, Hardin-Simmons University, the Global Hands-On Universe Association, the Lawrence Hall of Science. **Participation and software stated to be at no cost.**

Collaboration in the strict sense this section demands: a team contributing measurements to a shared, permanent scientific record, with no travel and no residence filter.

**The honest part, which cuts against yesterday's framing.** IASC registers *schools*. Past campaigns selected teams largely on a one-paragraph statement from **a teacher** explaining why the teacher and students wanted to take part, and the instruction is that schools wishing to join contact **Dr Patrick Miller** at Hardin-Simmons. Some campaigns capped places per country.

Yesterday the "what counts as a school" obstacle was narrowed to two categories — state-mediated selection, and American programmes using school enrolment as a residency proxy. **IASC is neither.** It is a third category: a programme that is **capacity-rationed**, where the school is the unit because a small team of academics can only supervise so many groups. That is arithmetic, not gatekeeping — which is good news, because a rationing gate responds to a persuasive paragraph in a way a residency rule never does. And the family satisfies it in substance: a registered 非學校型態實驗教育 unit with the parent as registered educator *is* a teacher with students in the only sense IASC needs. The 團體自學 route would make it unarguable.

**Limits.** The current campaign calendar is unfindable from here — iasc.cosmosearch.org and science.nasa.gov both refuse fetches — and the 2026 global campaign closed registration 3 April 2026. The one 2026–27 campaign visible is **Nepal-only** and requires 45 participants from one institution, so it is no model. ## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *You read that drinking coffee makes people live longer. What would someone have had to actually do, for that sentence to be worth believing?*

**The move:** asking for the warrant instead of arguing with the claim. Most disagreements are about whether something is true; this refuses that and asks what *procedure* would have had to happen. It is the move Munroe says he chose twenty essays for.

**The trap:** treating "I need evidence" as the answer. It is the preface. The work is describing the actual thing someone would have to *do* — who was watched, for how long, compared with whom, and what they would have had to not do. "You'd have to find people who are the same except for the coffee" is real work. "You'd need a study" is not.

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask it. Then ask only: "who would you have to watch, and for how long?" Supply nothing. When something is offered, ask what would go wrong with it — and let the child find the problem that people who already feel healthy are more likely to drink coffee in the first place. Stop there.

**Why it is deliberately yesterday's question from the other side.** Yesterday asked what else might explain a pattern; today asks what would have to be true for a pattern to be worth reporting at all. Same muscle — and worth saying out loud that the two are relatives, because noticing that a new problem is an old problem in new clothes is the thing actually being trained.

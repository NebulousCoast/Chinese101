>

## 4 — Book of the day

### May We Feed the King
Rebecca Perry · Booker Prize 2026 shortlist · Waterstones Debut Fiction Prize

The **last unused title from the Booker Prize 2026 shortlist**, which this file confirmed from thebookerprizes.com's own 2026 pages on 2 November. A debut novel. The 2026 Booker itself went to M. John Harrison's *The End of Everything* on 9 November — covered here on 27 October, thirteen days early.

**The argument**, in the Booker's own words from its page for the book: it **"unsettles the idea that there's ever one universal historical truth."** The judges' commendation describes the structure: a reserved museum curator who recreates historical banquets using a careful array of **fake pies and pomegranates**, set against the story of a medieval king reluctant to occupy the throne and possibly unfit for it anyway. The publisher's description adds that the curator takes a commission on the private quarters of a medieval palace and becomes absorbed in a king almost forgotten by history, hurried to the throne after the sudden deaths of his brothers.

**Three ideas worth taking out of it.** First, **reconstruction is an argument.** A curator deciding what a banquet looked like is making a claim about the past using plaster fruit, and the book puts that next to a narrative of the thing itself so the reader can see the gap. Second, **the nearly-forgotten king** is a deliberate choice of subject: a reign history did not bother to keep is a test case for what the record is *for*. Third, **reluctance as a subject** — a ruler who does not want to rule inverts almost every narrative about power, and pairs oddly well with a curator whose job is to present other people's splendour.

**The dissent, and it is specific rather than general.** The *Michigan Daily*'s reviewer finds it "interesting, but lacking in specificity and clear thematic substance", and makes a concrete complaint: **the historical setting is not fleshed out, and even the general time period of the king's reign goes unmentioned.** For a novel about historical truth, that is the right objection to take seriously rather than wave away. The praise runs the other way: *The Guardian*'s Melissa Harrison calls it an ingenious novel that "deliberately wrongfoots the reader at every turn", and the Booker's own page notes its sumptuous writing about sex and desire — which is the judges, not a review.

**One detail I am not printing as fact:** a single review says the curator enters the medieval world through detailed *ledgers*. It is a nice hook and no second source corroborated it, so take it as one reviewer's reading rather than as the book's mechanism.

**For the 11-year-old: no.** Sumptuous writing about sex and desire is the Booker's own description, and the structure is demanding. The *idea*, though — that the past reaches us only through what somebody wrote down and what happened to survive — is today's thinking question, and it works at eleven.

> **And the slot got refilled today**, which matters because this was the last shortlistee.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, out of today's book.** How would we know about a day that nobody wrote down?

**The move it teaches:** noticing that the past arrives through **two filters, not one**. First, somebody had to record it. Second, the record had to survive. A medieval king "almost forgotten by history" is not a king about whom little happened; he is a king whose paperwork went missing. Those are very different facts and they look identical from here.

**The trap, and it has two sides that fail in opposite directions.** One is reading an absence of evidence as an absence of events — if nothing is written, nothing happened. The other is reading a surviving record as simply true, when what survived is whatever somebody thought worth keeping, which is itself a judgement made by people with interests. The harder question is in the middle: **what kind of thing tends not to get written down?** Push there. Ordinary days. Work done by people who could not write. Anything everybody already knew.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Try it on the household. What would a stranger be able to reconstruct about this week from what physically exists — messages, receipts, photographs, marks on things? Then list three true things about the week that left *no trace at all*. Then ask whether those three are unimportant, or just unrecorded.

**Relative of 22 November, at a different scale.** That day asked what an investigation could have detected, and therefore what its silence was worth. Today asks the same of the historical record — **which is an investigation nobody designed and nobody is running.** Same question, four hundred years long.

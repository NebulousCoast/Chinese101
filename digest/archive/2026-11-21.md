### Nature's Apothecary
Margaret Willes · Bodleian Library Publishing · 288pp · £25 · ISBN 978-1-85124-669-4

Picked up in Nature's *Books in brief* of 6 November 2026 (Andrew Robinson, five titles). **Date conflict:** a UK retailer and an Oxford alumni column give **2 July 2026**; US retailers give 8 September 2026, probably a separate US release. Prefer July for the first edition.

**What it is, and what it is not.** The shape is an extended essay on European herbalism from medieval times to the present, followed by **100 plants, each with a colour illustration taken from John Gerard's *Herball* of 1597**. The central claim is not that these plants work. It is that the medicinal use of plants was a *continuous body of recorded knowledge, transmitted in print*, and that the printed herbal is the thing to study — Gerard, Nicholas Culpeper, Elizabeth Blackwell. An Oxford alumni column puts it precisely: this is **cultural and social history, not a field guide to remedies**. An independent reviewer makes the same point from the other side — it is not a reference book for treatment or clinical work. Both are scope notes rather than complaints, and both are worth knowing before buying.

**Three ideas worth taking out of it.** First, the herbal is a genre with a *commercial* history: Blackwell produced *A Curious Herbal* to pay her husband's debts, which is a fact about the book trade as much as about botany. Second, herbalism is one of the few scientific traditions where **women are present in the record**, as practitioners and as compilers of recipe books — the one place reviewers agree Willes is strongest, and where the independent reviewer says she does a remarkable job of not erasing them. Third, the division between "food" and "medicine" is recent: flowers, fruit and vegetables sit here alongside herbs.

**The dissent, thin but real.** The one reviewer who records a reservation — a plant-history blog, so an individual writing under their own name rather than an editorial outlet, which is worth saying plainly — calls the writing well crafted but says the book **lacks comprehensive sourcing for some claims**. For a book whose argument rests on what the printed record says, that is the right place to press. Everything else reachable is favourable: Hortweek, 6 July 2026, calls it a gorgeous, deeply researched celebration of botanical history. No academic review was reachable, and Nature's own entry is three sentences.

*Limits:* nature.com, hortweek.com, the Oxford alumni site and the blog all refuse to load from this container, so the above is assembled from a search index of those pages. No long review was reachable anywhere — which means the praise is better sourced than the criticism, the opposite of what you want.

**For the 11-year-old:** yes, as an object to browse rather than a book to read through — 100 illustrated entries from a 1597 herbal is a good shape for a child who likes plants. The essay is adult history writing. And it pairs with something free: look up what any one of these plants is now known to do, and what it was claimed to do.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, out of today's book.** How would you tell the difference between a remedy that worked and getting better anyway?

**The move it teaches:** wanting a **comparison** before believing a cause. Willes's 100 plants were used for centuries by people who watched carefully and wrote it down. Some of them do work — willow bark became aspirin, foxglove became digoxin. Some did nothing. From inside a single case the two look identical, because almost everyone who takes anything also gets better eventually.

**The trap:** deciding that the people in the book were credulous. They were not. They had the only evidence available — this person took this and recovered — and that evidence genuinely cannot distinguish the two possibilities, however honest or attentive you are. So the harder question: **what would you have had to do to tell, using nothing they did not have?**

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Pick something in the house that someone believes helps — honey for a cough, a particular way of sitting to concentrate. Ask what would have to happen for them to decide it did nothing. If nothing could, say so out loud and sit with it.

**Relative of yesterday's, and of this morning's correction.** Yesterday asked how you would know, from inside a method, what it has made invisible. Today is the same question with the method removed: how do you know, from inside your own single case, what your evidence cannot show you? And it caught me this morning. NT$700 was true last year — which is a single case repeating itself, not evidence that it still holds.

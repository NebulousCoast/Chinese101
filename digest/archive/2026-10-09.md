*Grade fit:* both children — the 11-year-old for checking arithmetic and seeing a

> **Limit, a real one:** Wolfram will do the problem *for* you. For a student
> preparing for olympiad-style exams, a tool that produces the answer instantly is
> a hazard as much as a help. The honest use is **verification after an attempt,
> never before.** Without that discipline, **the free tier is safer than the paid
> one.**

**Duolingo** — free, with two catches that have grown teeth. The free tier is
ad-supported and now runs an **energy system: 25 energy units a day, draining
whether you answer correctly or not.** That turns the product from "practise as
much as you like, with ads" into "practise a fixed amount per day."

> **Cost — sources genuinely conflict.** Super quoted at roughly **$12.99/month or

> and **$143.99/year** for up to six users. **Duolingo does not publish pricing on

*Grade fit:* the 11-year-old, for a language not already used at home.
*Limit:* very good at vocabulary and daily habit, weak at producing someone who
can converse or read a book. **A warm-up, not a course** — and for a bilingual
household already receiving an English-language digest each morning, the least

## 4. Book of the day

### *Oscar and Lucinda* — Peter Carey (1988)

Winner of the 1988 Booker Prize and the 1989 Miles Franklin Award. Carey won a
second Booker in 2001 for *True History of the Kelly Gang*.

Chosen to close the week on purpose. Five days of books about evidence and
inference — Hofstadter on rhetoric that imitates scholarship, *Abundance* and
*Evicted* arguing one crisis from opposite altitudes, Gordin on why there is no
clean test for science, George on an industry hiding in plain sight. **This is the
same week's question in a novel: how do you act when you cannot know?**

**The central argument.** **Faith and gambling are the same act.** Oscar is a
young Anglican clergyman with a compulsion for betting; Lucinda a young heiress
who buys a glassworks and shares it. Carey's wager — the pun is the book — is that
religious belief and a bet on a horse have an identical structure: you stake
something irrecoverable on an outcome you cannot verify, and you call the staking
by a different name depending on whether you approve of it. The novel takes that
seriously rather than as a joke.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Belief as a wager.** Oscar decides his faith by a method he would be ashamed
   to describe, and the novel refuses to let him off. Pascal argued believing is
   the rational bet; Carey asks what kind of person actually *lives* that way.
2. **Compulsion is not courage, and they look identical from outside.** Both
   characters are brave and both are addicted, and the book never cleanly
   separates the two. For a student learning to take risks on purpose, **the
   distinction between a calculated bet and an inability to stop betting** is the
   one to carry.
3. **Glass.** A material simultaneously strong and brittle, transparent and able
   to cut. The famous image is a church of glass transported through country it
   does not belong in — as good a metaphor for an imported belief system as
   anything in fiction.
4. **The colonial reading, not optional.** Academic work treats the novel as a
   critique of colonial Australia, attentive to violence against Aboriginal
   peoples and the systemic position of women. The glass church is a European
   object carried into someone else's country, and the cost falls on people who
   did not choose it.
5. **Fate, and whether it is really fate.** A sense that the characters are at the
   mercy of something runs underneath — yet every disaster is traceable to a
   decision somebody made. That tension is the engine.

> **Difficult? Moderately.** Long, dense, obliquely narrated — a descendant
> telling a family story, so always slightly at a remove. Reports say it ends
> badly for Oscar following a reckless bet; **the mechanics of the final wager are
> unverified here and will not be described.** It is a tragedy and it earns that.

**Why it matters.** The whole month has been deadlines, probabilities and
decisions under incomplete information — which programme, which fee, which
unverified date to trust. Carey's book is about two people who cannot tell
conviction from compulsion, and it is the most useful possible companion to a
season of applications. **Making a bet is fine. Not knowing that you are making
one is the problem.**

**Suitability:** the elder child, if they read long novels willingly. **Not for
the 11-year-old** — adult themes, length, and an ending that needs an adult reader.

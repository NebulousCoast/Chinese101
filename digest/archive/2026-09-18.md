## 1. Learning platforms

### Desmos and Polypad — both free, and the closest thing to a mathematics laboratory

**Desmos:** graphing calculator, 3D graphing, scientific calculator. Completely
free, no subscription, no account needed, browser-based.

**Polypad** (from Mathigon, now Amplify): the most comprehensive library of
virtual manipulatives online — **50+ of them**, plus dynamic geometry and graph
plotting. No login, no installation, any device or browser. Webinars, tutorials
and lesson plans free too.

**What makes these different from everything else in this rotation:** they are
**tools, not courses**. Nothing is sequenced, nothing is assessed, nobody is
teaching. You pose a question and watch what happens.

> That matters more for a homeschooler than it sounds. The 2026-09-16 digest
> described **the lab gap** — a homeschooled science student has no bench, and
> Labster only partly closes it. **Polypad is the mathematics equivalent, except
> free and better**: a place to manipulate objects and watch structure emerge,
> which is how mathematical intuition is actually built. Textbooks give you
> results; this gives you the thing results are *about*.

**Practical note:** wide age range — Polypad's manipulatives work for an
elementary child; Desmos becomes essential around algebra and indispensable at
calculus.

**Limit:** total freedom is useless without a question to bring to it. These
reward a curious child and do nothing for a passive one. Best alongside a
curriculum, not instead of. <https://www.desmos.com/> ·
<https://polypad.amplify.com/>

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Trial (Der Prozess)* — Franz Kafka (1925)

Ranked **#3** on Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century, behind Camus and Proust.

> **First, something worth noticing.** This is the **fifth book in a row** about
> the same thing, unplanned:
>
> - **Camus** (09-12): Meursault is condemned for failing to weep at his
>   mother's funeral, not for the killing.
> - **Coetzee** (09-14): Lurie is offered restoration if he will perform a
>   repentance he does not feel.
> - **Alexander** (09-17): a formally colorblind system sorts people by race
>   anyway.
> - **Forman** (09-17): racism worked by narrowing the options available, not by
>   stating a rule.
> - **Kafka** (today): a man is prosecuted by a system that never tells him the
>   charge.
>
> Five books, three continents, eighty years, one question: **what happens when
> a system judges you on terms it will not state?**

**The central argument.** Josef K. is arrested one morning and never told what
he is accused of. The novel follows a process with every outward form of justice
— hearings, advocates, documents, officials — and none of its substance. The
German title, *Der Prozess*, means both "the trial" and "the process," and the
double meaning is the book.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The process *is* the punishment.** Nothing is ever decided. K. is consumed
   by procedure that never resolves — a more accurate account of how
   institutional power damages people than any verdict would be.
2. **Bureaucracy in the shape of theology.** The court is everywhere,
   unreachable, its reasoning unknowable — administration given the attributes
   of the divine. Kafka's innovation is noticing these can be the same structure.
3. **Guilt without a charge, which manufactures itself.** K. begins innocent and
   gradually starts behaving like a guilty man. The accusation does not need
   evidence; it only needs time.
4. **"Before the Law"** — the parable at the novel's centre. A man waits his
   whole life at a gate he is never permitted through, and is told at the end
   that the gate was made only for him and is now closing. Access offered and
   withheld in the same gesture.
5. **Complicity.** K. cooperates — attends, engages lawyers, takes the process
   seriously — and the system requires exactly that to function.

**Worth knowing:** the novel is unfinished, assembled and published after
Kafka's death by Max Brod, who ignored explicit instructions to burn it. The
chapter order is partly editorial, and the book's existence is a betrayal of its
author's wishes — uncomfortably on-theme.

**Difficulty, honestly:** the prose is plain and readable; the logic disorients.
Shorter and more approachable than most of this rotation, though the unfinished
structure leaves some chapters sitting oddly. The parable alone is worth the
entry price.

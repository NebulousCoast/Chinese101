### Divination Engines
Natural Language Processing, Artificial Intelligence, and the Making of Algorithmic Culture · Xiaochang Li · University of Chicago Press, 2026

From Nature's *Books in brief* of 23 October 2026 (Andrew Robinson, DOI d41586-026-03395-3). Li is an assistant professor in Communication at **Stanford** and a faculty affiliate in its Science, Technology and Society programme. **Conflicting details, reported rather than resolved:** listings give the publication date as 9, 20 and 27 July 2026, and the page count as 304 or 320 — the publisher's own page is the one to trust.

**The argument.** This is a history of how speech and language stopped being treated as *sound to be decoded* and became **data to be predicted**. Li traces the move from hardware-based signal-processing devices — machines built to recognise the physics of a spoken word — to data-driven statistical systems that work by finding patterns across enormous collections of text. Her central claim is that **the statistical turn was never merely a technical choice**: the problem of mapping human communication onto computation is what made "data-driven" into a privileged and now pervasive form of knowledge. The reviewer in the *International Journal of Communication* puts it well — the book is one answer to the question of how our era became so data-driven.

**Three ideas worth taking out of it.** First, **the title is the argument.** Divination is prediction *without* understanding, and the claim is that this is the shape of the technology rather than an insult to it. Second, **a method that succeeds spreads beyond its subject** — statistical pattern-matching worked on speech, then became the default way of knowing things in domains with nothing to do with language. Third, **the history is not the one usually told**: current arguments about language and AI trace back, as one reviewer puts it, to myriad and sometimes surprising sources in the history of computation, not to the last five years.

**The dissent, from a named organisation rather than a blog.** The **Mathematical Association of America**'s review notes that there is **no elaborate technical discussion** of the principles underlying contemporary AI tools. That is fair and specific: a reader who wants to understand how the systems work will not get it here, because the book is a history of how a way of thinking won, not a textbook. Worth knowing before buying. Praise exists but much of what I can see is *publisher-hosted endorsement* — Wendy Hui Kyong Chun calling it an important book in the history of big data and AI — and by this file's standing rule a publisher-hosted quotation is marketing, not reception. The *International Journal of Communication* review is reception.

> **And the uncomfortable note, which this file has a standing habit of making.** This email is written by one of the systems the book is about. It predicts text; it has not read Li's book; and three of this morning's search results were pages that *predicted plausible answers* rather than reporting true ones. **Today's book is partly an explanation of why those pages exist.**

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, which is today's book's title turned into a sentence.** What is the difference between predicting something and knowing it?

**The move it teaches:** noticing that a very good prediction and an understanding are not the same achievement — and that **the better the prediction gets, the harder they are to tell apart.** A machine that finishes your sentence correctly every time has done something remarkable. Whether it has understood you is a separate question, and it does not get answered by the machine getting better.

**The trap, and both versions are tempting.** One is deciding that accuracy settles it — if it always gets the right answer, it must understand. The other is deciding that it obviously never understands, which is a conclusion rather than an argument. The useful move is to look for a case where the two **come apart**: something that could be predicted reliably by someone who had no idea what was going on, and something that could be understood by someone who could not predict it at all. Both exist. Find one of each.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Start with somebody in the room who can predict another person's behaviour very well — who always knows what a sibling will say next. Ask whether that is the same as understanding them. Then the other way round: name something you understand and cannot predict. *The weather is a good case.*

That run was not planned; it came from whichever book the rotation threw up. **It is a real thread and it is also a rut.** If it starts to feel like a syllabus rather than an argument, say so and the section will change direction.

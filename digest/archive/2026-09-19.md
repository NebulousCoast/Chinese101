## 1. Learning platforms

### Wolfram Alpha — genuinely useful, and the one in this rotation worth a caution

**What it is:** a computational engine. Type a problem in plain language and it
computes, plots, and — on the paid tier — shows the solution step by step.

**Cost:** the free Basic tier is real and useful, but has **no step-by-step
solutions**. Pro is **$9.99/month or about $60/year**; Pro Premium is $99/year.
A 30% student discount exists. ⚠️ Figures from secondary sources — check the
official student pricing page before buying. **Grade fit:** useful from algebra
upward, essential-feeling at calculus. **Independent use:** yes, almost too much
so.

> **The caution, which matters more than the price:** step-by-step solutions are
> the paid feature, and they are a genuine double-edged tool.

- **For:** the student has no teacher marking their work. Wolfram is the only
  immediate feedback available, and seeing *where* a derivation went wrong — not
  just that it did — is real teaching.
- **Against:** it is also the easiest way in this entire rotation to **feel
  competent without becoming competent**. A student who reaches for step-by-step
  before struggling has outsourced exactly the part that builds ability.

This sits in direct tension with AoPS's philosophy, which is that productive
struggle *is* the learning. If Pro is bought, the rule worth setting is:
**attempt fully, then check — never consult first.** A household rule, not a
product feature, and no subscription enforces it.

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Undoing Project: A Friendship That Changed Our Minds* — Michael Lewis (2016)

Recommended for university students by Harvard's Eric Maskin (a Nobel laureate
in economics) and Penn's Maurice Schweitzer.

> **A deliberate pivot.** The 2026-09-18 digest noted that five books running had
> circled one question — what happens when a system judges you on terms it will
> not state. That thread is worth closing rather than flogging. This book opens a
> different one: not how institutions fail people, but **how minds fail
> themselves**.

**The central argument.** The story of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, two
Israeli psychologists whose collaboration demolished the assumption that people
reason about uncertainty the way statistics says they should. Lewis's framing is
that this was not primarily a scientific programme but a *friendship* — two very
different minds producing work neither could have produced alone, and then
losing each other.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The mind substitutes rules of thumb for the laws of chance.** Kahneman and
   Tversky called these **heuristics** — availability, representativeness,
   anchoring, simulation. They are efficient, and they fail in *systematic,
   predictable* directions. That last word is what made this science rather than
   a list of mistakes.
2. **Prospect theory: losses and gains are not symmetric.** People are
   risk-*averse* considering gains and risk-*seeking* facing losses. The same
   choice, described as a loss rather than a gain, flips the decision. This is
   why framing works.
3. **Rationality was an assumption, not a finding.** Economics had built its
   models on a rational agent; they showed the deviations were regular enough to
   be modelled — which is why behavioural economics exists.
4. **The collaboration itself is the subject.** Lewis is more interested in how
   two people thought together than in the papers. For a student that is the
   more useful lesson: the best work here came from **argument between friends**,
   not solitary genius.

> **Why this pairs with the first book this digest ever sent.** On 2026-09-07 it
> covered Schelling's *The Strategy of Conflict*, which reasons about actors
> making strategic choices under uncertainty — and *assumes those actors compute
> correctly*. Kahneman and Tversky show the computation itself is systematically
> bent. Read together they frame a real and unresolved question: **how much of
> strategic theory survives when the agents are actual humans?**

**Postscript worth knowing:** Tversky died in 1996. Kahneman received the Nobel
in 2002 for work they did together; the prize is not awarded posthumously. Lewis
does not soften what that meant, nor the fact that the friendship had already
broken before Tversky's death.

**Accessibility:** this is Michael Lewis, so it reads like narrative non-fiction
rather than psychology. Among the most approachable books this rotation has
offered — and the concepts are load-bearing for anyone heading toward economics,
psychology, medicine or public policy.

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Paranoid Style in American Politics* — Richard Hofstadter (title essay 1964, collection 1965)

Recommended by **Jill Abramson**, former executive editor of *The New York Times*
and lecturer in English at Harvard, as the one book every student should read.
Hofstadter: Columbia historian, twice a Pulitzer winner.

**The central argument.** A recurring *style* in American political rhetoric — not
a mental illness, and Hofstadter is insistent on this — in which conspiracy is
treated as the motive force of history. He is explicit that the people using it
are usually ordinary and often sincere. What makes it dangerous is that it is not
personal: the spokesman believes the plot is aimed at the nation, the culture, a
whole way of life. He is, in his own eyes, *disinterested*.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The defect is in the inference, not the evidence.** Paranoid literature is
   heavily footnoted, obsessively documented, often accurate in its particulars —
   then takes one unjustified leap from real details to a curdled conclusion. The
   style imitates scholarship. **Spotting the exact sentence where the leap
   happens is transferable**, and it is the skill a research application tests.
2. **The enemy is a perfect model of malice.** Not a rival with interests but an
   amoral superman: sinister, ubiquitous, powerful, cruel, and somehow also about
   to be defeated. Real opponents are never this tidy. Tidiness is the tell.
3. **The accuser imitates the accused.** Counter-conspiracies, front
   organisations, secrecy, pseudo-scholarly apparatus — anti-Masonic and
   anti-Catholic movements copying what they claimed to fight.
4. **Apocalyptic framing destroys bargaining.** A final war between absolute good
   and evil makes compromise betrayal; the style cannot do the ordinary work of
   politics, which is trading.
5. **Recurrent, and not one side's property.** The anti-Illuminati panic of the
   1790s, anti-Masonry, nativism, strands of Populist rhetoric, McCarthy, the John
   Birch Society. The point is structural: it surfaces wherever a group feels
   **dispossessed**. That diagnosis of status anxiety is his real contribution.

> **Read it with the objections in hand.** Hofstadter has been criticised heavily
> for **pathologising dissent** — a consensus-minded 1960s liberalism treating
> genuine grievance as a symptom. His Populists are the usual example and later
> historians have largely gone against him there. And since real conspiracies do
> occur, "paranoid" can be used lazily, as a way of not answering. His framework
> survives only if you hold him to it: *the marker is the unjustified leap, never
> the mere fact of suspicion.*

**Why today.** US midterms are a month away, which makes this live rather than
historical. More practically: October will be spent writing application prose that
has to separate what the evidence shows from what the writer wants it to show.
Hofstadter is a forty-page lesson in watching someone fail at that, at scale.

**Accessibility.** The title essay is a long magazine piece and stands alone —
start and possibly stop there. The rest of the collection is denser and more
dated. For a high schooler who reads nonfiction willingly; not for a fifth-grader.

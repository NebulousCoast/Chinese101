## 4 — Book of the day

**_Wolf Hall_, Hilary Mantel, 2009. #3 on the New York Times 100 Best Books of the 21st Century; Booker Prize 2009.**

Rotation index 1 (NYT 100 Best Books of the 21st Century).

**The rank, and a cross-check that settles something I printed without one.** A roundup of the Times' top ten gives the order as 1. _My Brilliant Friend_ (Ferrante), 2. _The Warmth of Other Suns_ (Wilkerson), 3. _Wolf Hall_ — and a Substack devoted to the novel says the same. That matters beyond today, because **_The Warmth of Other Suns_** was this rotation's book on 11 September and I recorded it with no rank at all. It is **number 2**. The Times' top ten in this file is now: 1 Ferrante, 2 Wilkerson, 3 Mantel, 7 Whitehead, 8 Sebald, 9 Ishiguro, 10 Robinson — seven of ten confirmed across nine weeks.

And the usual trap, which this book demonstrates better than any other: **the Guardian's own 100-best list put _Wolf Hall_ first, and a Vulture list placed it sixth.** Three different lists, three different numbers, one book. Always check which list a rank belongs to.

**What the book is.** England, 1500 to 1535. Thomas Cromwell, a blacksmith's son, rises from nothing to become the most powerful man in Henry VIII's government, and engineers the king's divorce and the break with Rome. It is narrated over Cromwell's shoulder in the present tense, which sounds like a gimmick and is in fact the whole machine.

**Three ideas.**

1. **Power is administrative rather than dramatic.** Mantel's Cromwell wins by knowing where documents are, who owes whom money, and what people said when they thought no one was listening. The book's argument is that history is made by people doing paperwork competently, and that we misremember it as made by speeches because speeches survive better.
2. **The present tense as an epistemology.** Because we are inside Cromwell's head as things happen, we never know more than he does. There is no hindsight, no irony from the future, no sense that the outcome was fated. It is the most effective demonstration I know of what it is like to act without knowing how it turns out — which is the condition of every decision anyone actually makes.
3. **A villain is usually someone else's protagonist.** Cromwell has been the villain of this story for four centuries, and Thomas More its saint. Mantel simply changes whose shoulder we stand behind, and the moral furniture rearranges itself without a single fact being altered. **Point of view is not a decoration, it is a claim.**

**And the dissent, which is unusually serious.**

- The Catholic objection is loud and specific. The Bishop of Shrewsbury, Mark Davies, called the 2015 adaptation "an extraordinary and perverse achievement" and said it presented More as a scheming villain, adding that _Wolf Hall_ "is not neutral." Catholic bishops publicly disputed the portrayal.
- The substantive version of the complaint is better than the indignant one. Critics argue the novels **invert** the traditional roles — Cromwell becomes "the apostle of humanist tolerance" and More "the hate-filled prophet of religious fanaticism" — and that this overstates More's part in the burning of heretics; one essayist counts only six cases in which More played a direct or indirect role.
- The sharpest scholarly objection is about sources: that Mantel leans on G.R. Elton's mid-century characterisations of Cromwell and More, so that the novels read "as if the last thirty years of research in the Tudor period never happened."
- Against all that, defenders credit her with going back to original documentation, and one reviewer argues she persuades readers that historians had not done Cromwell justice. The honest summary is that **historians remain split**, and that More's own writings support a harsher reading of him than his hagiography allows.

**The limit on all of this:** the strongest criticism I could find comes from Catholic outlets and from bishops, which is a partisan source on precisely this question, and I did not reach a systematic academic review. Treat the historiographical dispute as real and the characterisations above as contested rather than settled.

**Suitability.** The elder child, yes, emphatically — and the dissent above is the reason, not an obstacle to it. Reading a brilliant novel alongside the serious case that it is unfair to a real person is one of the few ways to learn that those two things can be true at once. For the 11-year-old, no: the prose is difficult, the pronouns are famously confusing even to adults, and the content includes executions.

## 6 — International collaboration (even-numbered dates)

**eBird, run by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology — and it clears a bar nothing else in this section has cleared.**

**What it is.** You record which birds you saw, where, when, and how many, and submit it as a checklist. The checklists become a research database. Cornell's own news service reports that eBird data have been used in **more than 1,250 studies published in scientific journals**, and that the Lab passed two billion bird sightings in August 2025.

**The thing that makes it different.** eBird publishes a **recommended citation format for an individual checklist**, in which the observer's name occupies the author position — "Lastname, Firstname" — exactly as an author is cited in a paper. The project also surfaces the name of the observer who **first reports** a species in a region.

Put against the rest of this section's pool, that is a tier of its own:

- **Zooniverse and Mozilla Common Voice:** no establishable individual credit at all.
- **Foldit and Eterna:** collective or mass authorship on papers, which is real but not personal.
- **Distributed Proofreaders:** a certificate stating pages completed, which is documentation rather than citation.
- **eBird: a citable record under the child's own name.** An eleven-year-old can produce something that is cited the way scientific data is cited, this week, with a pair of binoculars and a phone.

**Why Taiwan helps rather than hinders.** Taiwan sits on the East Asian–Australasian Flyway and its bird list is both rich and intensively watched, so records here are joining a serious body of local data rather than filling a void. I could **not** verify how Taiwan-submitted checklists are handled, nor confirm any relationship between eBird and 中華民國野鳥學會, and I am not going to assert one.

**The limits, stated plainly.**

- I could not establish a **minimum age** or any account-age rule. Check before registering a child's own account; the obvious alternative is that the parent holds the account and the child does the observing, which loses the citation benefit and is worth weighing against it.
- The eBird observation dataset is also published through GBIF under the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, and **I could not find the licence terms.** Do not assume free reuse of the data.
- The honest proportion: a checklist is citable, not important. Most checklists are never individually cited by anybody, and saying otherwise would be the same inflation I have spent a month stripping out of other items.

The first step costs nothing: an account, a local park, and a list of what was there. The skill it actually teaches is the one section 8 has been circling all week — looking carefully at something and writing down exactly what you saw rather than what you expected.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question.** The same story can be told with either person as the hero. If both versions get every fact right, is one of them still better?

**The move it teaches:** noticing that **selection and framing are claims**, not neutral containers for facts. A perfectly accurate account still had to decide whose shoulder to stand behind, what to begin with, and what to leave out — and each of those decisions argues for something.

**The trap:** concluding that every telling is equally valid because they are all just perspectives. That is the comfortable relativist exit and it fails immediately on examples — a history of a famine told from the point of view of the people who caused it *can* get every fact right and still be a bad account. So the real question: **what makes one framing better than another, if not accuracy?** Candidates worth arguing over: the one that explains more, the one that accounts for people who had no power to leave records, the one that would survive being told to everyone involved.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Ask it. Use a household argument if nothing comes — two people's accounts of the same disagreement, both honest. Then the hard question, and leave it open.

**Relative of this week, and of today's book.** _Wolf Hall_ is four hundred years of villain and saint, swapped by changing nothing but the vantage point — and the serious objection to it is precisely that the vantage point does work that the facts do not license. Wednesday asked when being inaccurate is permitted; today asks whether being accurate is enough. They are the two halves of one question.

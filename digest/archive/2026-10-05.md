## 1. Interactive learning platforms

**Exercism** — **100% free, forever**. Independent, community-funded
not-for-profit. **83 language tracks, 8,500+ exercises.** Solve problems in a real
language, submit, and **volunteer human mentors review the solution** and suggest
idiomatic improvements. Free personalised code review is close to unique in any
free tier. <https://exercism.org/>

The counterexample to this digest's own claim two days ago: the usual trade is
"free content, no feedback loop" and Exercism breaks it. It also produces the
**visible, accumulating evidence of competence** the computational-research path
depends on — solved problems with commentary from working developers.
*Grade fit:* no age limit; a motivated 11-year-old can start the Python track, a
high schooler can go as deep as they like.
*Limit:* mentoring is volunteer-run, so response times vary and popular tracks
queue. Teaches idiomatic programming, not algorithms or competition technique —
not a CodeWOOT substitute.

**CK-12** — **free**, a nonprofit publishing open educational resources. FlexBooks
are customisable digital textbooks across maths and science for K–12, enriched
with simulations, videos, interactive activities, adaptive practice and quizzes.
**A parent can take a FlexBook and customise it** — cut, reorder, add — which is
unusual and genuinely useful for homeschooling. <https://www.ck12.org/>
*Grade fit:* K–12, so it serves both children from one place, which almost nothing
else here does.
*Limit:* breadth over depth. A solid curriculum spine, useless for olympiad work.
Pair with Omega Learn or AoPS for the ceiling.

> For the 11-year-old, **CK-12 + Polypad + North Star rounds is a complete, free,
> genuinely interactive package.** There is no good argument for paying anything at
> that age yet.

## 4. Book of the day

### *Evicted: Poverty and Profit in the American City* — Matthew Desmond (2016)

Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction. On the NYT list; no rank asserted, none
verified.

Chosen to sit **directly against yesterday's book**, and the pairing is the point.
Klein and Thompson argued American scarcity is chosen and the answer is to build.
Desmond, a Princeton sociologist, spent years in Milwaukee — living in a trailer
park and a rooming house, following eight families and their landlords —
documenting what that scarcity does to the people inside it. Together the two
books argue about one crisis **at completely different altitudes**, and that gap
is the lesson.

**The central argument.** Eviction is not a symptom of poverty; it is a **cause**
of it. The conventional story: people become poor, then lose housing. Deshmond
shows the arrow also running the other way — losing a home costs possessions, job,
children's schooling, neighbours, standing with future landlords — so eviction
*manufactures* the deeper poverty that produces the next eviction. And
exploitation is profitable: there is serious money at the bottom of the rental
market, which is why it persists.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Housing is the hinge.** Once rent consumes most of a family's income, nothing
   else can be stabilised. Housing instability sits upstream of outcomes usually
   attributed to other causes.
2. **The arrow of causation runs both ways.** The analytic core, and the thing to
   keep: *a correlation you assumed ran one direction may run in a loop.*
3. **Exploitation, not just neglect.** Poor tenants often pay as much or more than
   better-off ones for far worse housing — a market working as designed.
4. **It happens in the private rental market.** Landlords appear as people with
   incentives rather than villains, which makes the account harder to dismiss and
   harder to fix.
5. **Method as argument.** Desmond lived there, for years, and published his data
   and methods openly. For a student thinking about research: **the claim is
   strong because the evidence was gathered in a way that could have proved him
   wrong.**

> **Difficult and contested.** Genuinely harrowing content — families with young
> children put on the street, addiction, violence, a death. Critics question how
> far eight Milwaukee families generalise to a national argument, and some
> economists dispute his reading of the rental market's profitability. His policy
> proposal, universal housing vouchers, is contested in both directions — and
> **yesterday's book would say vouchers without new supply simply raise rents.**
> Hold both at once; neither author would concede.

**Why it matters here.** *Abundance* makes its case from above, with systems and
statistics; *Evicted* from below, with eight families and a notebook. Both honest,
both incomplete — and **the strongest writing knows which altitude it is operating
at and says so.** More sophisticated than "cite your sources", and learnable from
reading these two in one week.

**Accessibility.** Narrative nonfiction, ~400 pages, no background needed, very
readable. **Not for the 11-year-old** — not because it is difficult but because it
is distressing, and the distress is the honest part.

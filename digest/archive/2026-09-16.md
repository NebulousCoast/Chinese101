## 1. Learning platforms

### Labster — and the gap it points at

**What it is:** virtual laboratory simulations — **303 of them** — split across
high school, university and professional levels, covering biology, chemistry and
physics. You run procedures, make choices, and see experiments fail.

**Cost:** around **$109/year** for single-user access, available to individual
homeschoolers. ⚠️ That figure comes from secondary sources and Labster's own
pricing page is quote-based — confirm before buying. **Grade fit:** has a
dedicated high school tier. **Independent use:** yes. **Interactivity:**
genuinely high — simulation, not video.

> **The structural point, which matters more than the product:** a homeschooled
> student aiming at RSI-calibre science **has no laboratory**. That is a real
> gap, not a cosmetic one. Selective research programs select for students who
> can work at a bench, and "I have done no wet lab work" is harder to write
> around than a weak test score.

Labster partially closes it: it teaches procedure, apparatus and experimental
logic, and lets a student fail safely. What it *cannot* give is manual
technique, the texture of things going wrong for boring reasons, or a supervisor
who has watched you work and can write about it.

Which is the same conclusion this digest keeps reaching from different
directions: **bench time in a real lab — via the mother's UT department — fills
exactly the gap no subscription closes.** Labster is a good stopgap and a poor
substitute.

## 4. Book of the day

### *Culpability* — Bruce Holsinger (2025)

Bill Gates's fiction pick for summer 2026.

**The setup.** A family road trip ends when their self-driving minivan strikes an
oncoming car; two elderly people in the other vehicle are killed. Noah Cassidy
was in the front passenger seat working on his laptop; his **seventeen-year-old
son Charlie** was in the driver's seat. His wife Lorelei — a MacArthur "genius"
grant winner whose academic field is *the ethics of AI* — and their two daughters
are injured.

**The central argument.** When an autonomous system causes harm, blame does not
land anywhere cleanly. The novel's question is who is culpable — the van, the
software, the company that built it, the teenager nominally at the wheel, or the
conditions that put all of them there — and its discipline is that it **refuses
to answer**.

**Key ideas:**

1. **"Human in the loop" can be a moral fiction.** Charlie is in the driver's
   seat and is not driving. The arrangement assigns him responsibility without
   giving him control — precisely how a great many automated systems are designed
   to distribute liability.
2. **Causation and culpability come apart.** Something caused the crash; that
   does not settle who is blameworthy. Our intuitions, built for a world of
   direct human action, handle the gap badly.
3. **Expertise does not protect you.** Lorelei studies exactly this problem
   professionally. It is no help at all when it is her own family — the novel's
   quietest and most cutting observation.
4. **Blame is a family dynamic before it is a legal one.** The apportioning
   happens at the dinner table, in silences and accusations, long before any
   court.
5. **It refuses to be a thesis novel.** Reviewers note Holsinger keeps his
   characters flawed and contradictory rather than turning them into mouthpieces
   — which is why it works as a novel rather than an op-ed with dialogue.

> **Why it matters, and why now:** pair it with *The Alignment Problem*
> (2026-09-13). That book is the argument; this is the lived version. Read
> together, an abstract problem becomes one with a family in it.

**And for a teenager specifically:** a seventeen-year-old in the driver's seat of
a car that is driving itself is not a subtle mirror. This student is roughly at
the age where they will start being handed responsibility for systems they do not
control.

**Note:** fiction, and a thriller — it moves. Considerably easier reading than
most of what this rotation has served up.

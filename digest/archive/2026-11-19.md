## 4 — Book of the day

**_Sniff: A History of Smells_ by Will Tullett.** Yale University Press, 2026.

Rotation index 4 (Nature & Science editors' picks).

**Where it comes from, and the queue did its job.** One of the five titles Andrew Robinson covered in Nature's "Books in brief" of **6 November 2026**, which I listed on 13 November and set aside. Second of that batch used; one remains.

**A name and a date to get right.** The Nature listing gave the author as *William* Tullett; the book itself is published as **Will Tullett**, and that is the form to use. On the date: **Yale's own page says 8 September 2026** and a retailer says 27 October 2026 — the publisher's own date is the one to trust, and I am reporting both rather than quietly picking. **Who is accountable:** Tullett is a historian of smell at the **University of York**, writing in his own research area rather than reporting on someone else's.

**And the queue is replenished in the same pass**, which is the procedure I set myself on Sunday. From the 23 October installment, three of its five titles now identified: **_The Age of Cures_** by Barry Werth (Simon & Schuster); **_Divination Engines_** by Xiaochang Li (University of Chicago Press), a cultural history of technology and communication; **_The Matter of Architecture_** by Paul Dobraszczyk (Reaktion). A fourth is a quantum-physics book by the physicist **Danni Holmes** whose title I could not establish. One installment, on Darwin and clouds, is still unextracted.

**The central argument, and the subtitle undersells it.** This is not a catalogue of historical smells. The publisher's own framing is the giveaway: it offers "biographies of the many noses that have sniffed them." **The subject is not smells but smellers** — how the capacity to notice has itself changed, which makes it a history of *perception* rather than of air.

**Three ideas.**

1. **That we have *lost* olfactory sensitivity, and that the causes are material rather than cultural** — pollution, pandemics, poverty, industry and commerce. Not "people stopped caring about smell" but "the conditions that trained noses were removed."
2. **A contradiction he identifies in his own field**, which is the most intellectually honest thing in the package. From his earlier research: the standard histories of smell argue *both* that smell became less important in a period *and* that urban space was deodorised in that same period. Those claims pull against each other — if the air got blander, the historian needs to explain why that would make noses matter *less* rather than more. **Spotting that a field's two favourite claims are in tension is exactly the move this household is trying to teach.**
3. **Smell as a weapon and not a metaphor.** There is a chapter on the history of stink bombs in warfare. Smell is used politically — to mark people, places and classes as foul — and that is a *use* rather than an association.

And the line most likely to stay with a reader, from the *Literary Review*: that modern life can leave us in "an olfactory world of **simulacra** in which anything can smell like anything else."

**The dissent, and it is the structural kind rather than disagreement.**

- **The Spectator Australia** calls the book accessible but says it has "a teacherly whiff about it" — and locates the problem precisely, in the **smell-by-smell structure**, which produces what the reviewer calls a repetitive narrative rhythm as the chronology restarts for each new odour. **A criticism of the architecture rather than the claims:** a good book organised in a way that works against it.
- **Kirkus** was mildly favourable — "a diverting study of what the nose knows", which is praise with its hands in its pockets.

**The limit, and it is the honest shape of today's reception:** I found **no full-length critical review beyond the Spectator piece.** The other favourable material is a **cover blurb** and an excerpt of the *Literary Review* hosted by the publisher's own publicity page — **which is not reception, it is marketing**, and I am not going to present it as the former. So: one real review, mildly critical on structure; everything else thin.

**Suitability. The elder child, yes**, and it is unusually good for a specific purpose: a short, accessible book whose own field's internal contradiction is visible on the page. **For the 11-year-old — the best answer in weeks.** Not the whole book, but the subject is entirely accessible, there is nothing distressing in it, and the activity it suggests costs nothing (see section 1). **A history of smell is the rare academic book an eleven-year-old can have a real opinion about by the end of the first chapter.**

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, from the book.** Why can you not describe a smell without naming something else?

**The move it teaches:** noticing when a difficulty belongs to **the world**, to **our language**, or to **us**. Colours have their own words — red is not "the colour of blood", it is *red*. Smells almost never do: lemony, smoky, musty, *of* rain. Three candidate explanations, and they are not the same kind of claim: smells may have no natural categories to name; English may simply have failed to build the vocabulary; or our noses may not discriminate finely enough to need one.

**The trap:** settling for "because smell is primitive". That is a label standing in for an argument. **The test that breaks it:** some languages *do* have abstract smell words, and some people — perfumers, sommeliers — build working vocabularies. If it can be done, the limit is not in the nose.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Everyone smells the same thing — coffee, a cut orange, rain on pavement — and describes it **without saying what it is or what it is like**. Watch how fast everyone cheats. Then ask whether the failure was the language's or theirs.

**Relative of yesterday's, and the pair is deliberate.** Yesterday asked where understanding lives — in the content or in the reading. Today asks whether something can be **known and not sayable**, which is the same question from underneath: if you can smell a thing precisely and cannot describe it at all, what exactly do you have?

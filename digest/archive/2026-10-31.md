## 4 · Book of the day

***Our Declaration: A Reading of the Declaration of Independence in Defense of Equality* — Danielle Allen.** 2014. The **2016 Princeton Pre-read**.

**Provenance, verified from Princeton's own newsroom.** Eisgruber selected it on 2 May 2016 for the incoming Class of 2020, calling it "a profound and engaging book that addresses liberty, equality, and the meaning of political documents." Allen is a Princeton alumna, Class of 1993, and was at Harvard when the book was chosen; she spoke at Opening Exercises in September 2016, with a book panel on 29 September. The fourth Pre-read in this rotation whose selection is confirmed from the university rather than a reading list.

**What the book is.** Allen reads the Declaration **one sentence at a time** across roughly three hundred pages. The method came out of teaching it to adult night-school students, and that origin shows: it assumes no background and refuses to summarise.

**Three ideas.**
1. **The central argument:** liberty and equality are not in tension, and in the Declaration **equality has precedence over freedom**. Genuinely contrarian about a document most readers were taught to read as being primarily about liberty, and the whole book is the case for it.
2. **The Declaration was written by a committee**, not by Jefferson. Allen treats the collaborative drafting as essential rather than incidental: the text is what group argument produced, and reading it as one man's prose misreads what kind of object it is.
3. **Why it is here today:** the book demonstrates *slow reading* as an argumentative method. Allen's claim is that the document's meaning is recoverable from the document, by attention, without an authority to supply it — yesterday's question answered in book form. *What makes one reading better than another when there is no answer key?* The one that accounts for more of what is actually on the page.

**The dissent, which is substantial.**
- **Steven B. Smith** (*New York Times*) calls the group-writing argument her *least plausible* assertion: a "collective mind" would undercut itself by Allen's own standards, and he asks whose Declaration is described. His sharpest line: Allen's case for a more robustly egalitarian Declaration makes the book timely, and **timeliness is not truth**.
- **Diana Schaub** (*Claremont Review of Books*) reads Allen as treating equality as a purely human creation. Allen replied directly, arguing "we are blessed with a natural equality" consisting of both moral equality and political capacity. Schaub also pressed on who the "our" refers to. **The exchange, rather than either piece alone, is what to read.**
- A *History News Network* reviewer complains Allen "sometimes seems to forget that Jefferson was not the author," and questions how several illustrations relate to the argument.

**The limit:** these reviews were read through the search index rather than in full — their claims, not their reasoning. Kirkus and a Key Reporter review are favourable; Smith and Schaub are the serious opposition.

**Suitability.** The elder child, yes, and more usefully than most books here: a worked example of what a strong application essay must do — take one text or problem and get something out of it the reader had not noticed, with evidence. The **11-year-old, no** for the book, but the *method* transfers immediately: take one paragraph of anything and spend twenty minutes on it.

## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *You are told a rule you cannot follow. Does it matter **why** the rule exists?*

**The move:** distinguishing a rule that is a **limit** from one that is a **ration**. "No one over fifteen" is a limit; no amount of asking changes it. "Only local students, because we have run out of chairs" is a ration — and rations have edges, waiting lists, and exceptions when a chair appears. They look identical from outside, and the difference decides whether asking is worth anything.

**The trap:** deciding all rules are secretly negotiable, the mirror of deciding none are. Both save you the work. The work is finding out which kind you face — by **asking for the reason** rather than for an exception, because a reason tells you what kind of rule it is, while an exception request only tells you whether this person feels generous today.

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask it. Use a household rule if nothing comes — bedtime, or screens — and ask which kind it is. Then the hard half: *can you tell from the outside?* Leave it there.

**Why today.** Not abstract in this household: two of the obstacles in section 7 turn out to be rations rather than limits, and that was only noticed because one page happened to state its reason.

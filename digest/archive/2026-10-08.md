## 1. Interactive learning platforms

Two platforms whose honest answer is "**the pricing is fog, and here is what to do
about it**."

**edX and Coursera audit tracks.** Same model on both: audit free, pay for graded
work and a certificate.

> **edX:** auditing is **free** for open courses, explicitly excepting
> Professional Education, Boot Camp and Executive Education. Audit gives no
> certificate and no graded work. Verified certificates: **edX's own help centre**
> says "usually between $90 and $300"; **edX's own free-courses page** says they
> start at **$50** — the platform's two pages disagree. Audit access is
> **time-limited to the course run**, typically 4–12 weeks, so not a permanent
> library the way MIT OpenCourseWare is.
>
> **Coursera:** whether auditing is still free is **genuinely unclear**. Several
> 2026 guides say yes; the most recent found says Coursera quietly ended most free
> auditing in late 2024, replaced by a preview of roughly the first module.
> **Check the specific course's enrolment page.** Paid: individual courses
> $29–99; Coursera Plus $59/month or $399/year.

One bright spot, flagged honestly: a guide reports **Harvard's CS50x is genuinely
free including the certificate** on completing the problem sets and final project.
Not confirmed on Harvard's own page — but CS50x is worth a look regardless of the
certificate.

*Limit for both:* university lecture courses are the least interactive thing in
this digest — video plus auto-graded quizzes, no mentor, no cohort. A certificate
from either carries very little weight with the section-2 programmes. **Audit for
the content; do not buy the paper.**

**Labster** — virtual laboratory simulations, looked at because of the real
problem it claims to solve: the elder child has **no wet-lab access**. The honest
finding is that **no price can be given**.

> A homeschool reseller lists a single-student annual high-school subscription at
> **"from $150" against a $599 list price**, with no way to tell if the discount
> is live; **Labster's own compare-plans page quotes $3,000/year** for 10
> simulations for up to 50 students, on a page roughly **566 days old**;
> aggregators quote **$6,049/year** and **€149/month** without saying per
> institution or per seat; older reports say **$79–109 per student**. Two orders of
> magnitude. Labster does not publish individual pricing and routes to sales.

> **Judgement: do not chase this.** PhET is free, genuinely interactive, and
> covers much of the same intuition-building ground. More importantly **a
> simulated lab does not solve the credential problem** — no selective programme
> is impressed by simulated bench work, whereas computational research produces
> real output. If pursuing it anyway, ask sales for an individual quote and
> whether the 30-day trial still exists.

## 4. Book of the day

### *Every Last Fish* — Rose George (2026)

*The subtitles differ by edition:* UK (Granta) *What Fish Do for Us and What We Do
to Them*; US (Norton) *A Deep Dive into Everything They Do for Us and We Do to
Them*. Same book.

**The central argument.** The global fishing industry is one of the largest systems
on earth that almost nobody looks at directly — and when you look, the legal and
illegal parts turn out to be *entangled* rather than separate. George reports from
inside both: the vast legal industries behind fish fingers and salmon sandwiches,
and the equally colossal illegal trade whose practices go unmonitored. Her method
is the one this digest keeps praising: go there, talk to the people doing the work,
let the system reveal itself through them.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Effort is the hidden measure of collapse.** We now spend roughly **twice as
   much effort** to catch the same number of fish as in the 1950s. Catch totals
   can look stable while the stock is destroyed, because we compensate with more
   boats, bigger nets, more fuel. **A stable number concealing a collapsing
   system** is a transferable lesson about statistics.
2. **Bycatch is not an accident; it is a design feature with a euphemism.** The
   industry term is "discards." Around **300,000 whales and dolphins** are killed
   every year as unintended catch. Notice the vocabulary doing work — the same move
   Hofstadter and Gordin were about last week.
3. **Farming has overtaken fishing.** For the first time in history humans eat
   more farmed fish than wild, and consumption is projected to rise.
4. **Illegality is routine, not marginal.** About **one in five fish imported into
   the United States is illegally caught**; the book documents fish-laundering,
   dredging and shipboard slavery. The labour story sits alongside the ecological
   one and George refuses to separate them.
5. **The people are the point.** Mostly men who fish, mostly women who process the
   catch and hold fishing communities together.

> **Contested — the sharpest objection**, one reader's and fair enough to pass on:
> the book is a *tasting plate of everything wrong* and so leaves out **what has
> worked** — the fisheries that recovered because fishermen and scientists
> collaborated. A catalogue of harms is not an analysis. Read it asking *"what
> succeeded, and why isn't it here?"*
> The *Literary Review* notes, with affection, that George does not eat fish and
> gets seasick on boats. *Kirkus* called it humane and impressively researched, and
> said it might not stop you buying seafood but will make you think twice.

> **Suitability — different this time.** **Not** for an 11-year-old as a whole;
> shipboard slavery and industrial killing are handled directly. **But the subject
> is exactly right for a younger child**, and section 5 has a free worldwide
> ocean-conservation contest with a division for exactly her age. The move is not
> to hand over the book — it is to let the book inform what an adult tells her
> about where fish come from, **and then let her make something.**

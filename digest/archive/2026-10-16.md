## 1. Interactive learning platforms

Today: the two free pillars of the physics-olympiad track, since Taiwan's olympiad pipeline is now half of the elder child's entire study-abroad strategy. One has no institutional accountability; the other is a university department.

**Physoly — free, and community-built.** A resource hub of notes, handouts and problems from olympiads worldwide, aimed at high school students, with a Discord of thousands of students preparing for physics competitions. Its best-known asset is its solution manuals to Jaan Kalda's handouts — the mechanics manual alone covers 86 problems and 74 "ideas." https://physoly.tech/ and https://physoly.tech/kalda/

**The disclosure:** credited to "Members of Physoly," no named founder or organisation, contributions invited by email, Kalda solutions begun as an AoPS community pilot project with most written by volunteers on a private forum. Strong peer work, not an answer key. If a solution and the student disagree, that is a real question, not a correction.

**The distinction that matters:** the *handouts* are not community work. They are Jaan Kalda's, a long-standing olympiad trainer, freely distributed from his own page at ioc.ee/~kalda/ipho along with an IPhO formula sheet. The problems have authority; the solutions are crowd-written. Handouts as the spine, Physoly manuals as a check, in that order.

Unconfirmed: whether every Physoly resource is free. The Kalda pages are.

**PhET Interactive Simulations — free, and the contrast case.** Run by the **University of Colorado Boulder** — a named department at a named university, worth saying out loud after two disclosures in four days. Interactive maths and science simulations across physics, chemistry, biology and earth science, usable in a browser or **offline** through a desktop app. https://phet.colorado.edu/

Numbers, with conflicts: the most recent source found says 170+ simulations in 130+ languages; a 2018 CU Boulder slide deck said 140+; a 2017 one said 100+ and 59 languages. Growth explains the spread, but PhET's own current figure did not surface. **Licence conflict:** Wikipedia says CC BY-NC 4.0; a 2017 CU Boulder deck says plain CC BY. Check the site before reusing anything publicly.

**The limit**, unchanged from the Labster verdict: a simulation is not a laboratory and solves no credential problem. What PhET is good for is the thing this household cannot buy — letting an eleven-year-old break something expensive repeatedly, and letting a seventeen-year-old see a mechanism before meeting its equations.

## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 0 → GatesNotes.

***Gray Matters: A Biography of Brain Surgery* — Theodore H. Schwartz** (2024). A practising neurosurgeon; his first book for a general audience. **On provenance:** Kirkus reported that Gates's summer 2026 list included a title by Theodore H. Schwartz, and Schwartz's own site lists Bill Gates among the book's endorsers alongside Sanjay Gupta. *Gray Matters* is Schwartz's book. The chain holds, but it is a chain, not a direct citation.

**The central argument.** The subtitle is doing real work: this is a *biography of a discipline*, not a memoir of a surgeon. Schwartz's claim is that neurosurgery became what it is through a long sequence of confident errors, and that the field's defining characteristic is not brilliance but the narrowness of the margin it operates in — where being wrong is not a setback but a person.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The field's history is its mistakes.** Reviewers single out Schwartz's honesty about past errors, held alongside the profession's progress. No march of triumphs.
2. **The patient and the organ are the same thing.** Operating on a brain is operating on the person it constitutes — which is why the book moves between technical description and philosophy without changing gear.
3. **Contested science, named as contested.** CTE and shaken baby syndrome both get substantial treatment, both areas where medicine and the courtroom have pulled in different directions.
4. **What is coming, and the restraint to say it is not here.** Emerging technologies, implants among them, with the question of what should be put into a healthy brain left open.
5. **Training as formation.** Candour about his own training and his family's experience of brain disease — the two things that made this book possible rather than a textbook.

**Reception, and the one criticism worth carrying.** Starred reviews from Kirkus ("a remarkable account of a crucial but misunderstood field") and *Publishers Weekly*; reviewers repeatedly call it as close to a page-turner as a detailed science book gets. The dissent, from a reader rather than a major outlet: Schwartz leans fairly hard into a **determinist** reading of modern neuroscience. That reader still rated the book highly. It is an argument, not a flaw, but it should be noticed being made rather than absorbed.

**Why it matters here.** This digest keeps saying "no wet-lab access" as though it were small. It is not: the gap between reading science and doing it is the gap this book is about. Schwartz's answer is that the doing is learned by apprenticeship, under someone accountable, over years — exactly what the JEI mentor requirement and the UT department conversation are reaching for, and exactly what no platform in section 1 can supply.

**Suitability:** the elder child, yes — accessible, no medical background needed, genuinely useful to anyone considering medicine. For the 11-year-old, **no**: unsparing surgical and clinical detail, and the shaken baby syndrome material is grim.

### A. The World Scholar's Cup

The first item found that serves **both children in the same event**, and the first with a round inside Taiwan.

**The shape:** teams of **three** scholars competing in four events — team debate, **collaborative writing**, the Scholar's Challenge, and the Scholar's Bowl. Regional round → Global Round → Tournament of Champions at Yale, with teams needing to exceed a points threshold at a Global or Mini-Global Round to reach Yale. One school's account of a recent Global Round describes roughly 8,000 scholars narrowing to about 2,000 at Yale.

**Divisions and ages:** Junior Division is for scholars born on or after 1 July 2013, who must be at least 10 at the regional round — fits the eleven-year-old. The elder child would be in the senior division. Two children, one event, different divisions.

**Taiwan:** there *is* a round — **Hsinchu**, which the official calendar lists as a Taiwan city usually visiting in **March**. The 2026 round ran 21–22 March. **2027 dates are not yet announced**, and the calendar flags estimated dates as the host's guess until confirmed. https://www.scholarscup.org/calendar/

**The catch, and it is the familiar one.** General rules allow mixed and **independent** teams — usually one school, but not necessarily. However, the 2026 Hsinchu round stated that only students **attending school in Taiwan** could register, because of capacity limits. Those two facts point in opposite directions for a 非學校型態實驗教育 family, and under Standing Rule 4 that does not get to sit as "contradictory" for six weeks. **The ask, now and not in February:** write to the Hsinchu host and ask whether a 自學 student with a 設籍學校 counts as attending school in Taiwan, and whether an independent team of three is accepted at that round.

**Cost: unknown for Taiwan.** The only figure found anywhere was 878 CNY for a round in China — order of magnitude and nothing more. Teams also pay to attend Global Rounds abroad, which is where this gets expensive; treat the regional round as the only committed cost.

**Honest framing:** a competition with collaboration inside it, not pure collaboration. But the collaborative writing event and a three-person team are the real thing, and unlike FIRST Global there is no national selection standing between this family and entry.

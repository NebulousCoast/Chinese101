## 4 — Book of the day

**_It Could Be Otherwise: Science in the Age of Uncertainty_ by Stuart Firestein.** Basic Books, 2026, 288 pages.

Rotation index 0 (GatesNotes) — **substituted**, see below.

**The substitution, stated.** Today's slot is GatesNotes, exhausted here since late October. This comes instead from **Nature's "Books in brief" of 6 November 2026** — one of the five titles Andrew Robinson covered, which I listed on Thursday and set aside for exactly this purpose. Two more from that column remain unused.

**A date conflict to report:** Kirkus gives 4 August 2026 as the publication date, while reviews run from August to October and Nature's notice is November. Probably a US publication date followed by a long review tail; I did not resolve it and am not going to assert one.

**Who is accountable for it.** Stuart Firestein is a neuroscientist at Columbia. This is described as the third of a loose trilogy about the untidy parts of science, following *Ignorance: How It Drives Science* (2012). A working scientist writing about his own practice rather than a journalist reporting on it.

**The central argument, and the title is the argument.** "It could be otherwise" is a claim about the world: that things did not have to turn out the way they did, and that science is the practice of working under that condition rather than of eliminating it. The book's target is **determinism** — the belief, which Firestein traces to the success of mathematical physical law, that the universe is in principle fully predictable. His counter-hero is **Darwin**, who explained the apparent orderliness of living things *without* fixed laws, by a mechanism that is historical and contingent rather than deducible.

**Three further ideas.**

1. **That optimism is a philosophical position rather than a mood.** Reviewers all single this out: Firestein does not mean cheerfulness, he means a working commitment that unanswered questions are answerable — and he argues it has been as much a driver of modern science as conflict and competition have.
2. **That complexity, irreducible complexity and uncertainty are three different things which are constantly confused** — and this is the most useful part for a household teaching reasoning. A reviewer at the Santa Fe Institute singles out the chapter that separates them as the book's best work. Those three words get used interchangeably in almost all public argument about science, and pulling them apart is a durable skill rather than a fact to memorise.
3. **That uncertainty is a feature and not a deficit.** The practical claim is that a science which admitted its uncertainty more openly would be both more honest and more trusted — the opposite of the usual assumption that confidence is what earns credibility.

**The dissent, and it is a proper one from a named outlet rather than a blog.**

- **Kirkus** calls it "a solid and even stirring defense of science" — and then makes a **structural** criticism worth more than a complaint about tone: **that the optimism recedes in the book's second half** as the author presses his case against determinism. In other words the book's advertised subject gives way to its actual argument. That is a real and specific objection, and the kind a student should learn to make: not "I disagree" but **"the book you wrote is not the book your title promised"**.
- **Undark**'s review frames it as a timely counterweight to pessimism, and the **Washington Independent Review of Books** describes it as wide-ranging — from the origin of the word "scientist" to complex systems — which is praise that doubles as a hint about structure: *wide-ranging* often means the argument is carried by accumulation rather than by a line.

**The limit:** the reception I could reach is broadly positive and I found **no scientist or philosopher arguing the opposite case** — that determinism is defensible, or that embracing uncertainty is a mistake. So the dissent above is internal criticism from sympathetic reviewers, not opposition. Unlike Friday's Al-Khalili entry, that gap is not something I can fill by naming an opposing camp, because I did not identify one today.

**Suitability. The elder child, yes**, and this is one of the best-matched books in the whole rotation: 288 pages, by a working scientist, explicitly about the difference between *not knowing* and *not being able to know*. **For the 11-year-old, not the book** — but the complexity/uncertainty distinction is teachable at eleven with everyday examples, and the question in section 8 is drawn straight from it.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question, drawn straight from the book.** Is it better to say "I don't know", or to give your best guess?

**The move it teaches:** distinguishing **not knowing** from **having nothing to offer**. Those feel identical and are not. "I don't know, but it is probably between ten and a hundred, and here is why" is more useful than either a confident wrong number or a flat refusal — and it is harder than both, which is why people reach for the other two.

**The trap:** treating "I don't know" as automatically the humble, honest answer. Sometimes it is the lazy one. If you have a reason to think something, withholding it protects *you* rather than the person asking — you cannot be wrong if you never said anything. So the hard question: **when is "I don't know" a way of avoiding being wrong rather than a way of being accurate?**

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Use a guessable quantity — how many leaves on the tree outside, how far to the next town. Make everyone give a *range* they would actually bet on, then argue about whose range was honest rather than whose middle was closest.

**Relative of yesterday's, and the pair is the point.** Yesterday asked whether you must pass on how sure *someone else* was. Today asks what you owe when the uncertain person is *you*. Same problem, moved inside — and it is today's book's whole subject, which argues that a science more willing to say "we do not know yet" would be trusted more rather than less.

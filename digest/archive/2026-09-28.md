## 1. Learning platforms

### Hugging Face — free, and one step past the GitHub argument

The 2026-09-26 digest argued a public repository beats an essay because it carries
dated commit history. **Hugging Face Spaces goes one step further:** it hosts a
**working demo** a reader can click and interact with. A repo asks someone to read
code; a Space lets them *use* the thing. For a reviewer with thirty seconds, that
difference is large.

**Free account:** access to 2M+ models and 500k+ datasets, **unlimited public
repositories**, git-based version control for models and datasets, a dataset
viewer, free CPU Basic Spaces (2 vCPU, 16GB RAM).

**Free GPU, with caveats:** limited "ZeroGPU" access to NVIDIA H200 hardware, but
the daily quota is small — ⚠️ sources said 3.5 minutes and 5 minutes per day, which
disagree, so the exact figure is unverified. Free accounts can create static
Spaces and up to two ZeroGPU Gradio Spaces; other Space types need a paid plan.
Inference credit figures also conflicted and are not quoted.

> **Limits worth being clear about:** the free GPU allowance is **minutes per day,
> not hours** — for demonstrating something, not training it. Train on Kaggle (30
> GPU hours/week) and host the result here.
>
> **And one conceptual limit:** a demo is **presentation, not research**. It shows
> the student can build; it does not show they asked a good question. A research
> programme's reviewer cares about the question first.

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Better Angels of Our Nature: Why Violence Has Declined* — Steven Pinker (2011)

With *Sapiens* and *Business Adventures*, one of the three books Bill Gates has
recommended most consistently.

> **Why this one, stated plainly:** this digest's book selections have run
> structurally **pessimistic** for two weeks. Steinbeck on dispossession,
> Kingsolver on extraction, Alexander on caste, Cohen on commerce colonising
> health, Butler on collapse. Each argues that systems grind people down. That is
> a coherent worldview, assembled unintentionally — but it is one-sided, and
> Pinker is the strongest available case for the other direction. Offering it with
> its critics attached beats pretending the digest was balanced.

**The central argument.** Violence has declined over millennia, and the present is
probably the most peaceful period in human history. The claim is deliberately
broad — the decline shows up in military conflict, homicide, genocide, torture,
criminal justice, and in the treatment of children, ethnic minorities,
homosexuals and animals.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Rates, not counts.** The argument turns on per-capita measurement. A world
   with more people can have more deaths and less violence. Whether that framing
   is right is exactly where the fight is.
2. **Proposed mechanisms, not just a trend.** The state's monopoly on force,
   expanding commerce, widening circles of empathy, rising literacy and reason — a
   causal story, more ambitious and more falsifiable than description.
3. **Historical myopia.** Violence feels like it is rising partly because
   reporting improved and partly because standards rose; we now count as atrocity
   what was once unremarkable.
4. **Moral progress as real.** A genuinely unfashionable claim, argued at length.

> ⚠️ **The critics — and one matters more than the book.**
>
> - **Nassim Taleb's** "The Pinker Problem" and "The 'Long Peace' Is a Statistical
>   Illusion" argue the statistics are mis-sampled and, more deeply, that in
>   **fat-tailed distributions you cannot infer declining propensity from a quiet
>   interval**. Rare catastrophic events dominate the average; a few decades
>   without a world war tells you very little about the probability of one. **This
>   is the objection to understand.**
> - Historians published a volume against it — *The Darker Angels of Our Nature* —
>   and anthropologists dispute his figures on violence in traditional societies.
> - Scholarly reception is genuinely split: some call the scholarship immaculate
>   and the conclusions plausible; others think the failures outweigh the merits.

**Why the critique is the more valuable half:** Taleb's point is a lesson about
what data can and cannot show, and it generalises far beyond this argument. **"You
cannot infer the tail from the middle"** is load-bearing in finance, epidemiology,
climate and engineering. Reading Pinker and Taleb together means encountering one
of the genuinely important disagreements about evidence — worth more than settling
who is right.

**Accessibility:** very long, but clearly written and readable in sections. The
thesis chapters plus Taleb's short critique is a reasonable substitute for the
whole.

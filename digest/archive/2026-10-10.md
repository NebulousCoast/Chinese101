## 4. Book of the day

### *When Everyone Knows That Everyone Knows…: Common Knowledge and the Mysteries of Money, Power, and Everyday Life* — Steven Pinker (September 2025)

One of the five books Gates recommended in his most recent holiday list.

Chosen for today deliberately: **a national day is the clearest example of the
thing this book is about.** A parade does not inform anyone of anything they did
not already know. What it does is make the knowing *public* — it converts private
belief into common knowledge, and that conversion is where its power lies.

**The central argument.** There is a difference between everyone knowing something
privately and **everyone knowing that everyone knows it**. The second state —
common knowledge — is the key cognitive tool for understanding when people
suddenly coordinate, and so when societies change abruptly rather than gradually.
Nothing new has to be learned; what changes is that the knowledge becomes
*mutually visible*.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Private knowledge versus common knowledge.** The emperor's new clothes:
   everyone can see he is naked, but nothing happens until a child says it aloud
   and everyone knows that everyone heard. **The child adds no information. The
   child creates common knowledge.**
2. **It explains suddenness.** Bubbles, crashes and revolutions that seem to come
   from nowhere are inexplicable if people update private beliefs gradually; they
   make sense if the trigger is a shift in what is *publicly acknowledged*.
3. **Diplomacy is mostly managing it.** Posturing and deliberate vagueness are
   rational: some things states know and cannot afford to say aloud, because
   saying them creates obligations silence does not. The clearest extension of
   Schelling, who opened this digest on day one.
4. **Social media cuts both ways.** More common knowledge, because anything can
   become publicly known instantly; less, because audiences fragment and what is
   common knowledge in one network is invisible in another. Shaming mobs analysed
   as common-knowledge events.
5. **Everyday awkwardness runs on the same machinery.** First-date hedging, things
   left unsaid in families, the euphemism that lets everyone avoid admitting what
   both understand.

> **Genuinely contested, not oversold.** Book Marks rates reception **mixed**
> across nine reviews. The *Irish Times* headline: "Padded out and repetitive,"
> arguing ~100 pages of original material stretched to three times that length.
> One reviewer called it Pinker's worst book — while still saying it is intriguing
> and worth reading. Against that, *The Economist* said it illuminates many
> enigmas of public and personal life, and Nobel laureate **Eric Maskin** — the
> same Harvard economist who appears in this digest's Princeton/Harvard rotation
> recommending *The Undoing Project* — calls it "an expository masterpiece." One
> reviewer notes a real gap: the link between common knowledge and the
> *enforceability* of laws, contracts and norms gets less analysis than the
> flashier topics.
>
> **A strong idea, a contested execution.** If the reader gets it from the first
> third, they probably have — and noticing that is itself a skill.

**Why it matters here.** The idea is portable and cheap. It explains why an
application essay is not information transfer but a *public claim*; why a
published repository beats a private one; and why this digest keeps saying "ask
the organiser" rather than "assume" — **an unverified date is private knowledge, a
confirmed one is common.**

> **Suitability:** the elder child, yes, and quick for the central idea. For the
> 11-year-old: not the book, but **the idea is perfectly teachable at eleven.** The
> emperor's new clothes is the whole thesis in a story she knows, and asking her
> *why the child's words changed anything* is a genuinely good conversation.

## 1. Learning platforms

### AoPS — the full picture, after a week of name-checking it

**What it is:** an accredited online school for grades 5–12 built around
competition-style problem solving rather than curriculum coverage.
WASC-accredited; most subject classes NCAA-approved. **Cost:** from about
**$31/week**.

**Three class formats, which matters more than people expect:**

- **Live online with video** — students and instructor on camera together.
- **Chat-based, no audio or video** — students type their reasoning and get
  immediate feedback. Unusual and polarizing: some think far better in text,
  others find it alienating. **Worth trialling before committing.**
- **Self-paced**, available anytime, adaptive.

**WOOT** — Worldwide Online Olympiad Training, in maths, CS, physics and
chemistry — is the serious tier, designed and taught by former olympiad
medalists. PhysicsWOOT was built by former IPhO coach Mark Eichenlaub and preps
F=ma, USAPhO and IPhO.

**Free tier worth knowing:** an account and the community forums cost nothing,
and the forums alone are one of the better maths communities online.

> **Honest limits:** it adds up fast across multiple subjects; it is genuinely
> hard and can demoralize a student who is not already strong and
> self-motivated; and it optimizes for *competition* mathematics, which is not
> the same as school mathematics or as research. **Why it matters for section
> 2:** RSI and similar select partly on AMC/AIME and F=ma/USAPhO results, and
> this is the standard route to those.
> <https://artofproblemsolving.com/school>

### Exercism — free, and unusually good

**100% free forever**, open-source, community-funded nonprofit. **8,100+
exercises across 83 programming languages**, with automated analysis on every
submission *and* voluntary human mentorship from experienced developers who
review code and suggest idiomatic improvements — **free personalized code
review**, which is the rare part. CLI-first, with an in-browser editor.
**Independent use:** yes, and the mentoring supplies the feedback loop most
self-study lacks. **Limit:** it teaches fluency and idiom in languages, not CS
theory or algorithms — pair it with something else if USACO is the target.
<https://exercism.org/>

## 4. Book of the day

### *The Alignment Problem: Machine Learning and Human Values* — Brian Christian (2020)

Recommended at Princeton as an entry point to modern AI for readers who want the
concepts rather than the hype.

**The central argument.** As we hand consequential decisions to machine learning
systems, we face a problem that is technical and philosophical at once: how to
make a system that optimizes *something* actually optimize the thing we meant.
Christian's core claim is that this is not one problem but a chain of them — and
that specifying human values precisely enough for a machine is far harder than
anyone assumed.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Specification is the hard part.** We rarely know what we want precisely
   enough to write down. A system given a proxy objective will optimize the
   proxy — faithfully, and disastrously.
2. **The failures are already here, and they are mundane.** Résumé-screening
   systems found years later to carry gender bias. Bail and parole algorithms
   that appear to assess Black and white defendants differently. Not
   hypotheticals.
3. **Opacity compounds everything.** If you cannot interpret why a model decided
   something, you cannot tell whether it is aligned with your values or merely
   appears to be. Interpretability is a prerequisite, not a nice-to-have.
4. **Inverse reinforcement learning as a partial answer.** Rather than
   specifying the objective, infer it: given observed behaviour, what reward is
   being optimized? An elegant idea — which inherits every flaw in the behaviour
   it observes.
5. **The unifying claim:** fixing biased algorithms today and preventing
   catastrophic misalignment later are two sides of one coin, not separate
   agendas. That framing is the book's real contribution.

**Why it matters.** The clearest available introduction to questions this
student would spend a career inside if they go into anything technical —
readable without a maths background, built on reporting and interviews rather
than equations. It also treats a technical problem as genuinely a values
problem, without letting either side off the hook.

> **Note on currency:** published 2020, so it predates the current generation of
> large models. The examples have aged; the framework has not. Read it for the
> structure of the problem, not the state of the field.

## 1 · Interactive and bring-your-own-device learning

Re-verifying, and closing the last open question in the section.

**The Duolingo English Test: the CEFR mapping.** Scored 10–160 in steps of 5, aligned to CEFR, IELTS Academic and TOEFL iBT. CEFR: **100–125 = B2, 130–150 = C1, 155–160 = C2.** Above 120 generally treated as proficient. IELTS: 120–125 ≈ 6.5, 130–135 ≈ 7, 160 ≈ 8.5–9. TOEFL iBT: 160 ≈ 120, 125 ≈ 93–97.

**Three warnings, which matter more than the numbers.**
1. **The official chart has changed and old charts inflate.** Duolingo previously equated 125 with IELTS *7.5*; it now equates the same 125 with *6.5*. A full point of drift in the unfavourable direction, and any undated chart online is as likely to be the old one.
2. **Some "2026 official tables" are not.** One site under that heading maps 120 to IELTS 6.5–7.0 and TOEFL 95–99, conflicting with the official figures and carrying no attribution to Duolingo. Same pattern as the Gates aggregators and the US AMC 8 dates on a Chinese-language page.
3. **The concordance is weaker than a table looks.** Built from a limited sample of score reports; at least one source notes the average of subscore comparisons does not always match the overall comparison. A rough bridge, not a conversion rate — and **universities set their own thresholds**, so the only number that matters is on the institution's own admissions page.

**Practically:** B2 requirement → a DET score around 105 clears it with room. C1 → target 130. A usable planning target, worth knowing before paying US$70, and the two-test bundle at US$118 is the sensible purchase if a first attempt lands near a boundary.

## 4 · Book of the day

***En attendant Godot* (Waiting for Godot) — Samuel Beckett.** **#12**, Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century.

**The provenance, now with actual numbers.** In **spring 1999** the retailer **Fnac** and the Paris newspaper **Le Monde** started from a preliminary list of **200 titles** compiled by booksellers and journalists, and asked **seventeen thousand** French participants: *which books have stuck in your mind?* A question about memory and attachment, not merit — which explains the shape of the list better than any theory of French taste. Two sources agree Godot is 12th; they disagree on the year (Wikipedia 1952, another 1953) — almost certainly publication versus premiere.

**What it is.** Two men wait by a tree on a road for someone called Godot, who does not come. A man with a rope arrives, driving another man before him. A boy brings a message. The second act repeats the first with small differences. Nothing is explained and nobody leaves.

**Three ideas, and the third is why it is here.**
1. **Structure can carry meaning without plot.** Beckett removes almost everything a play is supposed to have — development, revelation, change — and what remains is still unmistakably *about* something. Removing a thing is one of the strongest ways to show what it was doing.
2. **The refusal to say who Godot is.** Beckett spent decades declining to explain, including to actors and directors. The temptation is to decode it — God, death, meaning, hope — and the play is more interesting once you notice that *every* decoding makes it smaller. A thing that can be named has been contained.
3. **It is a comedy**, which is why a family should read it rather than read *about* it. On the page it looks forbidding; out loud it is a music-hall act — two men bickering about boots and hats, with timing that only works when spoken. Lucky's speech is a torrent of broken academic language that an eleven-year-old will find funny before finding it anything else, and finding it funny first is the correct order.

**The limit.** No substantive critical reviews reached today — what surfaces for a play this famous is encyclopaedia entries, ranking aggregators and study guides. So this describes the work rather than reporting its reception.

**Suitability — the rare one. Both children, together, out loud.** About sixty pages of dialogue, no background needed, and one of very few items in this rotation an eleven-year-old and a high-schooler can take on genuinely equal terms — because neither will understand it, and that is the point. Assign parts. Do not explain it afterwards. If the eleven-year-old asks who Godot is, the honest answer — "nobody knows, and Beckett wouldn't say" — is the whole lesson.

## 6 · International collaboration

**Distributed Proofreaders — possibly the lowest-barrier real collaboration in this entire file.**

**What it is.** Public-domain books are scanned, and volunteers proofread pages one at a time against the scanned image in a browser. Each page passes through multiple rounds of proofing and formatting by different people, and the finished text is published by **Project Gutenberg**. Running since 2000, explicitly international by design — "a web-based cooperation on an international scale," in its own description. Free. No institution. No application. Register and start.

**Why it meets this section's bar** rather than being busywork: the output is a *real shared artefact*. A finished Gutenberg e-text is a permanent, publicly available object that dozens of strangers made together.

**And the part I did not expect — it will certify the work.** DP has a published policy on certification of volunteer work, which is unusual and, for a 非學校型態實驗教育 portfolio, directly useful. From their own documentation:
- The certificate identifies the volunteer by **DP username, the real name they provided, and their registered email**, and states **the time period worked and the number of pages completed**.
- A volunteer must have been registered **a minimum of 21 days** before any confirmation is sent.
- No more than **50 pages per day** may be counted.
- They **do not track hours** and will not estimate them, because they cannot know how long a page took. They say explicitly this matters because many schools want hours.
- Certification is **by email only**, to the organisation and the volunteer. They state they will **not fill in an online form**, which some schools insist on.
- Not available for court-ordered community service, or programmes operated by federal, state or municipal governments.
- Longer-term volunteers with a different need should contact `dphelp`; for extra evidence they suggest output from the "Pages Proofread Per Day" script alongside the certificate.

**The twenty-one-day rule is the actionable part:** registering now costs nothing and makes a certificate possible later, whereas registering *in order to get one* is three weeks too late.

**The limits, and two are real gaps.** pgdp.net refuses fetches, so all of the above is read through the search index — the certification terms came back in enough detail to be confident, but the page itself was not seen. **No minimum age established**, and it could not be established whether individual volunteers are **named in the finished Gutenberg e-text** or only counted internally. Both are on their New Volunteer FAQ, which the family can open. Ask before assuming the 11-year-old may register.

**Why this fits Wednesday's argument.** The teammates problem has been searched for in the wrong place — competitions want teams that already exist, working groups want participants. Distributed Proofreaders is the second instance after the Exoplanet Watch Slack, and gentler: forums, mentoring, and advanced project-facilitator roles with *published prerequisites* — several months since registration, a set number of completed pages, and a mentor. A visible ladder, which is exactly what a child with no institution behind them cannot usually find.

## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *If nobody will ever explain what something means, can it still mean something?*

**The move:** separating *meaning* from *authorised meaning*. The instinct — a child's, and most adults' — is that somewhere a right answer is being withheld, and the thing is a puzzle with a solution in the back of the book. The move is to consider the other possibility: that the object is complete, and the demand for an explanation is a demand to be told what to think about it.

**The trap:** landing on "so it means whatever you want." The comfortable exit, and wrong, because some readings are plainly better than others — better supported by what is actually there. The real question is the hard middle: **what makes one reading better than another when there is no answer key?** "The one that uses more of what is actually in it" has found the whole thing.

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask it. Not about a book first — about a piece of music with no words, or a dream. Then ask what would make somebody's interpretation *wrong*, which is the harder and better half. Stop there.

**Relative of this week.** Sunday to Thursday all asked versions of "when should you believe this" — what else explains the pattern, what procedure would make it credible, what the evidence rules out, when inaccuracy is permitted, what makes a photograph proof. Today is the first that is not about evidence at all, and both children may enjoy noticing it is not the same *kind* of question.

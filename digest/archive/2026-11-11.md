## 4 — Book of the day

**_For Whom the Bell Tolls_ by Ernest Hemingway.** 1940. **Number 8 on Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century.**

Rotation index 2 (Le Monde's 100 Books of the Century).

**What that list is**, and it matters before any rank is quoted. It is not a critical canon. It is a **reader poll** run in 1999 by Le Monde and the Fnac bookshops, published 15 October 1999, from a shortlist proposed by Le Monde journalists and Fnac booksellers — a vote about attachment and memory rather than a judgement of quality. A high rank on it means a great many French readers loved the book, which is a real fact about a different thing.

**The rank, with its source stated plainly.** The reproduction I could reach is a **user-created Babelio list**, not Le Monde's own page. I am using it because it is internally consistent with what this file already has independently: it gives 7 Steinbeck and 9 Alain-Fournier, both of which this digest confirmed from other sources in September and October. A secondary source that reproduces three neighbours correctly is worth more than one that asserts a single number. fr.wikipedia.org does not resolve from this container, so Le Monde's own 1999 article is out of reach.

**What the book is.** Spain, 1937. Robert Jordan, an American explosives expert attached to a Republican guerrilla band in the mountains, is sent to blow a bridge to support an offensive. The novel covers about three days. He knows early on that the offensive is already compromised and that the bridge will probably accomplish nothing.

**Three ideas.**

1. **That a cause can be just and its conduct indefensible at the same time** — and it is the reason the book is still argued about. Hemingway's Republicans commit a massacre; the chapter in which villagers beat Fascist sympathisers to death and throw them off a cliff is told by one of the sympathetic characters, about her own side. He was writing as a committed partisan, and he put that in.
2. **The futility is the structure, not the mood.** Jordan carries out an order he has good reason to believe is pointless, because the alternative is the collapse of a discipline that other people's lives depend on. The question the book poses is not "is the war worth it" but **"what do you owe an order you think is wrong".**
3. **The title's claim.** Donne's "no man is an island… never send to know for whom the bell tolls; it tolls for thee" is quoted at the front, and the book is an argument for it that keeps testing it — a man who believes he is part of everyone, dying alone on a hillside in a country not his own.

**The dissent — and it is the best-documented dissent any book in this rotation has had.**

- The central attack is **Arturo Barea's 1941 essay in _Horizon_, "Not Spain but Hemingway"** — Barea being a Spaniard, a Republican, and a Madrid eyewitness. His conclusion, as scholarly summaries quote it: that as a novel about Spaniards and their war it is **"unreal and, in the last analysis, deeply untruthful."** His diagnosis is a sentence worth keeping: that Hemingway was **"always a spectator who wanted to be an actor."**
- Barea also objected specifically to the **language** — Hemingway's archaic "thou" and "thee" rendering of Spanish peasant speech, which Barea called "a curious translation, which is no real translation" at all.
- And the scholarship has largely conceded the point without abandoning the book: John Teunissen's essay on the novel says flatly **"I have no quarrel with Senor Barea, for he is right"** — and then argues that attacks on its realism are the most fruitful place for an admiring reader to start.
- A corrective worth having: recent work on the novel's reception in postwar Spain argues that Spanish responses were **far more varied** than Barea's essay alone, which has long been treated as standing for all of them.

**The limit:** I did not reach the full text of Barea's essay, only scholarly and biographical summaries of it, so the quotations above are quoted at second hand. The strongest sources I reached are academic; the weakest are blog and event pages, and I have leaned on the former.

**Suitability — the elder child, yes**, and the dissent is the reason rather than a caveat: this is a book you can read alongside a first-rate argument that it gets its own subject wrong, which is a rarer and more useful experience than reading an unchallenged classic. **Content:** war, a massacre described in detail, a suicide discussed, a rape referred to. **For the 11-year-old, no.**

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question.** Can someone write something true about a place they do not belong to?

**The move it teaches:** separating **the authority of experience** from **the quality of the account**. Those feel like one thing and are two. A witness can be present and still describe badly; an outsider can be wrong about the texture of a place and right about something happening in it. The move is to ask what *kind* of claim is being made, and then ask what would qualify someone to make that particular kind.

**The trap:** landing on "only insiders may describe." It is tempting and it proves too much — it would rule out history, most journalism, and any account of anyone who cannot speak for themselves. But the opposite exit is just as cheap: "anyone may write anything, judge the prose." The hard middle is whether *being wrong about a place* can be a literary failure rather than only a moral one.

**How to run it, ten minutes.** Today's book is the live case: a Spanish eyewitness said of Hemingway's Spanish war novel that it was "deeply untruthful", and diagnosed the author as "always a spectator who wanted to be an actor." The scholar who quotes that agrees with him *and* still thinks the novel is worth reading. Hold both.

**Relative of this week.** On 4 November we asked whether two accurate tellings can differ in quality; yesterday, whether a prize changes the thing it is given to. Today asks who is entitled to tell at all. **Same family, third angle** — and noticing that is the skill being trained, not the answer.

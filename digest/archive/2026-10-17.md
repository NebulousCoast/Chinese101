## 1. Interactive learning platforms — today, reasoning

**Calling Bullshit — free, and the best critical-reasoning course available at no cost.** INFO 270 / BIOL 270 at the University of Washington, taught by Carl Bergstrom and Jevin West. The entire course is public: syllabus, readings, tools, **case studies**, and **ten edited video lectures**. https://callingbullshit.org/

What it teaches: how to spot a claim dressed as data — causality versus correlation, statistical traps, misleading visualisation, the limits of big data, **publication bias**, fake news. Now taught at 130+ universities.

Limits: university-level, written for undergraduates — right for the elder child, too early for the eleven-year-old. The site's own syllabus lists "next offered Autumn Quarter 2023," so the page is stale even though the materials are not; no enrolment, grading or certificate. The same team has a newer LLM project at thebullshitmachines.com, **not examined** — named, not recommended.

Why this and not a logic textbook: a formal logic course teaches valid inference; this teaches the thing that actually goes wrong, a true-looking number with a broken provenance. It also matches this digest's own discipline — report the conflict, name the source, say what could not be verified.

## 4. Book of the day

Rotation index 1 → NYT 100 Best Books of the 21st Century.

***Never Let Me Go* — Kazuo Ishiguro** (2005). **Number 9** on the critics' list, voted by 503 literary figures. Ishiguro won the Nobel in 2017.

**A note on method:** Rebecca Skloot's *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks* was the intended pick today as the obvious ethics pairing — and it is **not** on this list. One commentator on the list complains about exactly that absence and notes the list contains almost no nonfiction science. Checked before writing rather than after.

**The central argument.** A boarding school in the English countryside, narrated in retrospect. The children are raised with great care, taught to make art, told they are special — and the reader gradually works out what they are *for*. The book's method *is* the slow arrival of comprehension, so the mechanism stays unspoiled here. What it argues: a society can do something monstrous while being unfailingly gentle about it, and the people inside the arrangement will mostly cooperate — not from stupidity, but because the arrangement was all they were ever given.

**Key ideas:**

1. **The moral weight of being raised for a purpose.** The education is real, kind and good. It is also preparation. Both true at once, and the novel refuses to let you drop either.
2. **Consent that was never asked for is not consent**, even when the person says yes. The narrator's acceptance is the most disturbing thing in the book.
3. **Art as evidence of a soul** — and what it means that someone had to be persuaded. That a proof was needed is the indictment.
4. **Complicity at every level**, from those who built the system to those who merely did not look.
5. **Memory as consolation and as evasion.** The narration is warm, nostalgic, and quietly unreliable about what it declines to say plainly.

**The pairing, and why it is today's book.** Yesterday's was *Gray Matters*, a neurosurgeon's history of his field's confident errors — the ethics of what is done to bodies by people who mean well. *Never Let Me Go* is the same question from inside the body. Read within a week of each other they give the two halves of medical ethics: the practitioner's view, and the view of the person being practised upon.

**Suitability:** the elder child, yes — plain prose, 300-odd pages, and one of the few novels a seventeen-year-old and a philosophy seminar can discuss on the same terms. **Not** for the eleven-year-old: the book is about mortality and the use of people, and its quietness is what makes it land hard.

## 8. Thinking

*New today. Short on purpose, every day. One question, and the move it teaches.*

**Today's question, for both children at once:** Your bicycle has had every part replaced, one at a time, over three years — wheels, frame, chain, saddle, everything. Is it the same bicycle? Then: almost every cell in your body is replaced over time. Are you the same person you were at five?

**The move it teaches:** separating a thing from its parts. The first half feels like a word game about bicycles. The second half makes clear it was never about bicycles — it is about what "the same" means, and whether the answer can differ for an object and for a person without anyone being inconsistent.

**The trap to watch for**, and it is the useful part: whoever says "it depends what you mean by *same*" has not ended the discussion, they have started it. Ask them to finish the sentence. That is the whole skill.

**How to run it**, per 楊茂秀's method: fifteen minutes, no correct answer, and the adult does not supply one. Ask "why?" and "would that still be true if…?" and keep the question open longer than is comfortable. Summarise briefly at the end, or not at all. An eleven-year-old and a seventeen-year-old can hold this one on genuinely equal terms, which is the rarest property of a good question.

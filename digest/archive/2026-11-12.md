## 4 — Book of the day

**_What Is Populism?_ by Jan-Werner Müller.** University of Pennsylvania Press, 2016. **The Princeton Pre-read for 2017.**

Rotation index 3 (Princeton/Harvard recommended reading).

**Provenance, verified from Princeton's own newsroom.** Eisgruber announced it on **28 April 2017** in a letter to incoming freshmen, for the **Class of 2021**, each of whom received a copy. Students discussed it with Müller himself during Orientation, and the September 2017 Pre-read assembly had Eisgruber introducing, then Müller, then two faculty commentators, Brandice Canes-Wrone and Dan-el Padilla Peralta. Müller has been on the Princeton politics faculty since 2005, working on democratic theory and the history of modern political thought.

**Slot note:** with this, the Pre-read list for 2013–2016 is exhausted in this rotation and 2017 is now used too. The 2018 title is still unverified and 2019 onward has never been established, so this slot needs new research before its next turn — saying so now rather than discovering it in December.

**The central argument, and it is a definition rather than a complaint.** Populism is not a mood, a style, or simply being against elites — plenty of decent politics is against elites. Müller's claim is that its defining feature is **anti-pluralism**: the populist asserts that *he alone represents the real people*, and it follows that anyone who disagrees is not merely mistaken but **not properly part of the people at all.** The quarrel stops being about policy and becomes about who counts.

**Three further ideas.**

1. **The division is moral rather than programmatic.** Pure people against corrupt elite is not a disagreement you can split the difference on, which is why populist politics does not produce the usual bargaining.
2. **The claim to represent is symbolic and therefore unfalsifiable.** If "the real people" is a moral category rather than a headcount, losing an election does not refute it — the result was stolen, or the voters were not the real ones. That single observation explains a great deal of recent political behaviour and is the book's most portable insight.
3. **Populists in power govern in recognisable ways** — occupying the state apparatus, mass clientelism, squeezing civil society, and rewriting constitutions as partisan documents. The point is that these are not incidental excesses but what the anti-pluralist premise implies once it holds power.

**And the conclusion that makes the book useful rather than merely satisfying:** populism is not democracy's authentic voice that elites ignore, nor is it simply anti-democratic. It is a **degraded form of democracy that promises to deliver democracy's own ideals** — which is why you cannot answer it by ruling its supporters out of the conversation, since that is the very move being objected to.

**The dissent, and it is substantial.**

- **The definitional objection** is the main one: that building anti-pluralism into the definition settles the empirical question in advance, so the book cannot discover that any given movement is *not* populist — it has defined populism as the bad thing. Scholars in the "ideational" tradition of populism studies press this.
- **From the left**, the standard counter is Chantal Mouffe's case for **left populism** — that populism is a legitimate way of reconstituting real democratic conflict against a technocratic consensus, and that Müller's definition conveniently places European left movements in the same box as the far right.
- That his **Latin American material is thinner** than his European material.
- That the account is **too purely political** — that it underweights the economic and material grievances that produce populist support in the first place, and so diagnoses a symptom at the level of rhetoric.

**The limit, stated plainly:** I verified the Pre-read provenance from Princeton's own pages, but I did **not** retrieve the full text of any review today — a *Journal of Democracy* piece ("What's in a Name?") surfaced in search and I could not open it. So treat the provenance as confirmed and **the dissent above as my own summary of the standard objections rather than as quoted criticism.** That is a weaker footing than yesterday's Hemingway entry had, and the difference is worth noticing.

**Suitability — the elder child, yes**, and this is the best-shaped book in the Princeton slot for the purpose: about 125 pages, one clear thesis, four clear objections, and an author who then stood in front of thirteen hundred eighteen-year-olds and defended it. Reading it with the definitional objection in hand is a complete exercise in how arguments are built. **For the 11-year-old, no as a book** — but its core distinction is completely accessible, and it is today's section 8.

## 6 — International collaboration (even-numbered dates)

**IASC, properly described this time** — and it turns out to be one of the best items in the whole pool, which I had made look like one of the worst.

**What it is.** The International Astronomical Search Collaboration, led by Dr Patrick Miller of Hardin-Simmons University, distributes real telescope images to teams who measure them for moving objects. Discoveries are reported, and confirmed ones go into the Minor Planet Center's records; provisional designations and eventually naming rights follow real detections. It is not a simulation and it is not clicking through a gallery: it is astrometry on current data, and NASA lists it on its own citizen-science pages.

## 8 — Thinking

**Today's question.** What is the difference between thinking someone is *wrong* and thinking they *don't count*?

**The move it teaches:** noticing when a disagreement has quietly changed category. Arguing that someone is mistaken keeps them in the conversation — you owe them reasons. Deciding they do not really belong in the conversation ends it, and the conversion often happens without anyone announcing it. The move is to catch the moment it happens, in other people and in yourself.

**The trap:** assuming the second move is always the dishonest one. Sometimes it is right to say a voice has no standing — a person arguing in bad faith, or an outsider dictating terms in someone else's business. So the hard question is the one to leave open: **what would entitle you to decide that somebody doesn't count?** And would you accept that same test applied to you?

**How to run it, ten minutes.** A household version works better than a political one: a sibling argument where one person stops answering and starts saying the other is not allowed an opinion. What changed at that moment?

**Relative of this week, and of today's book.** Müller's whole definition of populism rests on this distinction — his claim is that the characteristic move is not "you are wrong" but "you are not really one of us." And it is the near neighbour of yesterday's question, which asked who is entitled to *tell*. Today asks who is entitled to *disagree*. **Same family, fourth angle this week** — and recognising the family is the skill, not settling it.

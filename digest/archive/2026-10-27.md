## 4 · Book of the day

***The End of Everything* — M. John Harrison.** Serpent's Tail, June 2026. Booker Prize 2026 shortlist.

**Timing.** The Booker winner is announced **Monday 9 November**, thirteen days out, at Old Billingsgate in London; £50,000 and a trophy. Panel chaired by the classicist **Mary Beard**, with Raymond Antrobus, Jarvis Cocker, Rebecca Liu and Patricia Lockwood. The other five shortlisted novels: *The Disappearers* (Marlon James), *Black Bag* (Luke Kennard), *May We Feed the King* (Rebecca Perry), *The Things We Never Say* (Elizabeth Strout), *John of John* (Douglas Stuart).

**Provenance conflict, reported.** One book site describes the novel as being on the 2026 Booker *longlist*; NPR's coverage and the Booker Prizes' own 2026 prize-year page list it on the *shortlist*. Not actually in conflict — every shortlisted book was longlisted first — but the weaker source is the one to drop.

**Premise.** A near-future Britain in which people comb the Channel coast for alien relics that can be sold at a profit. That makes it sound like science fiction, and Harrison has spent fifty years writing things shelved as science fiction while refusing to do what the shelf promises.

**Three ideas.**
1. **Collapse is boring from the inside.** The relic-scavenging is scrap-dealing; people have jobs. The apocalypse is not an event but a *texture* — which matters because it is almost certainly correct about how large changes are actually experienced.
2. **The refusal of explanation as method, not mannerism.** Harrison withholds what the relics are and where they came from. The claim underneath: a story that explains itself has told you what to think about it, and an unexplained object in a familiar world does more work than an explained one.
3. **Place as the subject.** The *Spectator Australia* reviewer called it "both a bracing vision of environmental collapse and a post-Brexit *cri de coeur*" — that double reading *is* the book: a specific coastline, in a specific national mood, doing the work argument usually does.

**Reception, including the dissent.** The *Spectator Australia* review (June 2026) is positive. A review attributed to Michel Faber in the *Guardian* calls it dreamlike and baffling but clear about humanity's disintegration — **the Guardian piece was unreachable; passed on second-hand**, so the wording is approximate. The dissent is real: one reviewer admires the sentences but finds the book fails to cohere into anything more impactful when read as a novel; another calls it more weird than a beach read. Harrison divides readers on purpose; a review that found it satisfying would be slightly suspicious.

**Suitability.** The elder child, yes, with a caveat: it teaches more about how prose can work than about anything it depicts, and a reader who wants to know what happens will be annoyed — and that annoyance is instructive at seventeen. **Not** the 11-year-old, not for content reasons especially, but because a novel that withholds its own premise needs a reader who already trusts that withholding can be deliberate.

## 8 · Thinking

**Question.** *A competition gives a medal to everyone in the top thirty percent. You get a medal. What have you learnt about yourself?*

**The move:** asking what a signal would have had to *exclude* to mean anything. A result is informative in proportion to how many people it rules out. Thirty percent rules out almost nobody, so the medal says roughly that you are not in the bottom two-thirds — worth knowing, and not what the word "medal" is doing in the sentence.

**The trap:** deciding the medal is therefore worthless and stopping there. It is not worthless; it is much smaller than it looks, and knowing its actual size is the skill. The follow-on is the real question — *what would you have to be told for it to mean a lot?* "Where I came out of everyone, not which band" has found it.

**How to run it (10 minutes).** Ask it. Then: "how many people got one?" Then: "so who didn't?" Supply nothing, and do not let it land on cynicism — the point is calibration, not contempt. If the 11-year-old enters the 檢定賽 next month, have this conversation *before* the result arrives.

**Relative of the last two days.** Sunday asked what else might explain a pattern; Monday asked what procedure would make a claim believable; today asks what a piece of evidence rules out. All three are the same question — *how much does this actually narrow things down* — and saying that out loud is the point.

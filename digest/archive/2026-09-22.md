## 1. Learning platforms

### Schoolhouse.world — free, and the only one on this list the student can give *back* to

**What it is:** a 501(c)(3) non-profit founded by Sal Khan, running peer-led
tutoring on Zoom in small groups of 5–10. Free SAT tutoring in official
partnership with the College Board using their practice content, plus homework
help and college admissions mentorship from current college students. **Cost:
$0.** Everything.

**Three reasons this matters specifically for a homeschooler:**

1. **SAT preparation with actual humans, free.** Not an app — trained volunteer
   tutors, live, small groups, official College Board material.
2. **College admissions mentorship.** Homeschoolers have no school counsellor.
   This supplies current college students who recently did the thing — a genuine
   gap-filler for an international applicant navigating US admissions.
3. **The one worth acting on: the student can become a tutor.** Certification
   requires demonstrating mastery via Khan Academy unit tests; SAT tutors must be
   high schoolers or recent graduates with a verified **700+** on SAT Math or
   Reading and Writing, plus the certification programme.

> **Why point 3 is the strategic one:** a homeschooled student has no school
> activities to point at — no clubs, no team, no student council. Schoolhouse
> tutoring is a **documented, verifiable, sustained commitment** with a global
> volunteer community behind it, and it costs nothing but time. It is also the
> rare activity that is genuinely good rather than resume-shaped: **teaching a
> subject is the fastest way to find out whether you actually understand it.**

**Limit:** volunteer-dependent, so subject coverage and scheduling vary. Best for
SAT, core maths and admissions guidance; not a substitute for AoPS-level
difficulty. <https://schoolhouse.world/>

## 4. Book of the day

### *Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind* — Yuval Noah Harari (2011)

Along with Pinker's *Better Angels* and Brooks's *Business Adventures*, one of
the three books Bill Gates has recommended most consistently.

**The central argument.** Homo sapiens came to dominate not through strength or
intelligence but through an ability to cooperate flexibly in very large numbers —
and that ability rests on **shared fictions**. Money, nations, corporations,
laws, human rights: none exist in the physical world. They work because enough
people believe in them simultaneously.

**Key ideas:**

1. **Fiction as coordination technology.** A Cognitive Revolution roughly 70,000
   years ago gave humans the capacity to describe things that do not exist.
   Harari's claim is that this, not tool use, is the decisive advantage.
2. **Imagined orders scale cooperation past personal acquaintance.** You can know
   a few hundred people. You cannot know a nation — but you can share a story
   with one.
3. **The Agricultural Revolution as "history's biggest fraud."** More people,
   living worse: longer hours, poorer diets, more disease. Wheat domesticated
   *us*.
4. **Money, empire and religion as the three great unifiers** — the mechanisms by
   which strangers were made cooperable at scale.
5. **Progress does not track happiness.** A deliberately deflating thread.

> ⚠️ **Now the part more useful than the book.** *Sapiens* is seriously contested
> by historians:
>
> - **Attribution and citation.** Critics say Harari builds on others' work
>   without proper credit and sets aside established scholarship where it
>   complicates the narrative. One notes the entire first chapter carries a
>   single endnote.
> - **His own Oxford thesis advisor, Steven Gunn**, offered the sharpest version:
>   the approach is to "ask questions so large that no one can say, 'We think
>   this bit's wrong and that bit's wrong.'" **Unfalsifiability by scale.**
> - **Noam Chomsky** called Harari's account of language "fanciful."
> - Recurring academic charges: overgeneralization, conflation of concepts,
>   Eurocentrism, broad claims presented as settled.

> **And a second-order lesson, the real reason for this pick.** Searching for
> critiques of *Sapiens* returns serious academic objections mixed with pieces
> from organisations holding a prior theological or political stake in
> discrediting it. **Both sets call themselves criticism.** Telling them apart —
> asking what the critic *wants to be true* before weighing what they argue — is
> harder and more useful than reading the book.

Read *Sapiens* for its ideas, which are genuinely stimulating and beautifully
communicated. Then read its critics, and sort them. That exercise is worth more
than either alone, and is exactly the habit selective programmes select for.

**Accessibility:** very readable, fast, deliberately sweeping. The easiest book
this rotation has offered — part of what makes the critique worth attaching.

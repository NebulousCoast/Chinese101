## 1. Learning platforms

### Coursera and edX audit tracks — and the one thing worth knowing

The headline is that both let you take university courses free. The detail that
matters is **what "free" strips out**:

- **edX audit:** temporary access to all course materials **except graded
  assignments**. No certificate.
- **Coursera Audit Mode:** videos only. No assignments, no certificate.

For a homeschooler that is exactly the wrong thing to lose. The graded
assignments supply feedback and accountability — the thing self-study is usually
missing. Auditing gives you the lectures, which MIT OpenCourseWare gives away
anyway, and withholds the part you cannot easily replace.

> **The useful part: Coursera's financial aid.** If approved, it grants full
> access to graded assignments, peer-reviewed projects **and an official
> verified certificate at zero cost**. Not a discount — full access, free. This
> is the route for a student who wants structure without the price, and it is
> underused because most people do not know it exists. edX has no equivalent
> broad program, though its financial aid covers up to 90% where offered, with
> approval taking around two weeks.

⚠️ **Certificate pricing, flagged because sources disagree:** edX verified
certificates were quoted at both "$39–$79" and "$50 to $300 on average,"
Coursera at "$49–$79/month." None of these should be relied on — check the
specific course. The financial-aid route matters more than the list price.

**Limit on both:** these are university courses, so difficulty varies enormously
and nobody checks whether the student is out of their depth. **Pick a specific
course, not a platform.**

### Today's pick: *Hidden Guests* — Lise Barnéoud

> **Note on sourcing:** this is written from the shortlisting and the book's own
> framing, not a detailed review. What follows is **the science the book
> covers** — well established, and statable reliably — plus the thesis its
> subtitle announces. Flagged rather than implying close reading.

**The concept: microchimerism.** During pregnancy, cells cross the placenta in
*both* directions. Fetal cells enter the mother's body and some persist — in
blood, skin, liver, thyroid, even brain — for decades after birth. Maternal
cells likewise persist in the child. A great many people are walking around
containing living cells that are genetically someone else.

**Why this is a big idea, not a curiosity:**

1. **The bounded individual is a simplification.** Biology has long treated the
   organism as one genome in one body. Microchimerism says that is
   *approximately*, not exactly, true — and the exceptions are common rather
   than rare.
2. **Identity becomes a matter of degree.** A mother of several children may
   carry cell lines from each of them, and from her own mother. "You" is a
   population, not a unit.
3. **The medical implications are unresolved and genuinely contested.** Foreign
   cells have been proposed both as protective — participating in tissue repair
   — and as implicated in autoimmune disease, which disproportionately affects
   women. The evidence does not settle it.
4. **It reframes the immune system's job.** If non-self cells persist for
   decades without being destroyed, tolerance is not a failure state but a
   designed one.

**Why it is worth a teenager's attention:** a clean example of a fact that is
simultaneously technical and philosophical, where the biology genuinely disturbs
a concept — the self — that seemed too obvious to examine. That is what good
science writing does, and the kind of thinking selective programs look for.

Alternatives from the shortlist: *Bad Influence* is the most immediately
relevant to a family making decisions about screens and information; *The Age of
Alchemy* suits a chemistry-inclined reader.
